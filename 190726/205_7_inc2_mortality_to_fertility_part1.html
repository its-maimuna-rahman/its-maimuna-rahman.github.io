Measuring Death and Birth

From a district register to rates, standards, and replacement

A register gives counts. A rate turns them into a statement about risk. This page starts with one district's register, builds the standard mortality and fertility measures from it, and shows where each measure helps and where it misleads.

The register

Bhor is a made-up district with a register that behaves like a real one. The counts are constructed so the arithmetic is exact; the patterns are ordinary. Two neighbouring districts, Bhor and Sur, keep the same kind of register. Bhor is young. Sur is old. That single difference will turn out to explain most of the story.

Before using the register, ask what it is. A civil register counts events that were reported, not events that happened. Three things decide how close the two are. First, the target population: does the register cover everyone living in the area, or only residents with a legal claim, or only households the enumerator could reach? Second, completeness: a pilot survey in a sample of blocks suggested the register captures about 90% of deaths and that the population count is about 95% complete, mostly because people who moved in during the year are missed. Third, non-response: households that refused or were absent tend to differ from those that answered—they are often younger and more mobile, which biases the age structure young. The counts below are treated as complete for teaching. In real work, you would adjust them, and you would say so.
Stock, flow, rate, ratio. Population is a stock: a count at a moment. Births and deaths are flows: counts during a period. A rate puts a flow over the stock or person-years that produced it. A ratio puts one count over another count, and the numerator need not be part of the denominator. A proportion is a ratio whose numerator is a subset of the denominator. Much of demographic writing uses "rate" loosely for all three. The arithmetic does not care about the label, but the interpretation does.

The general form of a rate is

\[\text{rate} = \frac{\text{events in a period}}{\text{person-years at risk}} \times k.\]

The multiplier \(k\) only sets the size of the number: 1,000 for deaths and births, 100,000 for maternal deaths. In practice, the mid-year population stands in for person-years when the period is one year and migration is small. That approximation is good enough for the measures below, but it is still an approximation.

The crude death rate

The simplest mortality measure divides all deaths by the mid-year population:

\[\CDR = \frac{D}{P}\times 1000 = \frac{1{,}520}{200{,}000}\times 1000 = 7.6.\]

Read it as 7.6 deaths per 1,000 people per year, or 0.76%. It is not 7.6%. The multiplier is a convenience, not a percentage sign.

The word crude is not an insult. The rate is crude because its denominator includes everyone: infants, old people, men, women, the healthy and the sick. It summarizes the whole population in one number. That is useful for a quick description and dangerous for a comparison.

Averaging over three years

A single year can be distorted by an epidemic, a heat wave, or a registration glitch. A three-year average is common. There are three ways to do it, and they answer slightly different questions.

The first average gives each year's rate equal weight. The second gives each year's population equal weight. The third uses the mid-period population as a shortcut. Here they agree to three decimals because the population grows smoothly. When the population changes sharply, or when one year is exceptional, the three versions can differ. Say which one you used.

Nothing here is specific to deaths. Replace \(D\) with births \(B\) and the same three formulas give an averaged CBR, with the same choice to make.

When the register is incomplete

Death registration and census counts are rarely perfect. If deaths are less completely registered than people are counted, the raw CDR is too low. A simple correction divides each count by its completeness:

\[\CDR_{\text{adj}} = \frac{D/C_d}{P/C_p}\times1000,\]

where \(C_d\) is the completeness of death registration and \(C_p\) the completeness of the population count. With \(C_d=0.90\) and \(C_p=0.95\),

\[\CDR_{\text{adj}} = \frac{1{,}520/0.90}{200{,}000/0.95}\times1000 = \frac{1{,}688.9}{210{,}526}\times1000 = 8.02.\]

The raw rate was 7.60. The corrected rate is 8.02, about 5.6% higher. The direction is intuitive: if the register misses a larger share of deaths than of people, it understates mortality.

Try it: adjust for incomplete registration

The raw CDR stays at 7.60. The adjusted CDR moves with the ratio of completeness.

A month is not a year

If you only have one month of data, annualize it by the number of days in the month:

\[\text{annualized monthly rate} = \frac{365}{n_m}\times\frac{D_m}{P_m}\times1000.\]

For January, with \(n_m=31\), \(D_m=140\), and \(P_m=200{,}000\), the rate is \((365/31)(140/200{,}000)(1000)=8.24\) per 1,000. That is a valid annual estimate only if January is typical. In many populations January deaths are not July deaths, so the annualization can create a seasonal artefact.

Three slices of the same numerator. A cause-specific death rate is \(D^c/P\times1000\) (or per 100,000). A death ratio is \(D^c/D\times100\): the percentage of all deaths due to cause \(c\). The first is a rate; the second is a proportion. They answer different questions even though both use the same numerator. A cause can account for a large share of deaths while being rare in the population, and vice versa.

Age-specific rates

The crude rate hides the only thing everyone knows about death: it is concentrated at the end of life. Split the population by age and compute a rate in each group:

\[M_a = \frac{D_a}{P_a}\times 1000.\]

\(M_a\) is the age-specific death rate for age group \(a\). \(D_a\) is deaths in that group during the year; \(P_a\) is the mid-year population of the group. The multiplier is still 1,000.

Both curves are J-shaped: high in infancy, low in childhood, then rising steeply. Sur's curve sits below Bhor's at every age. Yet Sur's crude rate is higher. The weights, not the rates, are doing that.

Rate is not probability. The 80+ rate is 227.5 per 1,000. That is not a 22.75% chance of dying in the year. A rate divides deaths by the mid-year population; a probability divides deaths by the population at the start of the interval. If the force of mortality were constant, the one-year probability would be \(q=1-e^{-m}\). For \(m=0.2275\), \(q=1-e^{-0.2275}=0.203\), or 20.3%. The gap grows as the rate grows, which is why demographers convert rates to probabilities before building life tables.

The crude rate is a weighted average

Multiply and divide by \(P\):

\[\CDR = \frac{D}{P}=\frac{\sum_a D_a}{P}=\sum_a \frac{P_a}{P}\frac{D_a}{P_a}=\sum_a \frac{P_a}{P}M_a.\]

So the CDR is a weighted average of the age-specific rates, with weights equal to each age group's share of the population. Check it on two groups. The 0–4 group is 13% of Bhor and has a rate of 10.0, so it contributes \(0.13\times10.0=1.30\) to the CDR. The 80+ group is 0.4% of Bhor and has a rate of 227.5, so it contributes \(0.004\times227.5=0.91\). The old group has a rate 22 times larger, but a weight 32 times smaller.

This identity is the hinge of the next section. If you change the weights while holding every \(M_a\) fixed, the CDR changes. A population can have low mortality at every age and a high CDR simply because it has many old people.

Indirect standardization

Direct standardization needs age-specific rates for the study population. When those rates are based on small numbers, or when they are unavailable, indirect standardization works the other way. It takes the standard's age-specific rates and applies them to the study population's age structure:

\[\text{expected deaths}=\sum_a M_a^s P_a,\qquad \SMR=\frac{\text{observed deaths}}{\text{expected deaths}}\times100.\]

Using the combined population's rates as the standard, Bhor's expected deaths are 1,426.2 and its observed deaths are 1,520:

\[\SMR_{\text{Bhor}}=\frac{1{,}520}{1{,}426.2}\times100=106.6.\]

Sur's expected deaths are 3,876.8 and its observed deaths are 3,783:

\[\SMR_{\text{Sur}}=\frac{3{,}783}{3{,}876.8}\times100=97.6.\]

Bhor has 6.6% more deaths than it would have if its people died at the combined age-specific rates. Sur has 2.4% fewer. The SMR is a ratio of observed to expected, not a rate per 1,000. To put it back on a rate scale, multiply by the standard's crude rate:

\[\text{adjusted rate}=\SMR\times\CDR_{\text{standard}}.\]

For Bhor, \(1.066\times10.606=11.30\). For Sur, \(0.976\times10.606=10.35\). These are close to the direct standardized rates, as they should be when the same standard is used.

Direct vs indirect. Direct standardization needs the study population's age-specific rates and a standard age distribution. It produces a rate. Indirect standardization needs the standard's age-specific rates and the study population's age distribution. It produces a ratio (the SMR) and, after multiplication, an adjusted rate. Indirect is useful when the study rates are thin. Direct is more transparent when the study rates are solid. Neither is "the" truth; both depend on the standard.
The standard is a measuring rod, not a fact. A standardized rate is a weighted average with weights you chose. It is not the rate that anyone actually experiences. Do not compare a standardized rate computed with one standard to a crude rate, or to a standardized rate computed with another standard. Use standardization to compare populations on the same weights; use the crude rate, with its age structure, to describe what is happening in a place.

Infant and perinatal mortality

The infant mortality rate is the most quoted measure in demography and one of the most often misread. It is a ratio, not a rate in the person-years sense. The numerator is infant deaths in a year. The denominator is live births in the same year. Those are not the same infants: a child who dies in January 2021 may have been born in 2020.

\[\IMR=\frac{D_0}{B}\times1000=\frac{208}{5{,}200}\times1000=40.0.\]

Read it as 40 infant deaths per 1,000 live births, not 40 per 1,000 children. The denominator is a flow of births, not a stock of infants. This is why the measure is conventionally expressed per 1,000 live births.

Adjusting the IMR for the birth-cohort mix

A period IMR mixes two birth cohorts. In 2021 there were 208 infant deaths. Of those, 156 were to children born in 2021 and 52 to children born in 2020. The 2021 birth cohort had 5,200 births; the 2020 cohort had 5,000.

The separation factor is the share of this year's infant deaths that belong to this year's births. It tells you how to weight the two cohorts in the denominator:

\[\IMR_{\text{adj}}=\frac{D_t}{fB_t+(1-f)B_{t-1}}\times1000=\frac{208}{0.75(5{,}200)+0.25(5{,}000)}\times1000=40.39.\]

The denominator is a weighted average of the two birth cohorts, weighted by where the deaths came from. Two other versions appear in the literature. The additive version is

\[\left[\frac{D_t'}{B_t}+\frac{D_t''}{B_{t-1}}\right]\times1000=\left[\frac{156}{5{,}200}+\frac{52}{5{,}000}\right]\times1000=40.40.\]

The cohort version follows the 2021 births into 2022. If 22 of those children died in 2022 before their first birthday, the completed cohort IMR is

\[\frac{156+22}{5{,}200}\times1000=34.23.\]

The period IMR is 40.0, the adjusted period IMR is 40.39, and the cohort IMR is 34.23. They answer different questions. The period rate describes 2021 as a year. The cohort rate describes the 2021 births as a group. The period rate is higher here because the 2021 numerator includes 52 deaths from the 2020 cohort, while the denominator contains only 2021 births. The period rate is a mixture; it is not the experience of any single cohort.

Try it: the separation factor

At \(f=1\), the denominator is all 2021 births and the adjusted rate is the naive rate. At \(f=0.5\), the two cohorts get equal weight.

Fetal, perinatal, and the difference between a rate and a ratio

Fetal deaths are classified by gestation. The exact cut-offs vary by country, but the standard three-part split is:

Only late fetal deaths are used in perinatal mortality, because the measure is aimed at the period around birth. The fetal death ratio puts fetal deaths over live births:

\[\text{fetal death ratio}=\frac{96}{5{,}200}\times1000=18.46.\]

The fetal death rate puts fetal deaths over all deliveries at risk, including the stillbirths themselves:

\[\text{fetal death rate}=\frac{96}{96+5{,}200}\times1000=18.13.\]

The ratio is larger because its denominator is smaller. The rate answers "what is the risk per delivery?" The ratio answers "how many stillbirths per live birth?" Both are useful; they are not interchangeable. The gap is small here because fetal deaths are 1.8% of deliveries. In a population with many stillbirths, the gap grows.

Perinatal mortality adds late fetal deaths and early neonatal deaths, then divides by either births or deliveries:

\[\text{perinatal rate}=\frac{78+70}{78+5{,}200}\times1000=28.04,\qquad \text{perinatal ratio}=\frac{78+70}{5{,}200}\times1000=28.46.\]
Endogenous and exogenous infant deaths. Older infant mortality work split deaths into endogenous causes, arising from the pregnancy and birth (congenital anomalies, prematurity, birth injury), and exogenous causes, arising from the environment (infections, accidents, neglect). The ratio was used as a rough index of development: a high exogenous share suggested that environmental causes were still dominant. If 120 of Bhor's 208 infant deaths were endogenous and 88 exogenous, the two rates would be 23.1 and 16.9 per 1,000 births. The split is hard to apply consistently and is less used today, but it explains a common pattern: neonatal mortality, mostly endogenous, falls more slowly than post-neonatal mortality, mostly exogenous, as a population develops.

Fertility

Mortality measures count exits. Fertility measures count entries. The logic is the same: put a flow of events over the population that can produce them. The difficulty is deciding who that population is.

Three words get used as if they were the same, and they are not. Fertility is the actual production of births—a behaviour, measured by counts and rates. Fecundity is the biological capacity to reproduce, whether or not a birth occurs; a woman can be fecund and have no child. Reproductivity is the capacity of a population to replace itself, which depends on fertility and on survival. A population can be fecund and still have low reproductivity if women die young or few of them marry. Keep the three apart: the measures below estimate fertility, and only the last section touches reproductivity.

Crude birth rate and general fertility rate

\[\CBR=\frac{B}{P}\times1000=\frac{5{,}200}{200{,}000}\times1000=26.0.\]

26 births per 1,000 people per year. The denominator includes men, children, and the elderly—people who cannot give birth. The CBR is therefore a summary of both fertility and age structure, not a pure fertility measure.

The general fertility rate narrows the denominator to women of childbearing age:

\[\GFR=\frac{B}{P_{f,15-49}}\times1000=\frac{5{,}200}{49{,}300}\times1000=105.48.\]

105 births per 1,000 women aged 15–49. This removes men, children, and older people, but it still hides the age distribution within 15–49 and the marital distribution. A population with many women in their late thirties will have a lower GFR than one with the same number of women but younger.

The total fertility rate multiplies each ASFR by the width of its age group (five years) and sums:

\[\TFR=5\sum_a\frac{\ASFR_a}{1000}=5(0.040+0.150+0.180+0.150+0.105+0.060+0.00844)=3.47.\]

Read it as the number of children a woman would have if she lived to 50 and experienced this year's age-specific rates at every age. That is a synthetic cohort, not a real one. No woman actually lives through 2021's rates for thirty years. The TFR is a summary of one year's schedule, and it is sensitive to timing: if women postpone births, the TFR can fall even when completed family size does not.

Two shortcuts, and why they miss. A common rule of thumb is \(\GFR\approx4.5\times\CBR\). Here \(4.5\times26.0=117.0\), against the actual 105.5. The multiplier 4.5 assumes women aged 15–49 are \(1/4.5=22.2\%\) of the population. In Bhor they are 24.65%, so the shortcut overstates the GFR. Another is \(\TFR\approx30\times\GFR/1000\). Here \(30\times105.48/1000=3.16\), against the actual 3.47. The 30 assumes a flat fertility schedule over thirty years; Bhor's schedule is concentrated at 20–34, so the shortcut understates the TFR. Rules of thumb are checks, not substitutes.

\[\GMFMR=\frac{B_m}{P_{f,m}}\times1000=\frac{4{,}300}{30{,}000}\times1000=143.33.\]

\[\text{marital birth ratio}=\frac{4{,}300}{5{,}200}\times1000=826.9,\qquad \text{non-marital}=\frac{900}{5{,}200}\times1000=173.1.\]

A population can have a high marital birth ratio and low marital fertility if almost all births occur within marriage but married women have few children. The ratio tells you about the composition of births; the rate tells you about the behaviour of women.

The indirect version, step by step

Indirect standardization applies a standard age-specific fertility schedule \(F_a\) to the study population's women, compares observed with expected births, and multiplies the ratio by the standard CBR \(F\):

\[\AABR_{\text{in}}=\frac{b}{\sum_a F_aP_a^f}\times F.\]

Take the standard ASFR schedule 30, 120, 140, 110, 70, 30, 5 per 1,000. Applied to Bhor's female population:

\[\text{expected births}=\frac{30(9{,}800)+120(9{,}000)+140(8{,}000)+110(7{,}000)+70(6{,}000)+30(5{,}000)+5(4{,}500)}{1000}=3{,}856.5.\]

Observed births are 5,200, so the ratio is \(5{,}200/3{,}856.5=1.348\). The standard schedule produces 4,057.5 births in the standard population of 200,000, so the standard CBR is \(4{,}057.5/200{,}000\times1000=20.29\). Therefore

\[\AABR_{\text{in}}=1.348\times20.29=27.36\ \text{per 1,000}.\]

The direct and indirect versions agree to two decimal places, which is what you expect when the standard schedule is not too different from Bhor's. Neither is "the" birth rate; both are weighted averages with chosen weights.

Replacement

Fertility counts births. Replacement counts daughters who survive to have their own children. The bridge between the two is the sex ratio at birth and the life table.

Sex ratio at birth and the GRR

Bhor's register implies 105 boys for every 100 girls at birth. The share of girls is therefore

\[\frac{100}{100+105}=0.4878.\]

Of 5,200 births, about 2,538 are girls. The gross reproduction rate multiplies the TFR by the share of girls:

\[\GRR=\TFR\times0.488=3.467\times0.488=1.692.\]

A synthetic cohort of women would have 1.69 daughters each if every girl survived through the childbearing years. The GRR ignores mortality, so it is an upper bound on replacement.

Replacement-level fertility

Set NRR equal to 1 and solve for the TFR:

\[\TFR_{\text{repl}}=\frac{1}{0.488\times S}=\frac{1}{0.488\times0.899}=2.28.\]

At Bhor's mortality, women need about 2.28 births each to replace the population, not the familiar 2.1. The extra 0.18 is the toll of mortality before and during the childbearing years. In a population with higher mortality, replacement fertility is higher; in a population where every girl survives to 50, it approaches \(1/0.488=2.05\).

Natural increase and the vital index

\[r_n=\frac{B-D}{P}\times1000=\frac{5{,}200-1{,}520}{200{,}000}\times1000=18.4.\]

The natural increase rate is 18.4 per 1,000, or 1.84% a year before migration. It is also the difference between the crude birth and death rates: \(26.0-7.6=18.4\). The vital index is a simple ratio of births to deaths:

\[VI=\frac{B}{D}\times100=\frac{5{,}200}{1{,}520}\times100=342.1.\]

342 births per 100 deaths. The vital index is easy to read and easy to over-interpret: it ignores the age structure, so a young population can have a high index while its age-specific rates are not especially favourable.

Generation length and the annual rate. The mean age at childbearing in Bhor is 30.0 years. If NRR is 1.52 per generation, the implied annual growth rate is approximately \(\ln(1.52)/30=0.0140\), or 1.40% a year. The natural increase rate is 1.84%. The gap is momentum: a young age structure produces more births than deaths even at replacement fertility. NRR is a generational measure; \(r_n\) is an annual one. They are related, not identical.

What the numbers do not say

Every measure on this page is a ratio with a chosen numerator, a chosen denominator, and a chosen multiplier. The arithmetic is exact. The interpretation is where the mistakes live.

  • Rate, ratio, proportion. A rate puts events over person-years. A ratio puts one count over another. A proportion is a ratio whose numerator is a subset of the denominator. The IMR, the maternal mortality ratio, and the fetal death ratio are ratios by strict usage, even though everyone calls them rates. The label does not change the arithmetic, but it changes what you can claim.
  • Period and cohort. A period measure mixes the experience of several birth cohorts in one calendar year. A cohort measure follows one group through time. The period IMR and the cohort IMR can differ substantially when births or mortality are changing. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.
  • Denominator mismatch. Infant deaths in a year are not all from that year's births. Maternal deaths are not a subset of live births in the same way that infant deaths are. Fetal deaths are not live births. Always ask who is actually at risk.
  • Small numbers. A rate computed from eight deaths is unstable. The same is true of a maternal mortality ratio, a cause-specific rate for a rare cause, or an ASFR for a small age group. A confidence interval would show the instability; a single number hides it.
  • The standard is a choice. Direct and indirect standardization give different numbers, and the choice of standard changes the level. Standardized rates are for comparison, not for description. Do not compare a standardized rate to a crude rate from another population.
  • Registration completeness. A rate is only as good as its numerator and denominator. If deaths are underregistered, the CDR and the IMR are too low. If the census misses people, the denominators are too small and the rates are too high. The correction in the CDR section is a ratio of completeness, not a guarantee.
  • Age heaping and age misreporting. Ages ending in 0 and 5 are overrepresented in many censuses. That distorts age-specific rates and any standardization that uses them. The problem is worse in older populations and in self-reported ages.
  • The synthetic cohort is not a forecast. The TFR and the NRR describe what would happen if one year's rates continued forever. They do not predict what a real cohort will do. Tempo effects—later marriage, later first births—can move the TFR without changing completed family size.
  • Natural increase is not population growth. \(r_n\) ignores migration. A district can have a healthy natural increase and a falling population if young adults leave. It can also have a low \(r_n\) and a growing population if migrants arrive.
  • Shortcuts are checks. \(GFR\approx4.5\times CBR\) and \(TFR\approx30\times GFR/1000\) are rules of thumb. They assume a particular age structure and a particular fertility schedule. When those assumptions fail, the shortcuts fail quietly.

2. Compute the three-year CDR for 2019–2021 using the three averaging methods. Why do they agree here?

The data. Deaths: 1,480; 1,500; 1,520. Populations: 196,000; 198,000; 200,000.

Method 1: average of annual rates.

\[\frac13\left[\frac{1{,}480}{196{,}000}+\frac{1{,}500}{198{,}000}+\frac{1{,}520}{200{,}000}\right]\times1000=7.576.\]

Method 2: total deaths over total population.

\[\frac{4{,}500}{594{,}000}\times1000=7.576.\]

Method 3: average annual deaths over mid-period population.

\[\frac{1{,}500}{198{,}000}\times1000=7.576.\]

Why they agree. The population grows by a constant 2,000 a year and the death rate is nearly constant. When the weights change smoothly, the three versions are nearly identical. They would diverge if one year had a epidemic, a boundary change, or a census adjustment.

3. Adjust the 2021 CDR for incomplete registration when \(C_d=0.90, C_p=0.95\). Then reverse the two completeness rates and compare.

Step 2: plug in.

\[\frac{1{,}520/0.90}{200{,}000/0.95}\times1000=\frac{1{,}688.9}{210{,}526}\times1000=8.02.\]

The raw rate was 7.60, so the correction raises it by about 5.6%.

\[\frac{1{,}520/0.95}{200{,}000/0.90}\times1000=\frac{1{,}600}{222{,}222}\times1000=7.20.\]

The adjusted rate is now lower than the raw rate. What matters is the ratio \(C_p/C_d\). When deaths are undercounted more than people, the rate rises; when people are undercounted more than deaths, it falls.

Five identities worth remembering.
  • \(\CDR=\CBR-r_n\). The crude rates and the natural increase rate are three views of the same two counts.
  • \(\IMR=\text{NMR}+\text{PNMR}\). The two infant age ranges partition the first year.
  • \(\TFR=5\sum\ASFR/1000\). The five is the width of each age group, not a constant of nature.
  • \(\NRR=\GRR\times S\), where \(S\) is the average survival of women through the childbearing years.
  • \(\NRR=1\) at \(\TFR=1/(0.488S)\). At Bhor's mortality, that is 2.28, not 2.10.
