1. Estimation and properties of estimators

An estimator is a rule (a statistic) that turns the sample into a guess for a parameter. The main ways to find one are the method of moments, maximum likelihood, Bayes estimators and the EM algorithm. A good estimator should be unbiased, consistent, efficient and sufficient. The goal is an estimator that is close to the true value with high probability.

1.1 Maximum likelihood: quick examples

Write the likelihood, take the log (it turns products into sums and does not change the maximiser), differentiate, set to zero.

ExampleMLE for Bernoulli, Exponential, Geometric, Normal

Bernoulli: \(f(x,p)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x}\), \(x=0,1\). With \(s=\sum x_i\):

\[\log L=s\log p+(n-s)\log(1-p),\quad \frac{s}{p}-\frac{n-s}{1-p}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat p=\bar x\]

Exponential: \(f(x,\theta)=\frac1\theta e^{-x/\theta}\), \(x>0\).

\[\log L=-n\log\theta-\frac{\sum x_i}{\theta},\quad -\frac n\theta+\frac{\sum x_i}{\theta^2}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\theta=\bar x\]

Geometric: \(f(x,p)=(1-p)^{x-1}p\), \(x=1,2,\dots\)

\[\log L=n\log p+\Big(\sum x_i-n\Big)\log(1-p),\quad \frac np-\frac{\sum x_i-n}{1-p}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat p=\frac1{\bar x}\]

Normal \(N(\theta_1,\theta_2)\) with \(\theta_1=\mu,\ \theta_2=\sigma^2\):

\[\hat\theta_1=\bar X,\qquad \hat\theta_2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar X)^2\]

\(\hat\theta_1\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\mu\); \(\hat\theta_2\) is a biased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).

1.2 Unbiasedness, bias and MSE

\(T=t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) is unbiased for \(\tau(\theta)\) if its average value is the true value: no systematic over- or under-estimation.

\[\E[T]=\tau(\theta),\qquad \text{Bias}=\E(T)-\tau(\theta)\]

where \(T=t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) = the estimator (a statistic), \(\theta\) = the unknown parameter, \(\tau(\theta)\) = the parameter function being estimated, \(\E\) = expectation.

For a sample with \(\E(x)=\mu,\ \V(x)=\sigma^2\), the sample mean is unbiased for \(\mu\), and there are two variance estimators:

\[\hat S^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\ \text{(biased)},\qquad \hat S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\ \text{(unbiased)}\]

where \(x_i\) = the \(i\)th observation, \(n\) = sample size, \(\bar x\) = sample mean, \(\hat S^2\) = sample variance (divisor \(n\) in the first form, \(n-1\) in the second).

The mean squared error measures the average squared miss, and splits into variance plus squared bias:

\[\text{MSE}(T)=\E\big[(T-\tau(\theta))^2\big]=\V(T)+\big(\text{Bias}(T)\big)^2\]

where \(\text{MSE}(T)\) = mean squared error of \(T\), \(\V(T)\) = variance of \(T\), \(\text{Bias}(T)=\E(T)-\tau(\theta)\).

1.3 Consistency

\(T_n\) is consistent if it settles on the true value as \(n\) grows. Biased estimators can be consistent too (law of large numbers: \(\bar x\xrightarrow{p}\mu\)).

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(|T_n-\tau(\theta)|>\epsilon\big)=0\quad\text{for all }\epsilon>0\]

where \(T_n\) = estimator based on a sample of size \(n\), \(\tau(\theta)\) = true value, \(\epsilon\) = any small positive number, \(P\) = probability.

Asymptotically unbiased: \(\lim_{n\to\infty}B(T)=0\). MSE consistent: \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\E\{T-\tau(\theta)\}^2=0\). An unbiased estimator whose variance goes to \(0\) is MSE consistent.

ExampleExponential: \(\bar X\) unbiased and consistent

\(X_i\sim\) Exp with mean \(\theta\): \(\E(X_i)=\theta\), \(\V(X_i)=\theta^2\).

\[\E(\bar X)=\frac1n\sum\E(X_i)=\frac1n\cdot n\theta=\theta\] \[\V(\bar X)=\frac1{n^2}\sum\V(X_i)=\frac{n\theta^2}{n^2}=\frac{\theta^2}{n}\]

By Chebyshev's inequality:

\[P(|\bar X-\theta|>\epsilon)\le\frac{\V(\bar X)}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{\theta^2}{n\epsilon^2}\to0\]

So \(\bar X\xrightarrow{p}\theta\): consistent.

Example\(N(0,\theta)\): \(\tilde X=\frac1n\sum X_i^2\)

\(\E(X_i)=0\), \(\V(X_i)=\theta\), so \(\E(X_i^2)=\theta\). Since \(X_i^2/\theta\sim\chi^2_1\) has variance \(2\), \(\V(X_i^2)=2\theta^2\).

\[\E(\tilde X)=\frac1n\sum\E(X_i^2)=\theta,\qquad \V(\tilde X)=\frac1{n^2}\cdot n\cdot2\theta^2=\frac{2\theta^2}{n}\to0\]

So \(\tilde X\) is unbiased and consistent.

Example\(\bar x\) and \(s^2\) are consistent and MSE consistent

\(x_1,\dots,x_n\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), so \(\bar x\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\), \(\E(\bar x)=\mu\), \(\V(\bar x)=\sigma^2/n\to0\).

\[\E[(\bar x-\mu)^2]=\E[(\bar x-\E\bar x)^2]=\V(\bar x)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\to0\]

For \(s^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\): \(\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\), so \(\E(s^2)=\sigma^2\) and

\[\V(s^2)=\frac{\sigma^4}{(n-1)^2}\cdot2(n-1)=\frac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}\to0\]

Unbiased with variance \(\to0\) means MSE consistent, hence consistent.

Example\(\frac Tn\big(1-\frac Tn\big)\) is asymptotically unbiased for \(p(1-p)\)

\(x_i\sim b(1,p)\), \(T=\sum x_i\sim b(n,p)\): \(\E(T)=np\), \(\V(T)=np(1-p)\), \(\E(T/n)=p\).

\[\E(T^2)=\V(T)+\{\E(T)\}^2=np(1-p)+n^2p^2\] \[\E\Big[\frac Tn\Big(1-\frac Tn\Big)\Big]=\E\Big(\frac Tn\Big)-\frac{\E(T^2)}{n^2}=p-\frac{np(1-p)+n^2p^2}{n^2}=p(1-p)\,\frac{n-1}{n}\] \[\text{Bias}=p(1-p)-p(1-p)\frac{n-1}n=\frac{p(1-p)}{n}\to0\]

So it is biased but asymptotically unbiased. Also \(\E(T/n)=p\) and

\[\V\Big(\frac Tn\Big)=\frac{np(1-p)}{n^2}=\frac{p(1-p)}n\to0\]

so \(T/n\) is a consistent estimator of \(p\).

1.4 Efficiency and sufficiency (preview)

Efficiency: among unbiased estimators, smaller variance means more precise; the Cramér–Rao bound gives the minimum possible variance. Sufficiency: the estimator keeps all the information about the parameter that is in the sample; the factorization theorem finds such statistics (e.g. \(\bar x\) is sufficient for \(\mu\) in the normal).

2. Method of moments

The oldest method of finding point estimators (Karl Pearson). Idea: the sample looks like the population, so sample moments should match population moments. A good place to start when other methods are unavailable.

\[\mu_k=\E(X^k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^kf(x)\,dx,\qquad M_k=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^k\]

where \(\mu_k\) = \(k\)th population moment, \(M_k\) = \(k\)th sample moment, \(f(x)\) = the pdf, \(X_i\) = the \(i\)th observation, \(n\) = sample size.

2.1 Procedure

  1. Identify the \(r\) unknown parameters \(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_r\).
  2. Compute the first \(r\) population moments \(\E(X^k)\), \(k=1,\dots,r\).
  3. Compute the first \(r\) sample moments \(M_k\).
  4. Set \(\E(X^k)=M_k\) for \(k=1,\dots,r\).
  5. Solve these \(r\) equations together. The solutions \(\tilde\theta_1,\dots,\tilde\theta_r\) are the estimators.

Central-moment version (handy for location–scale families):

\[\E(X)=\bar X,\qquad \E\big[(X-\mu)^k\big]=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^k,\ \ k=2,3,\dots\]

where \(\bar X\) = sample mean, \(\mu=\E(X)\) = population mean, \(\E[(X-\mu)^k]\) = \(k\)th population central moment, \(\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^k\) = \(k\)th sample central moment.

2.2 Examples

ExampleOne parameter: \(f(x;\theta)=\theta x^{\theta-1}\)

\(00\).

\[\E(X)=\int_0^1x\cdot\theta x^{\theta-1}dx=\theta\int_0^1x^\theta dx=\frac{\theta}{\theta+1}\]

Set \(\frac{\theta}{\theta+1}=\bar X\) and solve:

\[\tilde\theta=\frac{\bar X}{1-\bar X}\]
ExampleGamma distribution, two parameters

\(f(x;\theta_1,\theta_2)=\dfrac{x^{\theta_1-1}e^{-x/\theta_2}}{\Gamma(\theta_1)\theta_2^{\theta_1}}\), \(x>0\). Moments:

\[\E(X)=\theta_1\theta_2=\mu,\quad \E(X^2)=\theta_1\theta_2^2+\mu^2,\quad \V(X)=\theta_1\theta_2^2\]

Equate to the sample mean and \(V=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\):

\[\theta_1\theta_2=\bar X,\qquad \theta_1\theta_2^2=V\]

Dividing, \(\theta_2=V/\bar X\); substituting back, \(\theta_1=\bar X^2/V\):

\[\tilde\theta_1=\frac{\bar X^2}{V},\qquad \tilde\theta_2=\frac{V}{\bar X}\]

Useful here because the gamma MLE is hard to get (it involves \(\Gamma\)).

ExampleNormal \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\)
\[\E(X)=\mu,\quad \E(X^2)=\sigma^2+\mu^2\] \[\frac1n\sum X_i=\mu,\qquad \frac1n\sum X_i^2=\sigma^2+\mu^2\] \[\tilde\mu=\bar X,\qquad \tilde\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=V\]

These are identical to the maximum likelihood estimators.

ExampleNumerical gamma data (\(n=25\))

Data: 6.9, 7.3, 6.7, 6.4, 6.3, 5.9, 7.0, 7.1, 6.5, 7.6, 7.2, 7.1, 6.1, 7.3, 7.6, 7.6, 6.7, 6.3, 5.7, 6.7, 7.5, 5.3, 5.4, 7.4, 6.9.

\[\bar X=\frac1{25}\sum X_i=6.78,\qquad V=\frac1{25}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=0.45\] \[\tilde\alpha=\frac{\bar X^2}{V}=\frac{6.78^2}{0.45}\approx102.2,\qquad \tilde\theta=\frac{V}{\bar X}=\frac{0.45}{6.78}\approx0.0664\]

Pros: simple, closed form, consistent, needs only moments. Cons: may have larger variance than MLE, may land outside the parameter space, not invariant, needs moments that may not exist. Use it when the MLE is hard (gamma), as a starting value for iterative MLE, or when only moments are known.

3. Efficiency of estimators

Take two unbiased estimators \(T_1,T_2\) of \(\tau(\theta)\). The relative efficiency compares their variances:

\[E=\frac{\V(T_2)}{\V(T_1)}\]

where \(T_1,T_2\) = two unbiased estimators of \(\tau(\theta)\), \(E\) = relative efficiency, \(\V(T_i)\) = variance of \(T_i\).

If \(\V(T_1)<\V(T_2)\) then \(T_1\) is more efficient. \(E>1\): \(T_1\) is more efficient. \(E<1\): \(T_1\) is less efficient.

3.1 Mean versus the other normal estimator

For \(X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), \(\V(\bar x)=\sigma^2/n\), while the other estimator \(\tilde x\) has \(\V(\tilde x)=\frac\pi2\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\). So

\[\frac{\V(\tilde x)}{\V(\bar x)}=\frac\pi2\approx1.6,\qquad \frac{\V(\bar x)}{\V(\tilde x)}=\frac2\pi\approx0.63\]

where \(\bar x\) = sample mean, \(\tilde x\) = the other estimator of \(\mu\) used above, \(\V(\cdot)\) = variance, \(\sigma^2\) = population variance, \(n\) = sample size.

\(\tilde x\) varies about \(1.6\) times as much as \(\bar x\); equivalently the variability of \(\bar x\) is only \(63\%\) of that of \(\tilde x\).

ExampleThree estimators for the exponential mean

\(x_1,x_2,x_3\sim\) Exp\((\theta)\), \(f=\frac1\theta e^{-x/\theta}\), so \(\E(x_i)=\theta\), \(\V(x_i)=\theta^2\). Estimators: \(\hat\theta_1=\frac{x_1+x_2}2\), \(\hat\theta_2=\frac{x_1+2x_2}3\), \(\hat\theta_3=\bar x\).

Unbiased:

\[\E(\hat\theta_1)=\frac{2\theta}2=\theta,\quad \E(\hat\theta_2)=\frac{3\theta}3=\theta,\quad \E(\hat\theta_3)=\theta\]

Variances:

\[\V(\hat\theta_1)=\frac{2\theta^2}{4}=\frac{\theta^2}2,\quad \V(\hat\theta_2)=\frac{\theta^2+4\theta^2}{9}=\frac{5\theta^2}9,\quad \V(\hat\theta_3)=\frac{3\theta^2}{9}=\frac{\theta^2}3\]

Numerically \(0.5\theta^2,\ 0.556\theta^2,\ 0.333\theta^2\), so \(\hat\theta_3\) is the most efficient. Relative to \(\hat\theta_3\):

\[\frac{\V(\hat\theta_3)}{\V(\hat\theta_1)}=\frac23\approx0.67,\qquad \frac{\V(\hat\theta_3)}{\V(\hat\theta_2)}=\frac35=0.6\]
ExampleNormal: \(t_1=\bar x\) versus \(t_2=\frac{2x_1+x_2+\lambda x_3}{3}\)

Unbiasedness of \(t_2\):

\[\E(t_2)=\frac13(2\mu+\mu+\lambda\mu)=\frac{(3+\lambda)\mu}{3}=\mu\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda=0,\quad t_2=\frac{2x_1+x_2}3\]

Variances:

\[\V(t_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}n,\qquad \V(t_2)=\frac19(4\sigma^2+\sigma^2+\lambda^2\sigma^2)=\frac{\sigma^2}9(5+\lambda^2)\overset{\lambda=0}{=}\frac{5\sigma^2}9\]

For \(n>2\), \(\frac{\sigma^2}n<\frac{5\sigma^2}9\), so \(t_1=\bar x\) is more efficient. The sample mean is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE).

ExampleTwo variance estimators

For \(X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\):

\[\hat\sigma_1^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2,\qquad \hat\sigma_2^2=\frac12(X_1-X_2)^2\]

Both are unbiased: \(\E(\hat\sigma_1^2)=\E(\hat\sigma_2^2)=\sigma^2\). Variances: \(\V(\hat\sigma_1^2)=\frac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}\), \(\V(\hat\sigma_2^2)=2\sigma^4\). Relative efficiency of \(\hat\sigma_2^2\) with respect to \(\hat\sigma_1^2\):

\[\frac{\V(\hat\sigma_1^2)}{\V(\hat\sigma_2^2)}=\frac{2\sigma^4/(n-1)}{2\sigma^4}=\frac1{n-1}\]

For \(n>2\) this is below \(1\), so \(\hat\sigma_2^2\) is less efficient.

Reminder (standardising): if \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) then \(Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)\).

4. Sufficiency of estimators

4.1 Definitions

A sufficient statistic carries all the information about \(\theta\) in the sample. Once you know it, the individual \(X_i\) tell you nothing more about \(\theta\), so the data can be thrown away after computing it. Formally, \(S=s(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) is sufficient iff the conditional distribution \(P(T\mid S=s)\) does not depend on \(\theta\) for any statistic \(T\).

Jointly sufficient: \(S_1,\dots,S_r\) are jointly sufficient iff \(P(X_1,\dots,X_n\mid S_1,\dots,S_r)\) does not depend on \(\theta\). For \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), \((\bar X,S^2)\) are jointly sufficient for \((\mu,\sigma^2)\); neither alone is enough.

Minimal sufficient: sufficient, and a function of every other sufficient statistic (maximum data reduction). It is unique up to one-to-one transformations.

Why it matters: it holds all the estimation information, simplifies the data, underlies many optimal estimators, arises naturally in exponential families, and is the basis of optimal decision rules.

4.2 Neyman factorization theorem

\[L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^nf(X_i;\theta)=g\big[s(X_1,\dots,X_n);\theta\big]\cdot h(X_1,\dots,X_n)\]

where \(L(\theta)\) = likelihood, \(f(X_i;\theta)\) = pdf/pmf of one observation, \(s(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) = the statistic \(S\), \(g\) = function of the data only through \(s\) (and of \(\theta\)), \(h\) = function that does not depend on \(\theta\).

\(S\) is sufficient iff the likelihood splits like this, where \(h\ge0\) does not depend on \(\theta\), and \(g\ge0\) depends on the data only through \(s\). For several parameters:

\[L(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_r)=g(s_1,\dots,s_r;\theta)\,h(X_1,\dots,X_n)\]

where \(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_r\) = the \(r\) parameters, \(s_1,\dots,s_r\) = the jointly sufficient statistics, \(g\) and \(h\) as above.

4.3 Examples

Example 1Poisson

\(f(x;\lambda)=\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}\).

\[L(\lambda)=\prod\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{X_i}}{X_i!}=e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^{\sum X_i}\cdot\frac1{\prod X_i!}=g\Big(\sum X_i;\lambda\Big)\,h(X_1,\dots,X_n)\]

Here \(g=e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^{\sum X_i}\) and \(h=1/\prod X_i!\). Hence \(\sum X_i\) is sufficient for \(\lambda\).

Example 2Bernoulli: MLE is a function of the sufficient statistic
\[L(p)=\prod p^{X_i}(1-p)^{1-X_i}=p^{\sum X_i}(1-p)^{n-\sum X_i}=g(s;p)\,h,\quad s=\sum X_i\] \[\log L=s\log p+(n-s)\log(1-p),\qquad \frac{\partial\log L}{\partial p}=\frac sp-\frac{n-s}{1-p}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat p=\frac sn\]

The MLE is a function of \(s\).

MLE theorem: if the MLE exists, it is a function of the sufficient statistic. One-to-one functions of a sufficient statistic are sufficient too (e.g. \(\sum X_i\) and \(\bar X=\frac{\sum X_i}n\) for Bernoulli). The function must be one-to-one; many-to-one functions may lose information.

Example 3Normal: \(\bar X\) and \(S^2\) jointly sufficient
\[L(\mu,\sigma^2)=\Big(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\Big)^ne^{-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum(X_i-\mu)^2}\]

Key identity:

\[\sum(X_i-\mu)^2=\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2+n(\bar X-\mu)^2=(n-1)S^2+n(\bar X-\mu)^2\] \[L(\mu,\sigma^2)=\Big(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\Big)^ne^{-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\left[n(\bar X-\mu)^2+(n-1)S^2\right]}\]

It depends on the data only through \(\bar X\) and \(S^2\), with \(h=1\). So they are jointly sufficient.

Example 4Normal with known \(\sigma^2\): sufficient for \(\mu\)
\[L(\mu)=e^{-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\bar X-\mu)^2}\cdot\underbrace{\Big(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\Big)^ne^{-\frac{(n-1)S^2}{2\sigma^2}}}_{h(X_1,\dots,X_n)}\]

Since \(\sigma^2\) is known, \(h\) does not involve \(\mu\). So \(\bar X\) is sufficient for \(\mu\).

4.4 Exponential family (introduction)

A pdf/pmf is in the exponential family if

\[f(x;\theta)=\exp\big[P(\theta)K(x)+S(x)+q(\theta)\big],\quad a\lt x\lt b\]

where \(P(\theta),\ q(\theta)\) = functions of the parameter, \(K(x),\ S(x)\) = functions of the observation, \((a,b)\) = the support, which does not depend on \(\theta\).

with: support \((a,b)\) free of \(\theta\); \(P(\theta)\) a non-trivial continuous function; \(K,S,q\) continuous. Normal, Poisson, Bernoulli, Binomial, Exponential and Gamma all belong. Then \(\sum_{i=1}^nK(X_i)\) is sufficient automatically; for \(k\) parameters,

\[f(x;\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k)=\exp\Big[\sum_{j=1}^kP_j(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k)K_j(x)+S(x)+q(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k)\Big]\]

where \(k\) = number of parameters, \(P_j\) = functions of the parameters, \(K_j(x),\ S(x)\) = functions of the observation, \(q\) = function of the parameters.

Example 5Binomial\((n,p)\) is in the family
\[f(x;p)=\binom nxp^x(1-p)^{n-x}=\exp\Big[x\log\frac p{1-p}+\log\binom nx+n\log(1-p)\Big]\] \[P(p)=\log\frac p{1-p},\quad K(x)=x,\quad S(x)=\log\binom nx,\quad q(p)=n\log(1-p)\]

So the Binomial is in the exponential family and \(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\) is sufficient.

Summary: factorization finds sufficient statistics in practice; MLEs are functions of them; exponential families give them automatically; they give optimal data reduction; one-to-one functions keep sufficiency.

5. Maximum likelihood: properties and examples

5.1 Properties of the MLE

  • Invariance: if \(\hat\theta\) is the MLE of \(\theta\), then for any function \(g\) the MLE of \(g(\theta)\) is \(g(\hat\theta)\).
  • Sufficiency: if a sufficient statistic exists, the MLE is a function of it.
  • Efficiency: if a UMVUE exists, the MLE is that estimator, reaching the Cramér–Rao bound.
  • Consistency: \(\hat\theta\xrightarrow{p}\theta\) as \(n\to\infty\).
  • Asymptotic normality: for large \(n\),
\[\hat\theta\ \dot\sim\ N\!\Big(\theta,\ \frac1{I_n(\theta)}\Big),\qquad I_n(\theta)=-\E\Big[\frac{\partial^2\log L}{\partial\theta^2}\Big]\]

where \(\hat\theta\) = the MLE, \(\dot\sim\) = approximately distributed as (large \(n\)), \(I_n(\theta)\) = Fisher information, \(L\) = likelihood, \(\log\) = natural logarithm.

\(I_n(\theta)\) is the Fisher information.

5.2 Examples

Example 1Poisson: MLE of \(\lambda\) and of \(g(\lambda)=(1+\lambda)e^{-\lambda}\)
\[L(\lambda)=\prod\frac{\lambda^{x_i}e^{-\lambda}}{x_i!}=\frac{\lambda^{\sum x_i}e^{-n\lambda}}{\prod x_i!}\] \[\log L=-n\lambda+\Big(\sum x_i\Big)\log\lambda-\log\prod x_i!,\qquad \frac{d\log L}{d\lambda}=-n+\frac{\sum x_i}\lambda=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\lambda=\bar x\]

By invariance:

\[\widehat{g(\lambda)}=g(\hat\lambda)=(1+\bar x)e^{-\bar x}\]

Consistency of \(\hat\lambda\): unbiased, \(\E(\bar x)=\frac1n\cdot n\lambda=\lambda\); and \(\V(\hat\lambda)=\V(x_i)/n=\lambda/n\to0\). So \(\hat\lambda\) is consistent.

Variance via Fisher information:

\[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\lambda}=-n+\frac{\sum X_i}{\lambda},\qquad \frac{\partial^2\log L}{\partial\lambda^2}=-\frac{\sum X_i}{\lambda^2}\] \[I_n(\lambda)=-\E\Big(-\frac{\sum X_i}{\lambda^2}\Big)=\frac{n\lambda}{\lambda^2}=\frac n\lambda,\qquad \V(\hat\lambda)\approx\frac1{I_n}=\frac\lambda n\]

This matches the direct result, so the MLE reaches the Cramér–Rao lower bound.

Example 2\(f(x;\theta)=\frac1{\theta^2}xe^{-x/\theta}\): MLE and consistency
\[L(\theta)=\prod\frac1{\theta^2}x_ie^{-x_i/\theta}=\frac1{\theta^{2n}}\Big(\prod x_i\Big)e^{-\sum x_i/\theta}\] \[\log L=-2n\log\theta+\sum\log x_i-\frac{\sum x_i}\theta,\qquad -\frac{2n}\theta+\frac{\sum x_i}{\theta^2}=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\theta=\frac{\sum x_i}{2n}=\frac12\bar x\]

Mean (with \(z=x/\theta\), and \(\int_0^\infty z^{k-1}e^{-z}dz=\Gamma(k)\)):

\[\E(X)=\frac1{\theta^2}\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x/\theta}dx=\frac1{\theta^2}\int_0^\infty\theta^3z^2e^{-z}dz=\theta\,\Gamma(3)=2\theta\]

Unbiased: \(\E(\hat\theta)=\frac12\cdot\frac1n\sum2\theta=\theta\).

Fisher information:

\[\frac{\partial^2\log L}{\partial\theta^2}=\frac{2n}{\theta^2}-\frac{2\sum X_i}{\theta^3},\qquad I_n(\theta)=-\frac{2n}{\theta^2}+\frac{2}{\theta^3}(2n\theta)=\frac{2n}{\theta^2}\] \[\V(\hat\theta)\approx\frac1{I_n(\theta)}=\frac{\theta^2}{2n}\to0\]

So \(\hat\theta\) is consistent.

Example 3Normal: jointly sufficient statistics via exponential form

The joint likelihood is \(L(\mu,\sigma^2)=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\!\big[-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i-\mu)^2\big]\). Write the pdf as

\[f(x;\mu,\sigma^2)=\exp\Big[\underbrace{\tfrac\mu{\sigma^2}}_{P_1}\underbrace{x}_{K_1}+\underbrace{\big(-\tfrac1{2\sigma^2}\big)}_{P_2}\underbrace{x^2}_{K_2}+\underbrace{0}_{S}+\underbrace{\Big(-\tfrac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\Big)}_{q}\Big]\]

So \(\sum x_i\) and \(\sum x_i^2\) are jointly sufficient for \((\mu,\sigma^2)\). Since \(\bar x=\frac{\sum x_i}n\) and \(s^2=\frac{\sum x_i^2-(\sum x_i)^2/n}{n-1}\) are functions of them, they are sufficient too.

6. Sufficient statistics and the exponential family

A distribution is in the exponential family if its pdf/pmf can be written

\[f(x;\theta)=\exp\Big[\sum_{j=1}^kP_j(\theta)K_j(x)+S(x)+q(\theta)\Big]\]

where \(k\) = number of parameters, \(P_j(\theta)\) = functions of the parameter, \(K_j(x),\ S(x)\) = functions of the observation, \(q(\theta)\) = function of the parameter.

Key properties: \(\sum_{i=1}^nK_j(x_i)\) are jointly sufficient; the number of sufficient statistics equals the number of parameters \(k\); it helps in finding MVUEs and simplifies theory. Method: take logs of the pdf, match it to this form, read off \(K_j\).

Example 1Gamma\((\alpha,\beta)\)

\(f(x;\alpha,\beta)=\dfrac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}\), \(x>0\).

\[f=\exp\Big[\alpha\log\beta-\log\Gamma(\alpha)+(\alpha-1)\log x-\beta x\Big]=\exp\Big[\alpha\log x-\beta x-\log x+\alpha\log\beta-\log\Gamma(\alpha)\Big]\] \[P_1=\alpha,\ K_1=\log x;\quad P_2=-\beta,\ K_2=x;\quad S(x)=-\log x;\quad q=\alpha\log\beta-\log\Gamma(\alpha)\]

Hence \(\sum\log x_i\) and \(\sum x_i\) are jointly sufficient for \((\alpha,\beta)\).

(The slide's "\(\sqrt\alpha\)" is read here as \(\Gamma(\alpha)\), the gamma function.)

Example 2Beta\((\theta_1,\theta_2)\)

\(f(x;\theta_1,\theta_2)=\dfrac1{B(\theta_1,\theta_2)}x^{\theta_1-1}(1-x)^{\theta_2-1}\), \(0 \[f=\exp\Big[\theta_1\log x+\theta_2\log(1-x)-\log x-\log(1-x)-\log B(\theta_1,\theta_2)\Big]\] \[P_1=\theta_1,\ K_1=\log x;\quad P_2=\theta_2,\ K_2=\log(1-x);\quad S=-[\log x+\log(1-x)];\quad q=-\log B(\theta_1,\theta_2)\]

Jointly sufficient: \(\sum\log x_i\) and \(\sum\log(1-x_i)\).

Example 3Poisson\((\lambda)\)
\[f(x;\lambda)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^x}{x!}=\exp\big[(\log\lambda)x-\log(x!)-\lambda\big]\] \[p(\lambda)=\log\lambda,\quad k(x)=x,\quad s(x)=-\log(x!),\quad q(\lambda)=-\lambda\]

One-parameter family; \(\sum x_i\) is sufficient for \(\lambda\).

Example 4Exponential\((\theta)\)
\[f(x;\theta)=\frac1\theta e^{-x/\theta}=\exp\Big[-\frac1\theta x-\log\theta\Big]\] \[p(\theta)=-\frac1\theta,\quad k(x)=x,\quad s(x)=0,\quad q(\theta)=-\log\theta\]

One-parameter family; \(\sum x_i\) is sufficient for \(\theta\).

Example 5Special case \(f(x;\theta)=\frac1{6\theta^4}x^3e^{-x/\theta}\)
\[f=\exp\Big[-\log(6\theta^4)+3\log x-\frac x\theta\Big]=\exp\Big[-\frac1\theta x+3\log x-\log6-4\log\theta\Big]\] \[p(\theta)=-\frac1\theta,\quad k(x)=x,\quad s(x)=3\log x-\log6,\quad q(\theta)=-4\log\theta\]

\(\sum x_i\) is sufficient. The \(x^3\) term goes into \(s(x)\), not into the sufficient statistic.

7. UMVUE and the Cramér–Rao bound

7.1 Best unbiased estimator

\(T^*=t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) is a UMVUE of \(\tau(\theta)\) iff (1) \(\E(T^*)=\tau(\theta)\), and (2) \(\V(T^*)\le\V(T)\) for every other unbiased \(T\) of \(\tau(\theta)\). It has the smallest possible variance among all unbiased estimators.

7.2 Cramér–Rao lower bound

Regularity conditions: (1) \(\frac\partial{\partial\theta}\log f(x_i;\theta)\) (or \(\frac\partial{\partial\theta}\log L\)) exists for all \(x,\theta\); (2) we may differentiate under the integral:

\[\frac\partial{\partial\theta}\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int L\,dx_1\cdots dx_n=\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}dx_1\cdots dx_n,\quad\text{and the same with }t(x_1,\dots,x_n)L\]

where \(L\) = likelihood, \(\theta\) = parameter, \(t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) = an unbiased estimator, \(\int\!\cdots\!\int\) = integral over all \(x_1,\dots,x_n\).

Then for any unbiased \(T\) of \(\tau(\theta)\):

\[\V(T)\ \ge\ \frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{n\,\E\Big[\big(\frac{\partial\log f(x;\theta)}{\partial\theta}\big)^2\Big]}=\frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{\E\Big[\big(\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\big)^2\Big]}=\frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{-\E\Big[\frac{\partial^2\log L}{\partial\theta^2}\Big]}\]

where \(T\) = an unbiased estimator of \(\tau(\theta)\), \(\tau'(\theta)\) = derivative of \(\tau\), \(n\) = sample size, \(f(x;\theta)\) = pdf/pmf, \(L\) = likelihood; the right side is the CRLB (the denominator is the Fisher information).

This is the Cramér–Rao inequality; the right side is the CRLB, the minimum possible variance of an unbiased estimator. (The proof is in the Derivations section.)

MVBE: an estimator attaining the CRLB is the minimum variance bound estimator, and is automatically a UMVUE. Uses: it bounds the variance, certifies a UMVUE, and measures efficiency. If your variance is close to the CRLB it is good; if equal, it is optimal.

7.3 Finding the UMVUE (Cramér–Rao approach)

Cauchy–Schwarz becomes an equality when

\[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}=k(\theta,n)\,\big[t(x_1,\dots,x_n)-\tau(\theta)\big]\]

where \(k(\theta,n)\) = a function of \(\theta\) and \(n\) that does not involve the data, \(t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) = the statistic, \(\tau(\theta)\) = the parameter function.

If this holds, \(t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) is the UMVUE of \(\tau(\theta)\), with variance

\[\V\big(t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\big)=\frac{\tau'(\theta)}{k(\theta,n)}\]

where \(\V(t)\) = variance of the UMVUE \(t\), \(\tau'(\theta)\) = derivative of \(\tau\), \(k(\theta,n)\) as above.

7.4 Examples

Example 1Exponential \(f=\theta e^{-\theta x}\): CRLB and UMVUE of \(\tau(\theta)=1/\theta\)

\(x>0,\ \theta>0\).

\[\log f=\log\theta-\theta x,\qquad \frac\partial{\partial\theta}\log f=\frac1\theta-x\] \[\E\Big[\Big(\frac1\theta-x\Big)^2\Big]=\E\Big[\Big(x-\frac1\theta\Big)^2\Big]=\E[x-\E(x)]^2=\V(x)=\frac1{\theta^2}\]

With \(\tau=\frac1\theta\), \(\tau'(\theta)=-\frac1{\theta^2}\):

\[\text{CRLB}=\frac{(-1/\theta^2)^2}{n\cdot1/\theta^2}=\frac1{n\theta^2}\]

Finding the UMVUE:

\[L=\theta^ne^{-\theta\sum x_i},\quad \log L=n\log\theta-\theta\sum x_i\] \[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}=\frac n\theta-\sum x_i=-n\Big(\frac{\sum x_i}n-\frac1\theta\Big)=k(\theta,n)\,[t-\tau(\theta)]\]

with \(k(\theta,n)=-n\), \(t=\bar x\), \(\tau=\frac1\theta\). So \(\bar x\) is the UMVUE of \(\frac1\theta\), and

\[\V(\bar x)=\frac{\tau'(\theta)}{k(\theta,n)}=\frac{-1/\theta^2}{-n}=\frac1{n\theta^2}\]

which equals the CRLB: the best possible unbiased estimator.

Example 2Poisson: CRLB and UMVUE of \(\tau(\lambda)=\lambda\)
\[\log f=-\lambda+x\log\lambda-\log x!,\qquad \frac\partial{\partial\lambda}\log f=-1+\frac x\lambda\] \[\E\Big[\Big(-1+\frac x\lambda\Big)^2\Big]=\frac1{\lambda^2}\E[x-\lambda]^2=\frac{\V(x)}{\lambda^2}=\frac\lambda{\lambda^2}=\frac1\lambda\]

With \(\tau'(\lambda)=1\):

\[\text{CRLB}=\frac{1^2}{n\cdot\frac1\lambda}=\frac\lambda n\]

Finding the UMVUE:

\[L=\prod\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!}=\frac{e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^{\sum x_i}}{\prod x_i!},\qquad \log L=\sum x_i\log\lambda-n\lambda-\log\prod x_i!\] \[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{\sum x_i}\lambda-n=\frac n\lambda\Big(\frac{\sum x_i}n-\lambda\Big)=k(n,\lambda)\,[T-\tau(\lambda)]\]

with \(k=\frac n\lambda\), \(T=\bar x\). So \(\bar x\) is the UMVUE of \(\lambda\) with

\[\V(T)=\frac{\tau'(\lambda)}{k(n,\lambda)}=\frac1{n/\lambda}=\frac\lambda n=\text{CRLB}\]

8. Derivations and proofs

The main proofs from the lectures, step by step. Tap one to open it.

D1MSE = variance + bias²
  1. Add and subtract \(\E(T)\): \[T-\tau=(T-\E T)+(\E T-\tau)\]
  2. Square and take expectation: \[\E(T-\tau)^2=\E(T-\E T)^2+2(\E T-\tau)\,\E(T-\E T)+(\E T-\tau)^2\]
  3. Since \(\E(T-\E T)=0\), the middle term vanishes: \[\text{MSE}(T)=\V(T)+\big(\E T-\tau\big)^2=\V(T)+\text{Bias}^2\]
D2Unbiased with variance \(\to0\) implies consistent (Chebyshev)
  1. Chebyshev: for any \(\epsilon>0\), \[P(|T_n-\E T_n|>\epsilon)\le\frac{\V(T_n)}{\epsilon^2}\]
  2. If \(\E T_n=\tau(\theta)\), this reads \[P(|T_n-\tau(\theta)|>\epsilon)\le\frac{\V(T_n)}{\epsilon^2}\]
  3. If \(\V(T_n)\to0\), the right side \(\to0\), so \(T_n\xrightarrow{p}\tau(\theta)\).
D3MLE is a function of the sufficient statistic
  1. If \(S=s(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) is sufficient, factorization gives \[L(\theta)=g(s;\theta)\,h(X_1,\dots,X_n)\]
  2. Take logs: \[\log L(\theta)=\log g(s;\theta)+\log h(X_1,\dots,X_n)\]
  3. Since \(h\) is free of \(\theta\), maximising \(L\) means solving \[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\partial\log g(s;\theta)}{\partial\theta}=0\]
  4. The solution \(\hat\theta\) depends on the data only through \(s\): the MLE is a function of the sufficient statistic.
D4Exponential family gives \(\sum K(X_i)\) sufficient
  1. Let \(f(x;\theta)=\exp[P(\theta)K(x)+S(x)+q(\theta)]\). Then \[L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n\exp\big[P(\theta)K(X_i)+S(X_i)+q(\theta)\big]\]
  2. Combine the exponents: \[L(\theta)=\exp\Big[P(\theta)\sum K(X_i)+\sum S(X_i)+nq(\theta)\Big]\]
  3. Split into two factors: \[L(\theta)=\underbrace{\exp\Big[P(\theta)\sum K(X_i)+nq(\theta)\Big]}_{g(s;\theta)}\cdot\underbrace{\exp\Big[\sum S(X_i)\Big]}_{h(X_1,\dots,X_n)}\]
  4. By the factorization theorem, \(s=\sum_{i=1}^nK(X_i)\) is sufficient.
D5Cramér–Rao inequality
  1. \(L=\prod f(x_i;\theta)\) is a joint density, so \[\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int L\,dx_1\cdots dx_n=1\tag{1}\]
  2. Differentiate (1) in \(\theta\) (regularity), and use \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}L\): \[\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\,L\,dx_1\cdots dx_n=0\tag{2}\] so \(\E\big[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\big]=0\).
  3. Differentiate (2) again: \[\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int\Big[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}+L\frac{\partial^2\log L}{\partial\theta^2}\Big]dx_1\cdots dx_n=0\] \[\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\Big)^2+\frac{\partial^2\log L}{\partial\theta^2}\Big]L\,dx_1\cdots dx_n=0\] \[\Rightarrow\ \E\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\Big)^2\Big]=-\E\Big[\frac{\partial^2\log L}{\partial\theta^2}\Big]\tag{3}\]
  4. \(T\) unbiased means \[\E[T]=\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int t(x_1,\dots,x_n)\,L\,dx_1\cdots dx_n=\tau(\theta)\tag{4}\]
  5. Differentiate (4): \[\tau'(\theta)=\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int t\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}\,dx=\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int[t-\tau(\theta)]\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}L\,dx\] (we may subtract \(\tau(\theta)\) because of (2)). So \[\tau'(\theta)=\E\Big[\{T-\tau(\theta)\}\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\Big]\tag{5}\]
  6. Cauchy–Schwarz on (5): \[[\tau'(\theta)]^2\le\E[T-\tau(\theta)]^2\cdot\E\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\Big)^2\Big]=\V(T)\cdot\E\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}\Big)^2\Big]\]
  7. Divide, and use (3): \[\V(T)\ge\frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{\E\big[(\partial\log L/\partial\theta)^2\big]}=\frac{[\tau'(\theta)]^2}{-\E\big[\partial^2\log L/\partial\theta^2\big]}\]
D6Equality condition and variance of the UMVUE
  1. Cauchy–Schwarz is an equality when \[\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}=k(\theta,n)\,[t(x_1,\dots,x_n)-\tau(\theta)]\]
  2. Multiply by \([t-\tau(\theta)]\) and integrate against \(L\): \[\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int[t-\tau]\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\theta}L\,dx=k(\theta,n)\int\!\!\cdots\!\!\int[t-\tau]^2L\,dx\]
  3. The left side is \(\tau'(\theta)\) by (5) of D5; the right side is \(k(\theta,n)\,\V(t)\): \[\tau'(\theta)=k(\theta,n)\V(t)\ \Rightarrow\ \V(t)=\frac{\tau'(\theta)}{k(\theta,n)}\]
  4. This variance equals the CRLB, so \(t\) attains the bound and is the UMVUE.
D7\(\Gamma(3)\) integral used for \(\E(X)=2\theta\)
  1. \(\E(X)=\int_0^\infty x\cdot\frac1{\theta^2}xe^{-x/\theta}dx=\frac1{\theta^2}\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x/\theta}dx\).
  2. Let \(z=x/\theta\), \(x=\theta z\), \(dx=\theta\,dz\): \[\E(X)=\frac1{\theta^2}\int_0^\infty(\theta z)^2e^{-z}\theta\,dz=\theta\int_0^\infty z^2e^{-z}dz\]
  3. With \(\int_0^\infty z^{k-1}e^{-z}dz=\Gamma(k)\) and \(k=3\): \(\Gamma(3)=2!=2\), so \(\E(X)=2\theta\).
