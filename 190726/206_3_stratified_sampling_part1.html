The idea

Some populations are made of groups that behave very differently. Stratified sampling takes those groups seriously instead of hoping a random draw will cover them all.

1. Split

Cut the \(N\) units into \(k\) groups with no overlap. Each unit sits in exactly one group. The groups are called strata.

2. Sample inside

In every stratum, draw its own simple random sample without replacement (SRSWOR). The draws in different strata are independent.

3. Combine

Join the stratum samples into one sample, and join the stratum results with weights (see "Mean").

Population size is the sum of stratum sizes

Every unit belongs to one stratum, so counting stratum by stratum and adding gives the whole population.

\[N=\sum_{i=1}^{k}N_i\]

Example

Question. A population is divided into three strata with 500, 300 and 200 units. How many units are in the whole population?

Solution

\[N=500+300+200\]
\[N=1000\]

Sample size is the sum of stratum sample sizes

The final sample is just the stratum samples put together.

\[n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_i\]

Example

Question. From the three strata we draw 50, 30 and 20 units. What is the total sample size?

Solution

\[n=50+30+20\]
\[n=100\]
Example: heights of school students (classes 1 to 12).
  • Height grows with age, so class matters a lot.
  • A single random draw could, by bad luck, pick mostly young children and miss older ones.
  • Make 4 strata: classes 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12. Draw a random sample in each, so every age band is guaranteed to appear.
  • Inside one band the heights are close to each other, which makes the final estimate steadier.

Procedure and advantages

Steps

  1. Say exactly which population you want to describe.
  2. Split it into non-overlapping strata that cover everyone.
  3. Find the size \(N_i\) of every stratum.
  4. Decide how many units \(n_i\) to take from each stratum.
  5. Draw an SRSWOR of \(n_i\) units in stratum \(i\).
  6. Do this for every stratum.
  7. Put all chosen units together. That is your sample.

Step 4 is a real decision. Equal sizes and size-proportional sizes are two simple choices (try both in the first widget).

Why people use it

More precise

  • Similar units sit together, so the estimate varies less.
  • Very large or unusual units can be given their own stratum instead of disturbing the whole sample.

Results for groups

  • You get estimates for each stratum and for the whole population.
  • Important strata can be studied more closely.

Easier fieldwork

  • Districts or offices can act as strata.
  • Each stratum may have its own list of units, which is often easier to get.
  • Different parts can be handled in different ways.

Fair coverage

  • Every segment of the population is in the sample on purpose, not by luck.

Notation with one running example

Everything below uses one tiny population, so you can check each number by hand.

Running population: \(N=10\) units, \(k=2\) strata.
  • Stratum 1 has \(N_1=4\) units: 4, 8, 12, 16.
  • Stratum 2 has \(N_2=6\) units: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60.
  • One sample we will reuse: from stratum 1 take \(n_1=2\) units: {4, 12}. From stratum 2 take \(n_2=3\) units: {10, 30, 50}.

Capital letters (\(Y,\bar Y,S^2\)) describe a whole stratum. Small letters (\(y,\bar y,s^2\)) describe the sample. The value of unit \(j\) in stratum \(i\) is \(Y_{ij}\) in the population and \(y_{ij}\) in the sample.

Stratum weight \(W_i\)

The share of the population that lives in stratum \(i\). Bigger strata get bigger weights, and the weights always add to 1.

\[W_i=\frac{N_i}{N}\]
\[\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_i=1\]

Example

Question. In the running population, \(N_1=4\) and \(N_2=6\). Find the weights \(W_1\) and \(W_2\) and check that they add to 1.

Solution

\[W_1=\frac{4}{10}=0.4\]
\[W_2=\frac{6}{10}=0.6\]
\[W_1+W_2=1\]

Stratum population mean \(\bar Y_i\)

The true average of all units in stratum \(i\). We usually do not know it; this is what we want to learn about.

\[\bar Y_i=\frac{1}{N_i}\sum_{j=1}^{N_i}Y_{ij}\]

Example

Question. Stratum 1 has the units 4, 8, 12, 16 and stratum 2 has the units 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60. Find the population mean of each stratum.

Solution

\[\bar Y_1=\frac{4+8+12+16}{4}=10\]
\[\bar Y_2=\frac{10+20+30+40+50+60}{6}=35\]

Stratum sample mean \(\bar y_i\)

The average of the units we actually picked in stratum \(i\). It is our estimate of \(\bar Y_i\). Under SRSWOR it is right on average: \(\E(\bar y_i)=\bar Y_i\).

\[\bar y_i=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}y_{ij}\]

Example

Question. From stratum 1 the sample {4, 12} is drawn, and from stratum 2 the sample {10, 30, 50}. Find the sample mean of each stratum.

Solution

\[\bar y_1=\frac{4+12}{2}=8\]
\[\bar y_2=\frac{10+30+50}{3}=30\]

The sample means (8 and 30) are close to, but not equal to, the true means (10 and 35). That gap is sampling error.

Stratum population variance \(S_i^2\)

How spread out the units are inside stratum \(i\). It is the average squared distance from the stratum mean (dividing by \(N_i-1\)). Variance formulas later are built from it.

\[S_i^2=\frac{1}{N_i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_i}(Y_{ij}-\bar Y_i)^2\]

Example

Question. Find the population variance \(S_1^2\) of stratum 1 and \(S_2^2\) of stratum 2 of the running population.

Solution

\[S_1^2=\frac{(4-10)^2+(8-10)^2+(12-10)^2+(16-10)^2}{3}\]
\[S_1^2=\frac{80}{3}\approx 26.67\]
\[S_2^2=\frac{625+225+25+25+225+625}{5}=350\]

Stratum sample variance \(s_i^2\)

The same idea computed from the sample only. It is what we can really calculate, and it stands in for \(S_i^2\).

\[s_i^2=\frac{1}{n_i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}(y_{ij}-\bar y_i)^2\]

Example

Question. For the samples {4, 12} and {10, 30, 50}, find the sample variances \(s_1^2\) and \(s_2^2\).

Solution

\[s_1^2=\frac{(4-8)^2+(12-8)^2}{2-1}=32\]
\[s_2^2=\frac{(10-30)^2+(30-30)^2+(50-30)^2}{3-1}=400\]

Estimating the population mean

The true mean is a weighted average

The population mean is not the plain average of the stratum means. A stratum with more people must count for more. The weights \(W_i\) do exactly that.

\[\bar Y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_i\bar Y_i\]

Example

Question. (a) Find the population mean \(\bar Y\) of the running population using the weights \(W_i\). (b) Check it by averaging all 10 values directly. (c) Show that the plain average of the two stratum means gives a different, wrong answer.

Solution

(a)

\[\bar Y=0.4(10)+0.6(35)\]
\[\bar Y=4+21=25\]

(b) The 10 values add to 40 + 210

\[\bar Y=\frac{250}{10}=25\]

(c) Plain average of the two stratum means

\[\frac{10+35}{2}=22.5\neq 25\]

The stratified mean \(\bar y_{st}\)

We do not know \(\bar Y_i\), so we put the sample mean \(\bar y_i\) in its place and keep the weights.

\[\bar y_{st}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_i\bar y_i\]

Example

Question. In our sample, \(\bar y_1=8\) and \(\bar y_2=30\). With the weights 0.4 and 0.6, find \(\bar y_{st}\) and compare it with the true mean 25.

Solution

\[\bar y_{st}=0.4(8)+0.6(30)\]
\[\bar y_{st}=3.2+18=21.2\]

The true mean is 25, so this one sample is 3.8 too low. Another sample could be too high. "Unbiased" (next section) says these misses cancel out on average.

A bigger example

Question. Three strata have \(N_i=500,\ 300,\ 200\) and sample means \(\bar y_i=40,\ 55,\ 70\). Find the weights and \(\bar y_{st}\).

Solution

\[W=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2)\]
\[\bar y_{st}=0.5(40)+0.3(55)+0.2(70)\]
\[\bar y_{st}=20+16.5+14=50.5\]

Why the stratified mean is unbiased

Imagine repeating the whole sampling process again and again. Each time you get a slightly different \(\bar y_{st}\). Unbiased means the average of all those answers is exactly the true mean: no habit of landing too high or too low.

Unbiasedness

\[\E(\bar y_{st})=\bar Y\]

Reason in three lines. The weights are fixed numbers. Each \(\bar y_i\) is right on average. So the weighted sum is right on average. Full proof: D1.

Check by hand with a tiny population

Question. Stratum A has the units 4, 8 and stratum B has the units 10, 20, 30, 40. One unit is drawn from each stratum. (a) Find the weights and the true mean \(\bar Y\). (b) List every possible value of \(\bar y_{st}\). (c) Show that the average of all possible \(\bar y_{st}\) equals \(\bar Y\).

Solution

(a)

\[W_A=\frac{2}{6}=\frac13,\qquad W_B=\frac{4}{6}=\frac23\]
\[\bar Y=\frac13(6)+\frac23(25)\approx 18.67\]

(b) There are \(2\times4=8\) possible samples

\(\bar y_{st}\)B = 10B = 20B = 30B = 40
A = 48.0014.6721.3328.00
A = 89.3316.0022.6729.33

(c)

\[\text{Average of the 8 values}=\frac{149.33}{8}\approx 18.67=\bar Y\]

No single sample hits 18.67, yet the average of all of them does.

The weight trap: \(N_i/N\) or \(n_i/n\)?

The tempting but wrong estimator

It feels natural to weight each stratum by its share of the sample. That only works when the sample shares match the population shares. Otherwise a stratum that was sampled more heavily gets too much say.

\[\bar y_{naive}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{n_i}{n}\bar y_i\]
\[\E(\bar y_{naive})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{n_i}{n}\bar Y_i\]

Example

Question. In the running population take \(n_1=3\) and \(n_2=3\) (so \(n=6\)). Find \(\E(\bar y_{naive})\) and its bias.

Solution

\[\E(\bar y_{naive})=\frac36(10)+\frac36(35)\]
\[\E(\bar y_{naive})=22.5\]
\[\text{bias}=22.5-25=-2.5\]

Another example

Question. Now take \(n_1=2\) and \(n_2=3\), so that \(n_i/n=0.4,\ 0.6\). Find \(\E(\bar y_{naive})\).

Solution

\[\E(\bar y_{naive})=0.4(10)+0.6(35)=25\]
Rule. Always weight by \(W_i=N_i/N\). The weights \(n_i/n\) are only safe when they happen to equal \(N_i/N\). Proof: D2.

How much does \(\bar y_{st}\) vary?

An estimate without a measure of its reliability is half an answer. Variance tells us how far the estimate typically wanders from the truth.

Variance of one stratum mean

Inside a stratum we use SRSWOR. The variance goes down when the stratum is less spread out (small \(S_i^2\)) and when we take more units (large \(n_i\)).

\[\V(\bar y_i)=\Big(1-\frac{n_i}{N_i}\Big)\frac{S_i^2}{n_i}\]

The factor \(1-n_i/N_i\) is the finite population correction (FPC). Sampling without replacement from a finite stratum teaches us more each time. If you sample the whole stratum the factor is 0 (no uncertainty left). If you sample a tiny fraction it is close to 1.

Example

Question. In the running population (\(N_1=4,\ N_2=6\)) take \(n_1=2\) and \(n_2=3\). Using \(S_1^2=26.67\) and \(S_2^2=350\), find \(\V(\bar y_1)\) and \(\V(\bar y_2)\).

Solution

\[\V(\bar y_1)=\Big(1-\frac24\Big)\frac{26.67}{2}=0.5\times 13.33\approx 6.67\]
\[\V(\bar y_2)=\Big(1-\frac36\Big)\frac{350}{3}=0.5\times 116.67\approx 58.33\]

Variance of the stratified mean

Strata are sampled independently, so one stratum's luck says nothing about another's. Then the variances simply add, and each is multiplied by the square of its weight (because \(\bar y_i\) is multiplied by \(W_i\)). Proof: D3.

\[\V(\bar y_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_i^2\,\V(\bar y_i)\]
\[\V(\bar y_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_i^2\Big(1-\frac{n_i}{N_i}\Big)\frac{S_i^2}{n_i}\]

Example

Question. For the same design and the weights \(W=(0.4,\ 0.6)\), find \(\V(\bar y_{st})\) using the stratum variances found above.

Solution

\[\V(\bar y_{st})=0.4^2(6.67)+0.6^2(58.33)\]
\[\V(\bar y_{st})=1.07+21.00\]
\[\V(\bar y_{st})\approx 22.07\]

Stratum 2 supplies almost all of it: it has the bigger weight and the bigger spread.

Estimating the variance from a sample

\(S_i^2\) is unknown in real life. Swap in the sample variance \(s_i^2\). This estimate is unbiased (proof: D4). The standard error (SE) is its square root, in the same units as the mean.

\[\vh(\bar y_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}W_i^2\Big(1-\frac{n_i}{N_i}\Big)\frac{s_i^2}{n_i}\]
\[SE(\bar y_{st})=\sqrt{\vh(\bar y_{st})}\]

Example

Question. From the samples {4, 12} and {10, 30, 50} we found \(s_1^2=32\) and \(s_2^2=400\). Find the estimated variance and the standard error of \(\bar y_{st}\).

Solution

\[\text{Stratum 1: }0.4^2(0.5)\frac{32}{2}=1.28\]
\[\text{Stratum 2: }0.6^2(0.5)\frac{400}{3}=24.00\]
\[\vh(\bar y_{st})=1.28+24.00=25.28\]
\[SE(\bar y_{st})=\sqrt{25.28}\approx 5.03\]

Read it as: an estimate like 21.2 is typically about 5 units away from the truth.

A second full example

Example

Question. Two strata have \(N=(600,\ 400)\), \(n=(60,\ 40)\) and sample variances \(s^2=(100,\ 64)\). Find the weights, the estimated variance of \(\bar y_{st}\) and its standard error.

Solution

\[W_1=0.6,\qquad W_2=0.4\]
\[\text{Stratum 1: }0.6^2(1-0.1)\frac{100}{60}=0.36(0.9)(1.667)=0.5400\]
\[\text{Stratum 2: }0.4^2(1-0.1)\frac{64}{40}=0.16(0.9)(1.6)=0.2304\]
\[\vh(\bar y_{st})=0.5400+0.2304=0.7704\]
\[SE(\bar y_{st})=\sqrt{0.7704}\approx 0.878\]

How to build good strata

The variance formula contains only the spread inside each stratum (\(S_i^2\)). Differences between strata do not add to the variance, because every stratum is always represented. So:

Good strata are alike inside and different from each other. Smaller \(S_i^2\) gives a smaller \(\V(\bar y_{st})\).
  • Pick a grouping variable that is linked to what you measure (age for height).
  • It must be known for every unit before sampling.
  • Groups that mix very different units give no gain over a plain random sample.
Strata builder. A school has 12 classes of 10 students (\(N=120\)). Choose how to group the classes and see the variance of \(\bar y_{st}\). Each stratum gets a share of \(n\) in proportion to its size, so only the grouping changes.
