Mortality and Fertility Measures

Reference table and practice problems

Part 1 lists every measure with its formula, symbols, the meaning of its range and an example of interpretation. Part 2 gives datasets and one problem per measure, with solutions you can open after you try.

Part 1. The measures

Ranges are rough guides used in teaching, not laws. Green marks a favourable reading, red an unfavourable one, grey a neutral or conditional one.

MeasureFormulaNotationWhat the range meansExample of interpretation
A. Mortality measures
1. Crude death rate (CDR)\(\CDR=\dfrac{D}{P}\times1000\)\(D\): deaths in the year
\(P\): mid-year population
  • Below 6: usually a young population, or very good health. Check age structure first.
  • 6–10: typical of most countries today. Deaths are modest.
  • Above 12: old population, or heavy mortality from poverty, war or epidemic.
CDR = 8 means 8 deaths per 1,000 people per year (0.8%). It looks moderate, but it cannot show whether health is good, because age structure drives it.
2. Three-year average CDR (also CBR)\(\dfrac13\sum\dfrac{D_i}{P_i}\times1000\)
\(\dfrac{\sum D_i}{\sum P_i}\times1000\)
\(\dfrac{\bar D}{P_2}\times1000\)		\(D_i,P_i\): deaths and population in year \(i\)
\(\bar D\): mean deaths
\(P_2\): middle-year population
  • No fixed range. It smooths one odd year; it is not a level of mortality.
  • Three methods differ by under 0.1: the population changed smoothly.
  • Methods differ clearly: an epidemic, boundary change or census error hit one year.
Three methods give 7.97, 7.97 and 7.97. They agree, so one smooth series is behind the numbers. Always say which method you used.
3. CDR adjusted for incomplete registration\(\CDR_{adj}=\dfrac{D/C_d}{P/C_p}\times1000=\CDR\times\dfrac{C_p}{C_d}\)\(C_d\): share of deaths registered
\(C_p\): share of people counted
  • Completeness 90% or more in both: raw and adjusted rates are close.
  • \(C_p/C_d>1\): deaths are missed more than people. Raw CDR understates mortality.
  • \(C_p/C_d<1\): people are missed more than deaths. Raw CDR overstates mortality. Below 50% completeness, do not trust either.
Raw 8.0, adjusted 8.9. Mortality is about 12% higher than the register shows, so health plans built on the raw figure would be too small.
4. Annualized monthly rate\(\dfrac{365}{n_m}\times\dfrac{D_m}{P_m}\times1000\)\(n_m\): days in the month
\(D_m,P_m\): deaths and population in the month
  • No fixed range. Compare it with the annual CDR.
  • Close to the annual rate: the month was typical.
  • Far from the annual rate: a seasonal effect (winter, flood, heat, cyclone), not a yearly level.
January gives 8.83, annual CDR is 8.0. January is probably a high-death month, so do not use it as the annual rate.
5. Cause-specific death rate and death ratio\(\dfrac{D^c}{P}\times1000\)
\(\dfrac{D^c}{D}\times100\)		\(D^c\): deaths from cause \(c\)
\(D\): all deaths
  • The rate shows risk to the population. The ratio shows a share of deaths. They are not interchangeable.
  • A share above 50% means one cause dominates. Planning should target it.
  • A large share with a small rate is possible when total mortality is low.
Heart disease: rate 2.4 per 1,000, ratio 30%. Three in ten deaths come from one cause, so screening gives a large payoff.
6. Age-specific death rate (ASDR)\(M_a=\dfrac{D_a}{P_a}\times1000\)\(D_a\): deaths in age group \(a\)
\(P_a\): mid-year population of group \(a\)
  • J shape: high in infancy, lowest at 5–14, rising steeply after 45.
  • High rates at 15–44: HIV, injuries, maternal deaths, violence or conflict.
  • High rate at 0–4: weak child survival (infection, malnutrition).
A rate of 75.5 at 65+ means 75.5 deaths per 1,000 older people a year. It is far above the other groups, as expected, but compare it with a standard.
7. Rate to probability\(q=1-e^{-m}\)\(m\): death rate per person (rate/1000)
\(q\): probability of dying in the year
  • \(m<0.05\): \(q\) and \(m\) are almost equal.
  • Larger \(m\): \(q\) falls below \(m\). At \(m=0.2\), \(q=0.18\).
  • \(q\) is always smaller than \(m\). Life tables use \(q\).
A rate of 200 per 1,000 at ages 80+ means an 18.1% chance of dying within a year, not 20%.
8. Direct standardized death rate\(\sum_a\dfrac{P_a^s}{P^s}M_a\)\(P_a^s\): standard population in group \(a\)
\(M_a\): study death rate in group \(a\)
  • No absolute range. Compare only rates made with the same standard.
  • Standardized rate below the crude rate: the study population is older than the standard.
  • Standardized rate above the crude rate: the study population is younger, so crude rate hid heavy mortality.
Crude 8.0 but standardized 11.9. The district is young, and its crude rate was hiding high mortality at every age.
9. SMR and indirect adjusted rate\(\SMR=\dfrac{D_{obs}}{\sum M_a^sP_a}\times100\)
\(\text{adj. rate}=\dfrac{\SMR}{100}\times\CDR^s\)		\(M_a^s\): standard rate
\(P_a\): study population
\(\CDR^s\): standard crude rate
  • SMR below 100: fewer deaths than the standard rates predict.
  • SMR = 100: same as the standard.
  • SMR above 100: excess deaths. SMR 120 means 20% more deaths than expected.
SMR 115.8: the district has about 16% more deaths than expected at the state's age-specific rates.
10. Infant mortality rate (IMR)\(\IMR=\dfrac{D_0}{B}\times1000\)\(D_0\): deaths under age 1
\(B\): live births in the same year
  • Below 10: low. Good newborn care, sanitation and nutrition.
  • 10–30: moderate. Gaps in care remain.
  • Above 30: high. Above 60: very high. Infection, malnutrition and poor delivery care cause many deaths.
IMR = 45 means 45 of every 1,000 live births die before age 1. It is high, so child survival services need work.
11. Neonatal and post-neonatal rates\(\NMR=\dfrac{D_{0\text{–}27}}{B}\times1000\)
\(\PNMR=\dfrac{D_{28\text{–}364}}{B}\times1000\)		\(D_{0-27}\): deaths in first 28 days
\(D_{28-364}\): deaths at 28–364 days
  • NMR below 12 (the SDG target): newborn care is good.
  • NMR over two-thirds of IMR: deaths are from birth causes (prematurity, asphyxia). Delivery care is weak.
  • High PNMR: infection, diarrhoea, malnutrition. The home environment is the problem.
NMR 30 and PNMR 15 add to IMR 45. Two-thirds of infant deaths occur in the first month, so the weak point is care at birth.
12. Maternal mortality ratio (MMR)\(\MMR=\dfrac{D_{mat}}{B}\times100{,}000\)\(D_{mat}\): maternal deaths
\(B\): live births
  • Below 100: low (WHO). SDG target is under 70.
  • 100–299: moderate.
  • 300–499 high; 500–999 very high; 1,000 and over extremely high. Few skilled births and weak emergency care.
MMR 200: 200 women die per 100,000 live births. Moderate by WHO bands, but each death also harms the children left behind.
13. Fetal death ratio and rate\(\dfrac{F}{B}\times1000\)
\(\dfrac{F}{F+B}\times1000\)		\(F\): fetal deaths (stillbirths)
\(B\): live births
  • Below 10 per 1,000: low.
  • 10–20: moderate. The 2030 target is 12 or less.
  • Above 20: high. Weak antenatal care, infections, unsafe delivery.
Ratio 15.0, rate 14.8. The ratio is a little larger because its denominator leaves out the stillbirths. Both show moderate risk.
14. Perinatal mortality rate and ratio\(\dfrac{F_{late}+D_{early}}{F_{late}+B}\times1000\)
\(\dfrac{F_{late}+D_{early}}{B}\times1000\)		\(F_{late}\): fetal deaths at 28+ weeks
\(D_{early}\): deaths at 0–6 days
  • Below 15: low.
  • 15–30: moderate.
  • Above 30: high. Care around delivery is weak.
Rate 29.7 means about 30 deaths per 1,000 total births from week 28 to day 6. Care in the delivery room is where lives are lost.
15. Adjusted IMR and cohort IMR\(f=\dfrac{D_t\,'}{D_t}\)
\(\IMR_{adj}=\dfrac{D_t}{fB_t+(1-f)B_{t-1}}\)
\(\text{cohort}=\dfrac{\text{all deaths of one birth cohort}}{B}\)		\(f\): separation factor
\(D_t\,'\): deaths to this year\'s births
\(B_t,B_{t-1}\): births this and last year
  • Adjusted is near the naive rate when births are stable.
  • Adjusted is higher when births are rising, lower when they fall.
  • Cohort IMR below period IMR may signal falling mortality, but it needs a full follow-up year.
Naive 45.0, adjusted 45.5, cohort 37.0. The period rate mixes two cohorts; the cohort rate follows one.
16. Endogenous and exogenous infant mortality\(\dfrac{D_{endo}}{B}\times1000,\ \dfrac{D_{exo}}{B}\times1000\)\(D_{endo}\): deaths from birth and genetic causes
\(D_{exo}\): deaths from environment
  • Endogenous over 70% of infant deaths: the environment is mostly controlled. Only medical care can lower it.
  • Roughly 50–70% endogenous: mixed.
  • Exogenous over 50%: infections, water, sanitation and nutrition dominate.
Endogenous 27.0, exogenous 18.0 (60% and 40%). Mixed picture: improve both newborn care and sanitation.
B. Fertility measures
17. Crude birth rate (CBR)\(\CBR=\dfrac{B}{P}\times1000\)\(B\): live births
\(P\): mid-year population
  • Below 15: low. Population is ageing, and may shrink.
  • 15–25: moderate.
  • Above 30: high; above 40 very high. Rapid growth strains schools, jobs and health services.
CBR 25 means 25 births per 1,000 people a year. Moderate-to-high. It counts men and children in the denominator, so it is crude.
18. General fertility rate (GFR)\(\GFR=\dfrac{B}{P_{f,15\text{–}49}}\times1000\)\(P_{f,15-49}\): women aged 15–49
  • Below 50: low fertility.
  • 50–100: moderate.
  • Above 120: high. Many births per woman of reproductive age.
GFR 100 means 100 births per 1,000 women aged 15–49. Moderate to high; it still hides age and marriage patterns.
19. Age-specific fertility rate (ASFR)\(\ASFR_a=\dfrac{B_a}{P_{f,a}}\times1000\)\(B_a\): births to women in group \(a\)
\(P_{f,a}\): women in group \(a\)
  • Hump shape with the peak at 20–29: a usual pattern.
  • Rate at 15–19 above 75: high adolescent fertility. Health risks and school dropout.
  • Peak at 30+: childbearing is being delayed.
ASFR 60 at 15–19 means 6% of girls aged 15–19 gave birth in the year. That is high, so early marriage may be common.
20. Total fertility rate (TFR)\(\TFR=5\sum_a\dfrac{\ASFR_a}{1000}\)5: width of age group
\(\ASFR_a\): rate in group \(a\)
  • Below 1.5: very low. The population will age and shrink.
  • About 2.1: replacement level.
  • Above 4: high. Fast growth, heavy burden on mothers and services.
TFR 3.19 means a woman would have 3.2 children if she lived through this year's rates. That is well above replacement.
21. Rules of thumb\(\GFR\approx4.5\times\CBR\)
\(\TFR\approx30\times\GFR/1000\)		Both are rough checks, not measures
  • First rule holds when women 15–49 are about 22% of the population.
  • If they are above 22%, rule 1 overstates the GFR.
  • Rule 2 assumes flat fertility; a peaked schedule makes it understate the TFR.
Rule 1 gives 112.5 against 100. Rule 2 gives 3.00 against 3.19. Use them to catch mistakes only.
22. General marital fertility rate\(\GMFR=\dfrac{B_m}{P_{f,m}}\times1000\)\(B_m\): births to married women
\(P_{f,m}\): married women 15–49
  • Below 100: low marital fertility, often with contraception.
  • 100–200: typical.
  • Above 250: close to natural fertility. Little birth limiting.
GMFR 130 is higher than the unmarried rate of 44. Most childbearing happens inside marriage.
23. Marital birth ratio\(\dfrac{B_m}{B}\times1000\)\(B_m\): births to married women
\(B\): all births
  • Near 1,000: nearly all births are marital (traditional pattern).
  • Falling below 700: non-marital births are rising.
  • It shows composition, not behaviour. Do not read it as fertility.
Ratio 845: 84.5% of births are marital, and 155 per 1,000 are non-marital. Teenage and unmarried mothers may need support.
24. Duration-specific marital fertility\(\DSFR_i=\dfrac{B_i^m}{P_{f,m,i}}\times1000\)\(B_i^m\): marital births at duration \(i\)
\(P_{f,m,i}\): married women at duration \(i\)
  • Steady fall with duration: spacing and stopping are used.
  • High and flat across durations: little stopping. Large families.
  • Very high at 0–4 years: births come soon after marriage.
DSFR falls from 220 at 0–4 years to 28 at 20+. Fertility is concentrated early in marriage.
25. Age-order specific fertility\(f_a^i=\dfrac{B_a^i}{P_{f,a}}\times1000\)\(B_a^i\): births of order \(i\) to women in group \(a\)
  • First births highest at 20–24 and falling at older ages: an orderly pattern.
  • High share of 4th-or-higher births: large families, low contraception.
  • High first-birth rate at 15–19: early marriage and early motherhood.
Orders add to the ASFR of 190. Fourth-order births are 17.5 per 1,000, so some women still have large families.
26. Direct age-adjusted birth rate (AABR)\(\AABR=\dfrac{\sum f_aP_a^s}{P^s}\)\(f_a\): study ASFR (per 1,000)
\(P_a^s\): standard women
\(P^s\): standard total population
  • No absolute range. Compare with the standard's own CBR.
  • Above the standard's CBR: higher fertility than the standard.
  • Below the standard's CBR: lower fertility than the standard.
AABR 23.35 against the standard's CBR of 19.46 means fertility here is about 20% higher than the national schedule.
27. Indirect age-adjusted birth rate\(\AABR_{in}=\dfrac{b}{\sum F_aP_a^f}\times F\)\(b\): observed births
\(F_a\): standard ASFR
\(F\): standard CBR
  • Observed/expected above 1: more births than the standard schedule predicts.
  • Ratio near 1: same fertility.
  • Ratio below 1: fewer births than predicted.
Ratio 1.20: births are 20% above the expected count. Rate 23.38, close to the direct rate.
C. Reproduction and growth
28. Sex ratio at birth (SRB)\(\SRB=\dfrac{\text{male births}}{\text{female births}}\times100\)Boys per 100 girls
  • 103–107: normal biological range.
  • Above 107: sex-selective abortion or under-reported girls. Future shortage of brides.
  • Below 102: unusual; check registration.
SRB 106.2: normal. The girls' share is 48.5%, used in GRR and NRR.
29. Gross reproduction rate (GRR)\(\GRR=\TFR\times p\)\(p\): share of births that are girls
  • Below 1: daughters do not replace mothers, even with no deaths.
  • About 1.0 (TFR about 2.05): exact replacement.
  • Above 1.5: fast growth in the next generation.
GRR 1.55: each woman has 1.55 daughters if all survive. Moderate-to-fast growth ahead.
30. Net reproduction rate (NRR)\(\NRR=\sum\dfrac{\ASFR_a}{1000}\,p\,\dfrac{L_a}{l_0}\)\(L_a\): life-table person-years in group \(a\)
\(l_0\): radix (100,000)
  • Below 1: each generation is smaller. NRR 0.8 means 20% smaller. Ageing follows.
  • About 1.0: replacement.
  • Above 1: growth. Above 2: very fast growth.
NRR 1.46: the next generation of mothers will be about 46% larger. The population is growing.
31. Replacement-level TFR\(\TFR_{repl}=\dfrac{1}{p\times S},\ S=\dfrac{\NRR}{\GRR}\)\(S\): average survival of women through childbearing ages
  • 2.05 if all girls survive; about 2.1 with low mortality.
  • 2.3–2.5 or more where female mortality is higher.
  • TFR above the level means growth; below it means shrinking (with a lag).
Replacement is 2.18 here, not 2.1. Actual TFR of 3.19 is about 1 child above it.
32. Natural increase rate\(r_n=\dfrac{B-D}{P}\times1000=\CBR-\CDR\)\(B,D\): births and deaths
\(P\): mid-year population
  • Below 0: natural decrease.
  • 0–10: slow growth. 10–20: moderate.
  • Above 25: very fast; the population doubles in under 28 years.
r = 17.0 per 1,000 (1.7% a year, no migration). Doubling time is about 41 years. Growth is fast.
33. Vital index\(\VI=\dfrac{B}{D}\times100\)\(B,D\): births and deaths
  • Below 100: deaths exceed births. Population falls.
  • 100–200: slow growth.
  • Above 300: high growth with a young structure.
VI 312.5: 312 births for every 100 deaths. It ignores age structure.
34. Generation length and annual growth\(r\approx\dfrac{\ln\NRR}{T}\)\(T\): mean age at childbearing (usually 25–32)
\(r\): annual growth
  • Typical \(T\) is 25–32 years.
  • \(r_n\) above this \(r\): momentum from a young age structure.
  • \(r_n\) below \(r\): an ageing population.
r = 1.33% a year against \(r_n\) = 1.70%. The gap is momentum, because many young women are still entering childbearing ages.

Part 2. Practice problems

Work each problem first. Round to two decimals unless told otherwise.

Q1. CDR

Use Table A. Calculate the crude death rate. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\CDR=\frac{3{,}200}{400{,}000}\times1000=8.00\]

Interpretation. There are 8.00 deaths per 1,000 people per year (0.8%). This is moderate.

Impact. It sounds safe, but a young district would show a low CDR even with poor health. Do not decide on this number alone.

Policy. Compute age-specific and standardized rates before planning. Then direct health services to the age groups with the highest rates.

Q2. Three-year average CDR

Use Table B. Calculate the CDR for 2020–2022 by the three methods. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\frac13\sum\frac{D_i}{P_i}=7.965,\quad\frac{9{,}320}{1{,}170{,}000}\times1000=7.966,\quad\frac{3{,}106.7}{390{,}000}\times1000=7.966\]

Interpretation. All three methods give about 7.966 per 1,000, so the series is smooth. Mortality is slowly rising from 7.9 to 8.0.

Impact. A single year was not distorted. The small rise comes from a growing, ageing population.

Policy. Report the three-year figure in planning documents and track it yearly. Start a plan for older-age care early.

Q3. Adjusted CDR

Use Table A. Adjust the CDR using \(C_d=0.85\), \(C_p=0.95\). Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\frac{3{,}200/0.85}{400{,}000/0.95}\times1000=8.94\]

Interpretation. Corrected CDR is 8.94 per 1,000, higher than the raw 8.0. Deaths are missed more than people are counted.

Impact. True mortality is about 12% higher than the register shows. Hospital beds, medicines and budgets sized on the raw rate fall short.

Policy. Strengthen death registration: train local registrars, link burial and union council records, and run a post-enumeration survey.

Q4. Annualized monthly rate

Use Table A. Annualize the January deaths. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\frac{365}{31}\times\frac{300}{400{,}000}\times1000=8.83\]

Interpretation. January gives 8.83 against 8.0 for the year. January is a high-mortality month, probably from cold-season illness.

Impact. Using January as the annual rate would overstate mortality by about 10%. It also points to a seasonal peak in deaths.

Policy. Prepare winter plans: vaccination, respiratory care stock, and warm-season data comparisons before action.

Q5. Cause-specific death rate and ratio

Use Table A. Calculate the heart-disease death rate (per 1,000 and per 100,000) and the death ratio. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\frac{960}{400{,}000}\times1000=2.40,\ \ 240\text{ per }100{,}000,\quad\frac{960}{3{,}200}\times100=30\%\]

Interpretation. Heart disease kills 2.4 per 1,000 people and makes up 30% of deaths.

Impact. A third of deaths come from one non-communicable cause. Mortality is shifting away from infections.

Policy. Add blood-pressure screening at community clinics, tobacco control and affordable statins.

Q6. ASDR

Use Table C. Calculate the age-specific death rates for Padma. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[M_{0-14}=5.0,\ M_{15-44}=2.0,\ M_{45-64}=11.0,\ M_{65+}=75.5\]

Interpretation. Rates form a J shape. The 65+ rate is 75.5, about 38 times the 15–44 rate. The 0–14 rate of 5.0 is high against the State rate of 4.0.

Impact. Old-age deaths dominate counts. But children also die above the State level, so child survival is not secure.

Policy. Fund child health (immunization, nutrition) and begin geriatric care. Review each against the State rates.

Q7. Rate to probability

Use Table A. Find the probability of dying at 80+ from the rate 800/4,000. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[m=0.200,\quad q=1-e^{-0.2}=0.1813\]

Interpretation. An 80+ person has an 18.1% chance of dying within the year. It is lower than the 20% rate.

Impact. Using the rate as a probability overstates risk, so life tables and pension costs would be wrong.

Policy. Use \(q\) in actuarial work for pensions and long-term care planning for those over 80.

Q8. Direct standardized rate

Use Table C. Standardize Padma's CDR with the State as standard. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\sum\frac{P_a^s}{P^s}M_a=.25(5.0)+.45(2.0)+.20(11.0)+.10(75.5)=11.90\]

Interpretation. The standardized rate is 11.90 per 1,000 against a crude rate of 8.0. Padma is younger than the State.

Impact. Padma's mortality is higher than the State's at every age (5.0 vs 4.0, 2.0 vs 1.6, 11.0 vs 9.0, 75.5 vs 70). The young structure hid it.

Policy. Treat Padma as a high-mortality district: allocate more doctors and clinics, and raise health budget per person.

Q9. SMR and indirect adjusted rate

Use Table C. Compute the SMR and the adjusted rate. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\text{expected}=2,764,\quad\SMR=\frac{3{,}200}{2,764}\times100=115.8,\quad\text{adj.}=115.8\%\times10.52=12.18\]

Interpretation. The SMR is 115.8: Padma has about 16% more deaths than expected at State rates. Adjusted rate is 12.18.

Impact. About 440 deaths a year are in excess of the State pattern. This agrees with the direct result.

Policy. Set a target to bring SMR to 100: audit the excess deaths by age, then expand the services for the worst age group.

Q10. IMR

Use Table A. Calculate the infant mortality rate. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\IMR=\frac{450}{10{,}000}\times1000=45.0\]

Interpretation. 45 of every 1,000 live births die before age 1. This is high.

Impact. About 450 families lose a baby each year. Child deaths also keep families wanting more births (insurance effect).

Policy. Scale up immunization, oral rehydration, exclusive breastfeeding and community health worker visits.

Q11. NMR and PNMR

Use Table A. Calculate NMR and PNMR and compare with IMR. Then (a) interpret the result, (b) interpret the impact of the result on the population, (c) state one demographic policy you would take.

Show solution
\[\NMR=\frac{300}{10{,}000}\times1000=30,\quad\PNMR=\frac{150}{10{,}000}\times1000=15,\quad30+15=45\]

Interpretation. NMR is 30 (SDG target is 12). It is two-thirds of IMR. PNMR is 15.

Impact. Most deaths occur in the first month, from prematurity and birth complications, so care at and just after birth is failing.
