Background and basics

  • Interval estimation gives a range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter, based on sample data.
  • It exists to quantify the uncertainty of using sample statistics to learn about populations.
  • Its foundation is probability theory and sampling distributions.

Point estimate

A single best guess, computed from the sample. Convenient, but says nothing about variability or reliability.
Example: \(\xb=75\) gives \(\mu\approx75\).

Interval estimate

A range plus a confidence level (\(95\%\), \(99\%\)), with a margin of error for sampling variability.
Example: \(95\%\) CI \(\mu\in[72,78]\).

Precisely: before sampling, the random interval covers \(\mu\) with probability \(1-\alpha\). After sampling, the computed interval is called a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval, and \(1-\alpha\) is the confidence coefficient.

CI for a mean, \(\sigma\) known

\(X_1,\dots,X_n\) is a random sample from \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2\) known, so \(\Xb\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\). Find \(\zz\) so that

\[P\!\left(-\zz\le\frac{\Xb-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\le\zz\right)=1-\alpha\]

where \(\Xb\) = sample mean, \(\mu\) = population mean, \(\sigma\) = known population standard deviation, \(n\) = sample size, \(\zz\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha/2\), \(1-\alpha\) = confidence level.

Examples: \(1-\alpha=0.95\) gives \(z_{0.025}=1.96\); \(1-\alpha=0.90\) gives \(z_{0.05}=1.645\). Since \(\sigma>0\) these are equivalent:

  1. \(-z\sigma/\sqrt n\le\Xb-\mu\le z\sigma/\sqrt n\)
  2. \(-\Xb-z\sigma/\sqrt n\le-\mu\le-\Xb+z\sigma/\sqrt n\)
  3. \(\Xb-z\sigma/\sqrt n\le\mu\le\Xb+z\sigma/\sqrt n\)
\[\xb\pm\zz\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\quad\text{is a }100(1-\alpha)\%\text{ CI for }\mu\]

where \(\xb\) = observed sample mean, \(\zz\,\sigma/\sqrt n\) = margin of error, \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) = confidence level.

Notes

  • The interval is centered at \(\xb\) and extended by \(\zz\,\sigma/\sqrt n\) on each side.
  • As \(n\) increases the margin shrinks: a shorter, more precise interval at the same confidence. With unlimited time and money you can make it as short as you like.
  • For fixed \(n\), lowering \(1-\alpha\) also shortens it, but you lose confidence.
Width widget. Move the sliders and watch the margin.
Example: light bulbs. \(X\sim N(\mu,1296)\), so \(\sigma=36\). \(n=27\) bulbs gave \(\xb=1478\) h. \(95\%\) CI:
\[1478\pm1.96\,\frac{36}{\sqrt{27}}=1478\pm13.58=[1464.42,\,1491.58]\]

CI for a mean, \(\sigma\) unknown

Large \(n\) (30 or more)

\((\Xb-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\) is approximately \(N(0,1)\), whether or not the population is normal. Use \(\xb\pm\zz\,(s/\sqrt n)\). But if the distribution is badly skewed or has outliers, even \(n=50\) may not work well.

Example: Lake Macatawa sodium. 32 water samples (ppm): 13.0 18.5 16.4 14.8 19.4 17.3 23.2 24.9 20.8 19.3 18.8 23.1 15.2 19.9 19.1 18.1 25.1 16.8 20.4 17.4 25.2 23.1 15.3 19.4 16.0 21.7 15.2 21.3 21.5 16.8 15.6 17.6. Approximate \(95\%\) CI with \(\xb\pm1.96\,(s/\sqrt n)\): \(\B{[17.94,\,20.20]}\). Paste the data into the calculator below to check.

Small \(n\), normal data: the \(t\) distribution

When \(n\) is small and \(\sigma\) is unknown, use

\[T=\frac{\Xb-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t\text{ with }n-1\text{ degrees of freedom}\]

where \(T\) = the \(t\) statistic, \(\Xb\) = sample mean, \(S\) = sample standard deviation, \(\mu\) = population mean, \(n\) = sample size, \(n-1\) = degrees of freedom.

\(S^2\) is the unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\). Pick \(\ta(n-1)\) with \(P[T\ge\ta(n-1)]=\alpha/2\). Then \(1-\alpha=P(-t\le T\le t)\), and rearranging for \(\mu\) gives

\[\xb\pm\ta(n-1)\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n}\]

where \(\ta(n-1)\) = \(t\) value with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom and upper-tail area \(\alpha/2\), \(s\) = observed sample standard deviation, \(s/\sqrt n\) = estimated standard error of the mean.

Example: butterfat. \(n=20\) cows, \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). Data: 481 537 513 583 453 510 570 500 457 555 618 327 350 643 499 421 505 637 599 392. Find a \(90\%\) CI. Here \(\xb=507.50\), \(s=89.75\), \(t_{0.05}(19)=1.729\):
\[507.50\pm1.729\,\frac{89.75}{\sqrt{20}}=507.50\pm34.70=[472.80,\,542.20]\]

One-sided CI for a mean

Sometimes you only need a lower or upper bound. With \(\sigma\) known, \(P\!\left(\frac{\Xb-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\le\za\right)=1-\alpha\), equivalently \(P[\Xb-\za\sigma/\sqrt n\le\mu]=1-\alpha\). Note the tail is \(\alpha\), not \(\alpha/2\).

Lower bound
\[[\xb-\za\sigma/\sqrt n,\ \infty)\]
Upper bound
\[(-\infty,\ \xb+\za\sigma/\sqrt n]\]

where \(\za\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha\) (not \(\alpha/2\)), \(\xb\) = observed sample mean, \(\sigma\) = known standard deviation, \(n\) = sample size.

With \(\sigma\) unknown use \(T\): bounds are \(\xb-t_{\alpha}(n-1)s/\sqrt n\) and \(\xb+t_{\alpha}(n-1)s/\sqrt n\).

CI for a difference of two means

1. Variances known

Independent samples from \(N(\mu_X,\sigma^2_X)\) and \(N(\mu_Y,\sigma^2_Y)\). Then \(\Xb\) and \(\Yb\) are independent and

\[W=\Xb-\Yb\sim N\!\left(\mu_X-\mu_Y,\ \frac{\sigma^2_X}{\nX}+\frac{\sigma^2_Y}{\nY}\right)\]

where \(W\) = difference of sample means, \(\Xb,\Yb\) = sample means of the two independent samples, \(\mu_X,\mu_Y\) = population means, \(\sigma^2_X,\sigma^2_Y\) = known population variances, \(\nX,\nY\) = sample sizes.

With \(\sigma_W=\sqrt{\sigma^2_X/\nX+\sigma^2_Y/\nY}\), the interval is centered at \(\xb-\yb\):

\[\xb-\yb\pm\zz\,\sigma_W\]

where \(\sigma_W\) = standard deviation of \(W\), \(\xb-\yb\) = observed difference of sample means, \(\zz\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha/2\).

Example. \(\nX=15\), \(\nY=8\), \(\xb=70.1\), \(\yb=75.3\), \(\sigma^2_X=60\), \(\sigma^2_Y=40\), \(90\%\) (\(z=1.645\)). \(1.645\sqrt{60/15+40/8}=4.935\), so \([-5.2-4.935,\,-5.2+4.935]=\B{[-10.135,\,-0.265]}\). The whole interval is below zero, so we suspect \(\mu_Y>\mu_X\).

2. Unknown variances, large samples

\[\xb-\yb\pm\zz\sqrt{\frac{s^2_X}{\nX}+\frac{s^2_Y}{\nY}}\]

where \(s^2_X,s^2_Y\) = sample variances of the two samples, used in place of \(\sigma^2_X,\sigma^2_Y\).

3. Unknown variances, small samples (assume \(\sigma^2_X=\sigma^2_Y=\sigma^2\))

\(T=Z/\sqrt{U/(\nX+\nY-2)}\) has a \(t\) distribution with \(\nX+\nY-2\) df. With \(t_0=\ta(\nX+\nY-2)\) and the pooled standard deviation

\[S_p=\sqrt{\frac{(\nX-1)S^2_X+(\nY-1)S^2_Y}{\nX+\nY-2}}\]
\[\xb-\yb\pm t_0\,s_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nX}+\frac{1}{\nY}}\]

where \(S^2_X,S^2_Y\) = sample variances, \(S_p\) = pooled standard deviation (\(s_p\) its observed value), \(\sigma^2\) = common population variance, \(t_0=\ta(\nX+\nY-2)\) = \(t\) value with \(\nX+\nY-2\) degrees of freedom.

Example: large vs small schools. \(\nX=9\), \(\xb=81.31\), \(s^2_X=60.76\); \(\nY=15\), \(\yb=78.61\), \(s^2_Y=48.24\). \(t_{0.025}(22)=2.074\). \(95\%\) CI \(=2.70\pm2.074\sqrt{\frac{8\cdot60.76+14\cdot48.24}{22}\left(\frac19+\frac1{15}\right)}=\B{[-3.65,\,9.05]}\).

4. Dependent (paired) variables

Two measurements on the same subject (before and after) are dependent, so the independent-sample \(t\) intervals are not allowed. Form \(D_i=X_i-Y_i\), treat them as a sample from \(N(\mu_D,\sigma^2_D)\), and use \(T=(\Db-\mu_D)/(S_D/\sqrt n)\sim t_{n-1}\). This is the single-mean interval applied to the differences:

\[\db\pm\ta(n-1)\,\frac{s_D}{\sqrt n}\]

where \(\db\) = mean of the paired differences \(D_i=X_i-Y_i\), \(s_D\) = standard deviation of the differences, \(n\) = number of pairs.

Example: reaction to red vs green light. 8 subjects, \(d=-0.13,\,-0.09,\,-0.17,\,0.07,\,-0.03,\,-0.05,\,0.00,\,-0.10\). \(\db=-0.0625\), \(s_D=0.0765\), \(t_{0.025}(7)=2.365\). \(95\%\) CI \(=\B{[-0.1265,\,0.0015]}\). Zero is just inside; more data might exclude it, which would suggest faster reaction to red.

CI for a proportion

For \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials with success probability \(p\), \(Y/n\) is an unbiased estimator of \(p\), and \((Y/n-p)/\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) is approximately \(N(0,1)\) for large \(n\). The unknown \(p\) appears in the endpoints, so there are ways out:

Eq 3.2: replace \(p\) by \(\ph=y/n\)

\[\ph\pm\zz\sqrt{\frac{\ph(1-\ph)}{n}}\]

where \(y\) = number of successes, \(n\) = number of trials, \(\ph=y/n\) = sample proportion, \(\zz\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha/2\).

Eq 3.4: solve the quadratic \(H(p)\le0\)

The condition is equivalent to \(H(p)=(Y/n-p)^2-z^2_{\alpha/2}\,p(1-p)/n\le0\), a quadratic in \(p\). Its zeros are the endpoints:

\[\frac{\ph+\dfrac{z_0^2}{2n}\pm z_0\sqrt{\dfrac{\ph(1-\ph)}{n}+\dfrac{z_0^2}{4n^2}}}{1+\dfrac{z_0^2}{n}}\]

where \(z_0=\zz\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha/2\), \(\ph\) = sample proportion, \(n\) = number of trials.

For large \(n\), 3.2 and 3.4 are nearly equal.

Eq 3.5: Agresti–Coull adjustment

3.2 can miss its stated confidence when \(n\) is small or \(Y\) or \(n-Y\) is near 0 (\(Y=0\) or \(n\) is clearly broken). Agresti and Coull (1998) replace \(Y\) with \(Y+z^2/2\) and \(n\) with \(n+z^2\):

\[\tilde p=\frac{Y+z^2_{\alpha/2}/2}{n+z^2_{\alpha/2}},\qquad\tilde p\pm\zz\sqrt{\frac{\tilde p(1-\tilde p)}{n+z^2_{\alpha/2}}}\]

where \(Y\) = number of successes, \(n\) = number of trials, \(\tilde p\) = adjusted sample proportion, \(\zz\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha/2\).

The slide calls the \(\zz=1\) case the Wilson interval. If \(1-\alpha\) isn’t stated, these are usually read as \(95\%\) (\(z=1.96\)).

One-sided

Upper bound
\[\left[0,\ \ph+\za\sqrt{\ph(1-\ph)/n}\right]\]
Lower bound
\[\left[\ph-\za\sqrt{\ph(1-\ph)/n},\ 1\right]\]

where \(\ph\) = sample proportion, \(n\) = number of trials, \(\za\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha\).

Example 1 (candy weights). \(n=40\), \(y=8\), \(\ph=0.20\), \(90\%\) (\(z=1.645\)). Eq 3.2: \(\B{[0.096,\,0.304]}\). Eq 3.4: \(\B{[0.117,\,0.321]}\). With \(n=400\), \(y=80\): \([0.167,\,0.233]\) and \([0.169,\,0.235]\), which barely differ.
Example 3 (same data, Eq 3.5, \(95\%\)). \(\tilde p=0.226\), CI \(\B{[0.102,\,0.350]}\); for \(n=400\), \([0.181,\,0.242]\).
Example 2 (poll). \(y=155\) of \(n=351\); \(95\%\) CI by Eq 3.2 is \(0.527\pm1.96\sqrt{0.527\cdot0.473/351}=\B{[0.475,\,0.579]}\). Since \(0.5\) is inside, \(p\) may be below \(50\%\). Check: the slide says \(155/351=0.52\) and uses \(0.527\), but \(155/351\approx0.442\). Likely a typo in the slide.

CI for a difference of two proportions

\(n_1\) and \(n_2\) independent trials give \(Y_1\) and \(Y_2\) successes. \(Y_1/n_1-Y_2/n_2\) has mean \(p_1-p_2\) and variance \(p_1(1-p_1)/n_1+p_2(1-p_2)/n_2\), and is approximately normal. Replacing \(p_1,p_2\) in the denominator by \(Y_1/n_1,\,Y_2/n_2\) keeps the ratio approximately \(N(0,1)\) for large \(n_1,n_2\). So:

\[\frac{y_1}{n_1}-\frac{y_2}{n_2}\pm\zz\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)}{n_2}}\]

where \(y_1,y_2\) = numbers of successes, \(n_1,n_2\) = numbers of trials, \(\hat p_1,\hat p_2\) = sample proportions \(y_i/n_i\), \(\zz\) = standard normal value with upper-tail area \(\alpha/2\).

Example 4: detergents. 63 of 91 versus 42 of 79; \(\hat p_1=0.692\), \(\hat p_2=0.532\). \(90\%\): \(0.160\pm0.136=\B{(0.024,\,0.328)}\). It lies entirely right of zero, so detergent 1 is probably better.

Finding \(z\), \(t\) and \(F\) values from tables

Table finder.
\(t\), \(\chi^2\) and \(F\) values are computed from their exact distribution functions (for \(F\), \(r_1\) is the numerator and \(r_2\) the denominator df); always confirm against your printed table.

\(z\) table

  1. Compute \(\alpha=1-\text{confidence}\).
  2. Two-sided: tail area \(\alpha/2\); one-sided: \(\alpha\).
  3. Cumulative tables: look for \(1-\text{tail}\) (e.g. \(0.975\)) in the body. Row gives the first decimal, column the second: \(0.975\) is row \(1.9\), column \(0.06\), so \(z=1.96\).
  4. Common values: \(90\%\): \(1.645\), \(95\%\): \(1.96\), \(99\%\): \(2.576\).

\(t\) table

  1. Find df: \(n-1\) (one mean or paired), \(\nX+\nY-2\) (pooled).
  2. Go to the row for df.
  3. Pick the column for tail area \(\alpha/2\) (two-sided) or \(\alpha\) (one-sided), or cumulative \(1-\text{tail}\) if columns are labelled that way.
  4. Example: \(90\%\) two-sided, df \(19\) gives \(t_{0.05}(19)=1.729\); \(95\%\), df \(7\) gives \(2.365\).
  5. Large df: \(t\) approaches \(z\).

\(F\) table (for variance intervals)

  1. \(F\) depends on two dfs: numerator \(r_1\) (columns) and denominator \(r_2\) (rows). One table is printed per tail area.
  2. Choose the table with tail area \(\alpha/2\) and read \(F_{\alpha/2}(r_1,r_2)\) at column \(r_1\), row \(r_2\).
  3. Lower-tail values are not printed: \(F_{1-\alpha/2}(r_1,r_2)=1/F_{\alpha/2}(r_2,r_1)\), swapping the dfs.
  4. For a single variance the \(\chi^2\) table is used the same way: row = df, column = tail area.

\(\chi^2\) table

  1. \(\chi^2\) has one df \(r\) (rows). Tables list right-tail values for several tail areas (columns); the shape is skewed, not symmetric.
  2. Upper value \(\chi^2_{\alpha/2}(r)\): column with right-tail area \(\alpha/2\). Lower value \(\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2}(r)\): column with right-tail area \(1-\alpha/2\).
  3. For one variance, \(r=n-1\) and the usual interval is \(\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2}(n-1)}\le\sigma^2\le\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2}(n-1)}\). The lecture has not covered this yet (“To appear”), so it is shown only for table practice.

Derivations

Every derivation from the lecture, step by step. Tap one to open it.

D1Interval for a mean, \(\sigma\) knowntwo-sided \(z\) interval
  1. \(X_1,\dots,X_n\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) gives \(\Xb\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\), so \[\frac{\Xb-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1).\]
  2. Pick \(\zz\) from the normal table (\(z_{0.025}=1.96\), \(z_{0.05}=1.645\)): \[P\!\left(-\zz\le\frac{\Xb-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\le\zz\right)=1-\alpha\]
  3. With \(\sigma>0\), multiply by \(\sigma/\sqrt n\): \[-\zz\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\Xb-\mu\le\zz\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]
  4. Subtract \(\Xb\): \[-\Xb-\zz\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le-\mu\le-\Xb+\zz\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]
  5. Multiply by \(-1\) (inequalities flip): \[\Xb+\zz\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ge\mu\ge\Xb-\zz\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]
  6. Therefore \[P\!\left(\Xb-\zz\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\mu\le\Xb+\zz\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right)=1-\alpha\] The random interval covers \(\mu\) with probability \(1-\alpha\).
  7. After observing \(\Xb=\xb\), \(\xb\pm\zz\,\sigma/\sqrt n\) is the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI. Its length \(2\zz\sigma/\sqrt n\) shrinks as \(n\) grows or as \(1-\alpha\) falls.
D2Large-sample interval for a mean, \(\sigma\) unknown\(z\) with \(s\)

For large \(n\) (30 or more), \[\frac{\Xb-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\approx N(0,1)\] whether or not the population is normal. Repeat the steps of D1 with \(\sigma\) replaced by \(S\): \[P\!\left(-\zz\le\frac{\Xb-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\le\zz\right)\approx1-\alpha\ \Rightarrow\ \xb\pm\zz\,s/\sqrt n\] If the data are badly skewed or have outliers, even \(n=50\) may not be enough.

D3Student’s \(t\) interval for a mean, \(\sigma\) unknownsmall \(n\), normal data
  1. For a normal sample, \[T=\frac{\Xb-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\] has a \(t\) distribution with \(n-1\) df; \(S^2\) is the unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\).
  2. Select \(\ta(n-1)\) with \(P[T\ge\ta(n-1)]=\alpha/2\).
  3. \[1-\alpha=P\!\left(-\ta(n-1)\le\frac{\Xb-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\le\ta(n-1)\right)\]
  4. Multiply by \(S/\sqrt n\), subtract \(\Xb\), multiply by \(-1\) (as in D1): \[1-\alpha=P\!\left(\Xb-\ta(n-1)\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\le\mu\le\Xb+\ta(n-1)\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\right)\]
  5. With observed \(\xb\), \(s^2\): \(\xb\pm\ta(n-1)\,s/\sqrt n\).
D4One-sided confidence bounds for a mean\(\sigma\) known or unknown
  1. Put the whole tail \(\alpha\) on one side: \[P\!\left(\frac{\Xb-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\le\za\right)=1-\alpha\]
  2. Then \(\Xb-\mu\le\za\sigma/\sqrt n\), i.e. \[P\!\left[\Xb-\za\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\mu\right]=1-\alpha\]
  3. Observed: \([\xb-\za\sigma/\sqrt n,\ \infty)\), so \(\xb-\za\sigma/\sqrt n\) is a lower bound.
  4. Symmetrically \[P\!\left(\frac{\Xb-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\ge-\za\right)=1-\alpha\] gives \(\mu\le\Xb+\za\sigma/\sqrt n\): \((-\infty,\ \xb+\za\sigma/\sqrt n]\), an upper bound.
  5. \(\sigma\) unknown: use \(T=\dfrac{\Xb-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\) and \(t_\alpha(n-1)\): bounds \(\xb\mp t_\alpha(n-1)\,s/\sqrt n\).
D5Difference of two means, variances known\(z\) interval
  1. Independent samples from \(N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2)\), \(N(\mu_Y,\sigma_Y^2)\): \[\Xb\sim N\!\left(\mu_X,\tfrac{\sigma_X^2}{\nX}\right),\qquad\Yb\sim N\!\left(\mu_Y,\tfrac{\sigma_Y^2}{\nY}\right),\quad\text{independent.}\]
  2. A difference of independent normals is normal; means subtract, variances add: \[W=\Xb-\Yb\sim N\!\left(\mu_X-\mu_Y,\ \tfrac{\sigma_X^2}{\nX}+\tfrac{\sigma_Y^2}{\nY}\right)\]
  3. Standardize with \(\sigma_W=\sqrt{\sigma_X^2/\nX+\sigma_Y^2/\nY}\): \[P\!\left(-\zz\le\frac{(\Xb-\Yb)-(\mu_X-\mu_Y)}{\sigma_W}\le\zz\right)=1-\alpha\]
  4. Rewrite: \[P\Big((\Xb-\Yb)-\zz\sigma_W\le\mu_X-\mu_Y\le(\Xb-\Yb)+\zz\sigma_W\Big)=1-\alpha\]
  5. Observed: \(\xb-\yb\pm\zz\,\sigma_W\). For large \(n\) with unknown variances, replace \(\sigma^2\) by \(S^2\).
D6Difference of two means, pooled \(t\) intervalsmall \(n\), \(\sigma_X^2=\sigma_Y^2\)
  1. Assume \(\sigma_X^2=\sigma_Y^2=\sigma^2\). Let \[Z=\frac{\Xb-\Yb-(\mu_X-\mu_Y)}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/\nX+\sigma^2/\nY}}\sim N(0,1)\] and \[U=\frac{(\nX-1)S_X^2}{\sigma^2}+\frac{(\nY-1)S_Y^2}{\sigma^2},\] a \(\chi^2\) with \(\nX+\nY-2\) df.
  2. \[T=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{U/(\nX+\nY-2)}}\] has a \(t\) distribution with \(\nX+\nY-2\) df.
  3. \(\sigma^2\) cancels: \[T=\frac{\Xb-\Yb-(\mu_X-\mu_Y)}{\sqrt{\dfrac{(\nX-1)S_X^2+(\nY-1)S_Y^2}{\nX+\nY-2}\left[\dfrac1{\nX}+\dfrac1{\nY}\right]}}\]
  4. With \(t_0=\ta(\nX+\nY-2)\), \(P(-t_0\le T\le t_0)=1-\alpha\). Write the pooled standard deviation \[S_p=\sqrt{\frac{(\nX-1)S_X^2+(\nY-1)S_Y^2}{\nX+\nY-2}}\] and solve for \(\mu_X-\mu_Y\): \[P\!\left(\Xb-\Yb-t_0S_p\sqrt{\tfrac1{\nX}+\tfrac1{\nY}}\le\mu_X-\mu_Y\le\Xb-\Yb+t_0S_p\sqrt{\tfrac1{\nX}+\tfrac1{\nY}}\right)=1-\alpha\]
  5. Observed: \(\xb-\yb\pm t_0\,s_p\sqrt{1/\nX+1/\nY}\).
D7Paired-sample interval for dependent measurements\(t\) on the differences
  1. \(X\) and \(Y\) are measured on the same subject, so they may be dependent. The independent-sample \(t\) statistics and intervals are not allowed.
  2. Form \(D_i=X_i-Y_i\), \(i=1,\dots,n\), and treat them as a sample from \(N(\mu_D,\sigma_D^2)\), with \(\mu_D=\mu_X-\mu_Y\).
  3. \[T=\frac{\Db-\mu_D}{S_D/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1},\] where \(\Db\), \(S_D\) are the mean and s.d. of the \(n\) differences.
  4. As in D3: \(\db\pm\ta(n-1)\,s_D/\sqrt n\) is a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) CI for \(\mu_D=\mu_X-\mu_Y\).
D8Interval for a proportion: the plug-in methodEquations 3.1 and 3.2
  1. \(Y\sim b(n,p)\) counts successes in \(n\) Bernoulli trials; \(Y/n\) is unbiased for \(p\).
  2. Dividing top and bottom by \(n\): \[\frac{Y-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}=\frac{Y/n-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\approx N(0,1)\quad(n\text{ large})\]
  3. Eq 3.1: \[P\!\left(-\zz\le\frac{Y/n-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\le\zz\right)\approx1-\alpha\]
  4. Solving as in D1: \[P\!\left(\frac Yn-\zz\sqrt{\tfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\le p\le\frac Yn+\zz\sqrt{\tfrac{p(1-p)}{n}}\right)\approx1-\alpha\] but \(p\) sits in the endpoints.
  5. Way out 1: replace \(p\) by \(Y/n\) inside \(p(1-p)\). For observed \(Y=y\): \[\ph\pm\zz\sqrt{\ph(1-\ph)/n},\quad\ph=y/n\qquad(3.2)\]
D9Interval for a proportion: solving the quadraticEquations 3.3 and 3.4
  1. The inequality \[\frac{|Y/n-p|}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\le\zz\] is equivalent to \[H(p)=(Y/n-p)^2-z_{\alpha/2}^2\,\frac{p(1-p)}{n}\le0\qquad(3.3)\]
  2. \(H\) is quadratic in \(p\). With \(\ph=Y/n\), \(z_0=\zz\): \[H(p)=\left(1+\tfrac{z_0^2}{n}\right)p^2-\left(2\ph+\tfrac{z_0^2}{n}\right)p+\ph^2\]
  3. \(H(p)\le0\) between its two zeros. The discriminant is \[\left(2\ph+\tfrac{z_0^2}{n}\right)^2-4\left(1+\tfrac{z_0^2}{n}\right)\ph^2=4z_0^2\left[\tfrac{\ph(1-\ph)}{n}+\tfrac{z_0^2}{4n^2}\right]\]
  4. The quadratic formula gives the endpoints: \[\frac{\ph+\frac{z_0^2}{2n}\pm z_0\sqrt{\frac{\ph(1-\ph)}{n}+\frac{z_0^2}{4n^2}}}{1+z_0^2/n}\qquad(3.4)\]
  5. For large \(n\), \(z_0^2/4n^2\), \(z_0^2/2n\) and \(z_0^2/n\) are small, so 3.2 and 3.4 are about equal.
D10Interval for a proportion: one-sided bounds and Agresti–CoullEquation 3.5
  1. One-sided: put tail \(\alpha\) on one side, as in D4. With \(\ph=y/n\), the upper and lower bounds are \[\left[0,\ \ph+\za\sqrt{\frac{\ph(1-\ph)}{n}}\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left[\ph-\za\sqrt{\frac{\ph(1-\ph)}{n}},\ 1\right]\] with approximate coefficient \(1-\alpha\).
  2. Eq 3.2 can miss its stated confidence for small \(n\), or \(Y\) or \(n-Y\) near 0 (if \(Y=0\) or \(n\), the interval collapses to a point).
  3. Agresti and Coull (1998): replace \(Y\) by \(Y+z_{\alpha/2}^2/2\) and \(n\) by \(n+z_{\alpha/2}^2\): \[\tilde p=\frac{Y+z_{\alpha/2}^2/2}{n+z_{\alpha/2}^2}\]
  4. Use \(n+z_{\alpha/2}^2\) in the radical too: \[\tilde p\pm\zz\sqrt{\frac{\tilde p(1-\tilde p)}{n+z_{\alpha/2}^2}}\qquad(3.5)\] The slide calls the \(\zz=1\) case the Wilson interval. If \(1-\alpha\) is not stated, \(z=1.96\) is understood.
D11Difference of two proportions\(z\) interval
  1. \(Y_1/n_1\) and \(Y_2/n_2\) are independent with means \(p_1,p_2\) and variances \[\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1},\qquad\frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}.\]
  2. The difference has mean \(p_1-p_2\) and variance \[\frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2},\] and is approximately normal, so the standardized ratio is approximately \(N(0,1)\).
  3. Replacing \(p_i\) in the denominator by \(Y_i/n_i\) keeps it approximately \(N(0,1)\) for large \(n_1,n_2\): \[P\!\left(-\zz\le\frac{\frac{Y_1}{n_1}-\frac{Y_2}{n_2}-(p_1-p_2)}{\sqrt{\frac{\frac{Y_1}{n_1}(1-\frac{Y_1}{n_1})}{n_1}+\frac{\frac{Y_2}{n_2}(1-\frac{Y_2}{n_2})}{n_2}}}\le\zz\right)\approx1-\alpha\]
  4. Solving for \(p_1-p_2\) with observed \(y_1,y_2\): \[\frac{y_1}{n_1}-\frac{y_2}{n_2}\pm\zz\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)}{n_2}}\]
