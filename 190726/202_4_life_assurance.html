Life Assurance

A payment that happens when someone dies — but nobody knows when. This page prices that promise: the five product types, the expected present value built term by term, the \(C\) and \(M\) commutation functions, every formula derived, and a calculator that builds them straight from a life table.

1 · A real-life case, solved

Daniel, aged exactly 40, has just taken out a 5-year term assurance with a sum assured of \$100,000. The contract says: if Daniel dies within the next five years, the insurer pays \$100,000 at the end of the year of death; if he survives the five years, it pays nothing.

The insurer prices at \(i=5\%\) per year, and the mortality basis is summarised by this slice of the life table:

Age \(y\)404142434445
\(\ell_{y}\)90,00089,10088,11087,02985,85984,600
\(d_{y}=\ell_{y}-\ell_{y+1}\)9009901,0811,1701,259—

Question: how much must the insurer hold today for this policy, and why is that figure so much smaller than \$100,000?

Step 1 · What can happen? There are only six mutually exclusive outcomes: Daniel dies in year 1, or in year 2, or in year 3, or in year 4, or in year 5, or he survives all five years and the insurer pays nothing. Each outcome has a probability and a present value. The expected present value is the probability-weighted sum.
Step 2 · Probability that death happens in year \(k\). For the insurer to pay in year \(k\), Daniel must first survive \(k-1\) years, then die in the next one. Using the life table directly: \[P(\text{death in year }k)=\frac{d_{40+k-1}}{\ell_{40}},\qquad \frac{d_{41}}{\ell_{40}}=\frac{990}{90{,}000}=0.01100,\ \ \frac{d_{42}}{\ell_{40}}=\frac{1{,}081}{90{,}000}=0.01201,\ \dots\] This is exactly \({}_{k-1}p_{40}\,q_{40+k-1}\) — survival times the conditional death probability — with the \(\ell\)'s cancelling.
Step 3 · Present value of each payment. The payment lands at the end of the year of death, so the discount factor is \(v^{k}\) with \(v=1/1.05=0.952381\): \[v^{1}=0.952381,\quad v^{2}=0.907029,\quad v^{3}=0.863838,\quad v^{4}=0.822702,\quad v^{5}=0.783526.\]
Step 4 · Combine, term by term. Each term is \(\text{probability}\times v^{k}\times 100{,}000\).

Check the probabilities: \(0.010+0.011+0.012011+0.013+0.013989=0.060\) — that is exactly \({}_{5}q_{40}\), the chance Daniel dies within the term. The remaining \(0.94\) is the "no payment" outcome, which contributes nothing.

Step 5 · Read the answer. Per dollar of cover the factor is \[A^{1}_{40:\overline{5}|}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}v^{k}\frac{d_{40+k-1}}{\ell_{40}}=0.051534,\qquad \text{EPV}=100{,}000\times0.051534=\$5{,}153.44.\]
The insurer needs only \$5,153.44 today against a \$100,000 promise — because the promise is paid only with probability \(6\%\). The premium is small; the exposure is large.
Step 6 · The insurer is not guessing. Across 10,000 identical policies the insurer holds \(10{,}000\times\$5{,}153.44=\$51.5\) million. The actual number of claims will be close to \(600\), not exactly 600 — but the law of large numbers makes the total payout predictable even though each individual death is not.
What changes if the cover is whole-life instead? Then the sum does not stop at \(k=5\): every future year contributes a term, \(\ell_{40}\) being shared by all of them. The factor rises (from about 0.052 to roughly 0.15 on a typical table) and the insurer must hold a great deal more. That is the difference between term and whole-life assurance — same mechanics, different set of payment times.

2 · Definition

The keywords from the case were sum assured, specified death-related event, uncertain time of payment, probability-weighted and expected present value. Put together:

Life assurance A contract under which the insurer pays a specified amount — the sum assured — when a specified death-related event occurs. The event may be death at any time (whole-life), death within a term (term assurance), survival to the end of a term (pure endowment), or either of the last two (endowment assurance). Because the future lifetime is uncertain, the timing of the payment is uncertain, so the value of the contract is the expected present value: the probability-weighted value of all future payments.
Annuity
Pays while alive — many small payments, each conditional on survival. Uses \(D\) and \(N\).
Assurance
Pays once, on death (or on survival, for a pure endowment). Uses \(D\), \(C\) and \(M\).
Expected present value (EPV)
\(\sum(\text{probability of the event})\times(\text{present value if it happens})\). A weighted average, not a certain amount.
The one-line idea \[\text{EPV}=\sum_{\text{all possible death years}}v^{k}\times P(\text{death in year }k)\times(\text{sum assured})\]

3 · The idea in plain words

A life assurance policy is a bet the insurer hopes to lose — it hopes Daniel lives a long life and never claims. Pricing it means answering: what is a promise worth, when the promise is paid only if a particular person dies in a particular window of time?

Three separate things have to be combined for every possible year of death:

  1. The probability that death happens in that year — from the mortality table.
  2. The discount factor \(v^{k}\) — the payment is in the future, so it is worth less today.
  3. The amount — the sum assured, which is fixed.

Multiply the three and add up over all possible years of death. That sum is the EPV. This is precisely the formula for the expectation of a random variable, which is why probability, statistics, interest theory and mortality tables all meet in one expression.

Why the present value is random

Let \(K_{x}\) be the year of death of a life aged \(x\): \(K_{x}=0\) if death occurs in the first year, \(K_{x}=1\) in the second, and so on. For a whole-life assurance paying 1 at the end of the year of death, the present value is

\[Z=v^{\,K_{x}+1}.\]

\(K_{x}\) is a random variable, so \(Z\) is a random variable. Its possible values are \(v,v^{2},v^{3},\dots\) and the probability attached to \(v^{k}\) is \(P(K_{x}=k-1)\), i.e. death during year \(k\). Hence

\[E(Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}\,P(K_{x}=k-1)=v\,q_{x}+v^{2}\,{}_{1}p_{x}q_{x+1}+v^{3}\,{}_{2}p_{x}q_{x+2}+\cdots\]

That sum is \(A_{x}\). The actuarial present value is not an approximation of the expectation — it is the expectation.

A two-outcome illustration Suppose a 1-year policy on a life with \(q_{x}=0.02\), sum assured \$10,000, \(i=5\%\).
Death in the year (prob 0.02): PV \(=10{,}000\times0.952381=\$9{,}523.81\).
Survival (prob 0.98): PV \(=0\).
\(E(Z)=0.02\times9{,}523.81+0.98\times0=\$190.48\).
Nobody receives \$190.48; either the insurer pays \$9,523.81 or it pays nothing. \$190.48 is the fair price per policy before expenses.

The crucial distinction: \(M\) for death, \(D\) for survival

The one identity worth memorising \[A_{x:\overline{n}|}=\underbrace{A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}}_{\text{pays on death in term}}+\underbrace{{}_{n}E_{x}}_{\text{pays on survival}}\] An endowment assurance pays for certain — you either die in the term or you survive it. The two branches are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so the EPVs simply add.

5 · Whole-life assurance

The sum assured is paid at the end of the year of death, whenever that is. For a benefit of 1:

\[\boxed{A_{x}=v\,q_{x}+v^{2}\,{}_{1}p_{x}q_{x+1}+v^{3}\,{}_{2}p_{x}q_{x+2}+\cdots=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}\,{}_{k-1}p_{x}\,q_{x+k-1}}\]

Reading the first three terms off the mortality table:

Death during yearProbabilityPresent value of \$1Contribution
1\(q_{x}\)\(v\)\(v\,q_{x}\)
2\({}_{1}p_{x}q_{x+1}\)\(v^{2}\)\(v^{2}{}_{1}p_{x}q_{x+1}\)
3\({}_{2}p_{x}q_{x+2}\)\(v^{3}\)\(v^{3}{}_{2}p_{x}q_{x+2}\)

and so on to the limiting age. This is a probability-weighted average of the discounted payment, exactly as in the scenario.

Converting to commutation functions

Replace each probability by life-table deaths, since \({}_{k-1}p_{x}q_{x+k-1}=d_{x+k-1}/\ell_{x}\):

\[A_{x}=\frac{1}{\ell_{x}}\left(v\,d_{x}+v^{2}d_{x+1}+v^{3}d_{x+2}+\cdots\right)\]

Now multiply top and bottom by \(v^{x}\) — the same trick as in the annuity chapter. Writing \(C_{x}=v^{x+1}d_{x}\),

\[A_{x}=\frac{1}{v^{x}\ell_{x}}\left(C_{x}+C_{x+1}+C_{x+2}+\cdots\right)=\frac{M_{x}}{D_{x}},\qquad M_{x}=\sum_{k\ge x}C_{k}.\]
Result \[\boxed{A_{x}=\frac{M_{x}}{D_{x}}}\] The numerator counts discounted deaths, the denominator discounted lives. Their ratio is the probability-weighted discounted benefit.

6 · Term (temporary) assurance

Identical to whole-life, except the payments stop after \(n\) years: if the person survives the term, nothing is paid.

\[A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=v\,q_{x}+v^{2}{}_{1}p_{x}q_{x+1}+\cdots+v^{n}{}_{n-1}p_{x}q_{x+n-1}\]

In life-table form the sum is \(\dfrac{1}{\ell_{x}}\left(v\,d_{x}+v^{2}d_{x+1}+\cdots+v^{n}d_{x+n-1}\right)\), and after multiplying by \(v^{x}\) the bracket is exactly \(C_{x}+C_{x+1}+\cdots+C_{x+n-1}\). Since \(M_{x}\) contains all \(C\)'s from \(x\) onward and \(M_{x+n}\) contains the ones we do not want,

\[\boxed{A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}}{D_{x}}}\]

Superscript 1 marks a death benefit; the bar over \(n\) just marks the term. The subtraction is the same "keep the first \(n\) terms" device you met with \(N_{x+1}-N_{x+n+1}\) in the annuity chapter.

7 · Pure endowment and endowment assurance

7.1 Pure endowment

Pays the sum assured only if the person survives to the end of \(n\) years. There is no death benefit at all.

The payment of 1 at time \(n\) occurs with probability \({}_{n}p_{x}=\ell_{x+n}/\ell_{x}\), so

\[{}_{n}E_{x}=v^{n}\,{}_{n}p_{x}=v^{n}\frac{\ell_{x+n}}{\ell_{x}}.\]

Multiply top and bottom by \(v^{x}\):

\[{}_{n}E_{x}=\frac{v^{x+n}\ell_{x+n}}{v^{x}\ell_{x}}=\boxed{\frac{D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}}.\]
Sanity check Only survival is involved, so only \(D\)'s appear. At \(n=0\), \({}_{0}E_{x}=D_{x}/D_{x}=1\): paying 1 today with certainty is worth exactly 1. ✓

7.2 Endowment assurance

Pays on death if death occurs within \(n\) years, or at the end of \(n\) years if the person is still alive. The two events are disjoint and together cover every possibility, so the EPVs add:

\[A_{x:\overline{n}|}=A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}+{}_{n}E_{x} =\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}}{D_{x}}+\frac{D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}\] \[\boxed{A_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}}\]

A pure endowment of 1 is paid with certainty at the latest at time \(n\) — which is why an \(n\)-year endowment assurance is worth more than the \(n\)-year term assurance on the same life. It is the only one of the five that is guaranteed to pay something.

8 · Deferred whole-life assurance

Cover starts only after \(k\) years: no benefit is paid if death occurs during the first \(k\) years. The first possible death benefit falls at the end of year \(k+1\).

Whole-life assurance is \(M_{x}/D_{x}\); the first \(k\) years contribute \(C_{x}+\cdots+C_{x+k-1}\), so what remains is \(C_{x+k}+C_{x+k+1}+\cdots=M_{x+k}\):

\[\boxed{{}_{k|}A_{x}=\frac{M_{x+k}}{D_{x}}}\]
Why this must be true Every death is either "within the first \(k\) years" or "after them". So \[A_{x}=\underbrace{A^{1}_{x:\overline{k}|}}_{\text{dies in first }k\text{ yrs}}+\underbrace{{}_{k|}A_{x}}_{\text{dies after}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{M_{x}}{D_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+k}}{D_{x}}+\frac{M_{x+k}}{D_{x}}.\] The identity is just \(M_{x}=(M_{x}-M_{x+k})+M_{x+k}\) — nothing more. Watch for this as a free check on any exam answer.

9 · Worked examples

Example 1 · Whole-life assurance, age 50

A person aged 50 buys whole-life assurance with sum assured \$100,000. Given \(D_{50}=5{,}000\) and \(M_{50}=750\):

EPV factor. \[A_{50}=\frac{M_{50}}{D_{50}}=\frac{750}{5{,}000}=0.15.\]
Scale to the sum assured. \[\text{EPV}=100{,}000\times0.15=\$15{,}000.\]
Interpretation: \$15,000 today buys a certain \$100,000 whenever the person dies — because \$100,000 is paid once, in the future, and only with probability 1 in total but at a deeply uncertain time. The factor 0.15 is well below 1 because of discounting: the average payment date is decades away.

Example 2 · 10-year term assurance, age 40

Ten-year term assurance, sum assured \$200,000. Given \(D_{40}=8{,}000\), \(M_{40}=1{,}200\), \(M_{50}=720\):

Factor. \[A^{1}_{40:\overline{10}|}=\frac{M_{40}-M_{50}}{D_{40}}=\frac{1{,}200-720}{8{,}000}=\frac{480}{8{,}000}=0.06.\]
Scale. \[\text{EPV}=200{,}000\times0.06=\$12{,}000.\]
Only 6 cents per dollar of cover: the insurer pays only if death falls inside the ten-year window.

Example 3 · 10-year pure endowment, age 40

Sum assured \$100,000, paid at age 50 if the person is alive. Given \(D_{40}=8{,}000\), \(D_{50}=5{,}600\):

\[{}_{10}E_{40}=\frac{D_{50}}{D_{40}}=\frac{5{,}600}{8{,}000}=0.70,\qquad \text{EPV}=100{,}000\times0.70=\$70{,}000.\]
Here the factor is large (0.70) because survival to 50 is very likely — \(0.70\) is precisely \(v^{10}\times{}_{10}p_{40}\), i.e. the discount factor times the survival probability.

Example 4 · The same life, all three products

Using the same age-40 basis (\(D_{40}=8{,}000\), \(M_{40}=1{,}200\), \(M_{50}=720\), \(D_{50}=5{,}600\)), price all three \$100,000 products:

ProductFactorCalculationEPV
10-year term0.06\((1{,}200-720)/8{,}000\)\$6,000
10-year pure endowment0.70\(5{,}600/8{,}000\)\$70,000
10-year endowment assurance0.76\((1{,}200-720+5{,}600)/8{,}000\)\$76,000
Consistency check. Endowment \(=\) term \(+\) pure endowment: \[0.06+0.70=0.76\quad\checkmark\]
The pure endowment is the expensive part — it is essentially a savings contract. Term assurance is cheap because the event it insures against is unlikely.

Example 5 · 10-year deferred whole-life, age 40

Whole-life cover of \$200,000 that pays nothing if death occurs in the first 10 years. With \(D_{40}=8{,}000\), \(M_{50}=720\):

\[{}_{10|}A_{40}=\frac{M_{50}}{D_{40}}=\frac{720}{8{,}000}=0.09,\qquad \text{EPV}=200{,}000\times0.09=\$18{,}000.\]
Check against Example 2. Term (first 10 years) \(=0.06\); deferred (after 10 years) \(=0.09\); sum \(=0.15\), and indeed \(A_{40}=M_{40}/D_{40}=1{,}200/8{,}000=0.15\). ✓
Deferral shifts value from the early years to the later ones; the two pieces must always reconstruct the whole-life factor.

11 · Derivations

D1 · Why the EPV is a probability-weighted sum

Let \(K_{x}\) be the number of complete years lived before death (so \(K_{x}=0\) means death in the first year). The payment of 1 at the end of the year of death has present value \(Z=v^{K_{x}+1}\). By the definition of expectation for a discrete random variable,

\[E(Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}\,P(K_{x}=k-1).\]

Now \(P(K_{x}=k-1)=P(\text{survive }k-1\text{ years})\times P(\text{die in the next year}\mid\text{alive at }x+k-1)={}_{k-1}p_{x}\,q_{x+k-1}\). Hence

\[A_{x}=E(Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}\,{}_{k-1}p_{x}\,q_{x+k-1}.\]

Each term is (probability) × (present value), and the whole is the expected present value.

D2 · Probability of death in year \(k\), from the life table

Survive \(k-1\) years, then die within the next one:

\[{}_{k-1}p_{x}\,q_{x+k-1}=\frac{\ell_{x+k-1}}{\ell_{x}}\cdot\frac{d_{x+k-1}}{\ell_{x+k-1}}=\frac{d_{x+k-1}}{\ell_{x}}.\]

The intermediate \(\ell\) cancels. Summing over all \(k\) gives \(\sum_{k\ge1}d_{x+k-1}/\ell_{x}=\ell_{x}/\ell_{x}=1\): death happens in exactly one year, with total probability 1.

D3 · Whole-life: \(A_{x}=M_{x}/D_{x}\)

Start from the life-table form:

\[A_{x}=\frac{1}{\ell_{x}}\left(v\,d_{x}+v^{2}d_{x+1}+v^{3}d_{x+2}+\cdots\right).\]

Multiply numerator and denominator by \(v^{x}\):

\[A_{x}=\frac{1}{v^{x}\ell_{x}}\left(v^{x+1}d_{x}+v^{x+2}d_{x+1}+v^{x+3}d_{x+2}+\cdots\right).\]

The bracket is \(C_{x}+C_{x+1}+C_{x+2}+\cdots=M_{x}\), and the prefactor is \(1/D_{x}\). Therefore \(A_{x}=M_{x}/D_{x}\).

Why \(v^{x+1}\) and not \(v^{x}\)? Because \(C_{x}=v^{x+1}d_{x}\) is the present value at age \(x\) of the deaths during year \(x\to x+1\), which are paid at the end of that year.

D4 · Term assurance: \(A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=(M_{x}-M_{x+n})/D_{x}\)

Only the first \(n\) death-years contribute:

\[A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{1}{\ell_{x}}\left(v\,d_{x}+v^{2}d_{x+1}+\cdots+v^{n}d_{x+n-1}\right) =\frac{v^{x+1}d_{x}+\cdots+v^{x+n}d_{x+n-1}}{v^{x}\ell_{x}}.\]

The numerator is \(C_{x}+\cdots+C_{x+n-1}\). Split \(M_{x}\):

\[M_{x}=\underbrace{C_{x}+\cdots+C_{x+n-1}}_{\text{wanted}}+\underbrace{C_{x+n}+C_{x+n+1}+\cdots}_{=\,M_{x+n}}.\]

Therefore the wanted sum is \(M_{x}-M_{x+n}\), and \(A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=(M_{x}-M_{x+n})/D_{x}\).

D5 · Pure endowment: \({}_{n}E_{x}=D_{x+n}/D_{x}\)

The payment of 1 at time \(n\) happens with probability \({}_{n}p_{x}=\ell_{x+n}/\ell_{x}\):

\[{}_{n}E_{x}=v^{n}\frac{\ell_{x+n}}{\ell_{x}}=\frac{v^{x+n}\ell_{x+n}}{v^{x}\ell_{x}}=\frac{D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}.\]

Notice there is no \(q\) anywhere: a pure endowment is a survival benefit, so only lives \(\ell\) (and hence \(D\)) appear.

D6 · Endowment assurance: adding the two pieces

An \(n\)-year endowment assurance pays 1 if death occurs within \(n\) years, and pays 1 at time \(n\) if the person survives. These events are disjoint and exhaustive, so

\[A_{x:\overline{n}|}=A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}+{}_{n}E_{x} =\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}}{D_{x}}+\frac{D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}.\]

Common denominator \(D_{x}\); nothing else needed.

D7 · Deferred whole-life: \({}_{k|}A_{x}=M_{x+k}/D_{x}\)

Deaths in the first \(k\) years pay nothing, so the \(C\)-terms \(C_{x},\dots,C_{x+k-1}\) are dropped from \(M_{x}\). What is left is

\[C_{x+k}+C_{x+k+1}+\cdots=M_{x+k},\qquad\text{so}\qquad {}_{k|}A_{x}=\frac{M_{x+k}}{D_{x}}.\]

Equivalently, by the identity in §8, \({}_{k|}A_{x}=A_{x}-A^{1}_{x:\overline{k}|}\).

D8 · Every formula is "probability × present value"

Lay the five formulas side by side and the pattern is the same in each:

ProductEvent summed overColumn used
Whole-life \(A_{x}\)death in any year \(k\ge1\)\(M_{x}\)
Term \(A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}\)death in year \(k\le n\)\(M_{x}-M_{x+n}\)
Pure endowment \({}_{n}E_{x}\)survival to \(n\)\(D_{x+n}\)
Endowment \(A_{x:\overline{n}|}\)death in \(k\le n\) or survival to \(n\)\(M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n}\)
Deferred \({}_{k|}A_{x}\)death in year \(k\ge\) deferral \(+1\)\(M_{x+k}\)

In every row, the numerator is the discounted number of relevant events and the denominator \(D_{x}\) is the discounted number of people at risk. The quotient is the expected discounted payout per policy.
