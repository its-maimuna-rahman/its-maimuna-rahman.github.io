Law of Large Numbers

Why averages settle down as the sample grows. One page: a real case, the definition, the intuition, both versions of the law, the link to the CLT, solved problems, derivations and a live simulator.

1 · A real-life case, solved

A student draws n = 10,000 values from a process whose true mean is μ = 5 (and standard deviation σ = 2). The sample average comes out as 4.998. The student says: "The Law of Large Numbers is violated, because the average isn't exactly 5."

Question: what is wrong with this statement, and how big a gap should we normally expect?

Step 1 · What the law really says. The LLN never promises that \(\bar X_n=\mu\) exactly for any fixed \(n\). It only says that \(\bar X_n\to\mu\) in the limit (in probability, or almost surely). So a small gap is not a contradiction.
Step 2 · Typical size of the gap. The Central Limit Theorem tells us how large the random wobble usually is. The standard deviation of \(\bar X_n\) is \[\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{10{,}000}}=\frac{2}{100}=0.02\]
Step 3 · Compare. The observed gap is \(|4.998-5|=0.002\), which is \[\frac{0.002}{0.02}=0.1\ \text{standard deviations.}\]
A gap of 0.002 is only 0.1 standard deviations from μ: well within ordinary sampling fluctuation. It is not a contradiction of the LLN.

2 · Definition

The key phrases from the case were i.i.d. random variables, finite mean, sample mean and as the sample size grows. Put together:

Law of Large Numbers (LLN) A fundamental pillar of probability theory and mathematical statistics. It is the formal bridge between theoretical expectations and empirical observations: it describes what happens to the sample mean of i.i.d. random variables as the sample size grows.
Setup
Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) be i.i.d. random variables with finite mean \(E[X_i]=\mu\).
Sample mean
\(\displaystyle\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\)
Two versions
The Weak LLN (convergence in probability) and the Strong LLN (almost sure convergence).

3 · The idea in plain words

Think of it as "the average calms down as you collect more data." One observation can be far from the true mean. The average of ten is usually closer. The average of ten thousand is closer still.

  1. Small samples are noisy: a single value, or a handful, can land far from μ.
  2. Large samples are steady: unusual values get diluted by the rest, so the average hugs μ.

What the law does not say: it does not guarantee \(\bar X_n\) equals μ for a fixed \(n\). It only says that large deviations become increasingly unlikely.

Big picture The LLN answers "where does the average end up?" (at μ). It says nothing about the shape of the wobble along the way; that is the job of the Central Limit Theorem (§6).

4 · Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

Statement The sample mean converges in probability to the true mean μ, written \(\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu\). Formally, for every \(\epsilon>0\): \[\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\epsilon\big)=0\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}P\big(|\bar X_n-\mu|<\epsilon\big)=1\]

In words: as \(n\) grows, the probability that the sample mean strays more than \(\epsilon\) away from μ shrinks toward zero. It does not guarantee that \(\bar X_n=\mu\) for a fixed \(n\); only that large deviations become increasingly unlikely.

Proof (when the variance is finite)

Step 1 · Variance of the mean. If \(\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty\), then \[\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]
Step 2 · Chebyshev's inequality. \[P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\]
Step 3 · Let \(n\to\infty\). \[\lim_{n\to\infty}P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}=0\] Therefore \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\epsilon)=0\). ∎
Historical note In 1929, Aleksandr Khinchin removed the finite-variance requirement, proving that a finite \(E[X]\) is necessary and sufficient for the WLLN.

5 · Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)

Statement The SLLN strengthens the mode of convergence to almost sure convergence, written \(\bar X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu\). Formally: \[P\Big(\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar X_n=\mu\Big)=1\]

In words: for almost every possible infinite sequence of outcomes, the running average converges to μ as a real (ordinary) limit.

Historical development

  • Émile Borel (1909) proved the first SLLN, for independent Bernoulli trials, using measure-theoretic arguments on the unit interval.
  • A. N. Kolmogorov (1930/33) established the definitive SLLN for i.i.d. sequences, proving that \(E[|X_1|]<\infty\) is both necessary and sufficient for almost sure convergence.
  • Nasrollah Etemadi (1981) relaxed the condition to pairwise independence.

6 · LLN vs CLT

The two results are partners: the LLN finds the destination; the CLT describes the wobble around it.

AspectLLNCLT
Core question answeredWhere does \(\bar X_n\) converge as \(n\to\infty\)?What shape and size do the random fluctuations around that limit take?
Target quantitySample mean \(\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\)The standardized fluctuation \(Z_n=\dfrac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\)
End result\(\bar X_n\to\mu\): convergence to a single deterministic point\(Z_n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)\): convergence to a bell-curve distribution
Mode of convergenceIn probability / almost sureIn distribution

7 · Exact distribution of the sample mean

The exact distribution of \(\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i\) depends fundamentally on the parent distribution the sample \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) is drawn from. But regardless of the parent distribution, if the population has finite mean μ and finite variance \(\sigma^2\):

Always true \[E[\bar X]=\mu\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]

Exact sampling distributions for specific populations

1 · Normal population \((X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2))\)

A linear combination of independent normal variables is itself exactly normal: if \(X_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)\) independently, then \(\sum a_iX_i\sim N\big(\sum a_i\mu_i,\ \sum a_i^2\sigma_i^2\big)\). The sample mean is such a combination, with \(a_i=\frac1n\): \[\bar X=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\frac1n\Big)X_i\] This implies the sample mean is exactly normally distributed for every \(n\ge1\): \[\bar X\sim N\Big(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big)\] When σ is known: the standardized sample mean follows a standard normal distribution, \[Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)\] When σ is unknown: replacing σ with the sample standard deviation \(S\) gives Student's t-distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom, \[T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}\]

2 · Cauchy population \((X_i\sim\text{Cauchy}(\mu,a))\)

The Cauchy distribution lacks a finite mean or variance, so averaging \(n\) observations gives zero variance reduction. Hence \(\bar X\) is exactly Cauchy distributed with the same parameters: \[\bar X\sim\text{Cauchy}(\mu,a)\]

Why this mattersThe LLN needs a finite mean. The Cauchy has none, so its sample mean never settles down. Try it in the simulator (§11).
3 · Gamma population
\(X_i\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)\)
\(\bar X\sim\text{Gamma}(n\alpha,\,n\lambda)\)
4 · Exponential population
\(X_i\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)\)
\(\bar X\sim\text{Gamma}(n,\,n\lambda)\)
5 · Bernoulli population
\(X_i\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)\)
\(P\big(\bar X=\tfrac kn\big)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\ k=0,1,\dots,n\)
6 · Poisson population
\(X_i\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\)
\(P\big(\bar X=\tfrac kn\big)=\dfrac{e^{-n\lambda}(n\lambda)^k}{k!},\ k=0,1,2,\dots\)

Notes: in the Gamma cases \(\alpha\) is the shape and \(\lambda\) the rate. In cases 5 and 6, \(\bar X=k/n\) means the sum equals \(k\): a Binomial\((n,p)\) and a Poisson\((n\lambda)\) respectively.

8 · Solved problems

Problem 1 · How many dice rolls?

A fair die is rolled repeatedly. Let \(X_i\) be the outcome of roll \(i\); we know \(\mu=E[X_i]=3.5\) and \(\sigma^2=\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=2.917\). How many rolls guarantee \[P(|\bar X_n-3.5|>0.1)\le0.05\ ?\]

Step 1 · Chebyshev bound. \(P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\epsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\).
Step 2 · Force the bound below 0.05. \[\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\le0.05\ \Rightarrow\ n\ge\frac{\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2(0.05)}=\frac{2.917}{(0.1)^2(0.05)}=5834\]
nmin = 5,834 rolls.

Problem 2 · Component lifetimes

Component lifetimes (hours) are i.i.d. with \(\mu=500\) and \(\sigma=40\). For a sample of \(n=64\) components, approximate \(P(\bar X_{64}>510)\).

Standardize (CLT): \[P(\bar X_{64}>510)=P\Big(\frac{\bar X_{64}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}>\frac{510-500}{40/\sqrt{64}}\Big)=P(Z>2)\]
Look up \(=1-P(Z<2)=1-0.9772=0.0228\)
P ≈ 0.0228 (about 2.3%).

Problem 3 · Insurance claims

An insurer expects to process \(n=900\) independent claims next year. Each claim has mean \(\mu=\$2000\) and \(\sigma=\$500\). Approximate the probability that the total claim \(S_{900}\) exceeds \$1,830,000.

Step 1 · Turn the total into an average. \[S_{900}>1{,}830{,}000\iff\bar X_n>\frac{1{,}830{,}000}{900}=2033.33\]
Step 2 · Standardize. \[P(\bar X_n>2033.33)=P\Big(\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}>\frac{2033.33-2000}{500/\sqrt{900}}\Big)=P(Z>2)\]
P(Z > 2) = 0.0228

The handwritten notes show "0.228" at the end; the correct value for \(P(Z>2)\) is 0.0228, the same as Problem 2.

Problem 4 · "The LLN is violated!"

Solved in full in §1: with \(n=10{,}000\), \(\bar X_n=4.998\), \(\mu=5\), \(\sigma=2\), the standard deviation of \(\bar X_n\) is 0.02 and the gap of 0.002 is just 0.1 standard deviations. Not a contradiction.

9 · Derivations

D1 · WLLN via Chebyshev

Assume \(\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty\). Then \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n\). Chebyshev's inequality gives, for any \(\epsilon>0\), \[P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}.\] The right-hand side tends to 0 as \(n\to\infty\), and probabilities are never negative, so \(\lim P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)=0\). This is exactly \(\bar X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu\).

D2 · Sample size needed (used in Problem 1)

To guarantee \(P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\epsilon)\le\delta\), it is enough that the Chebyshev bound is at most δ: \[\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\le\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{n\ge\frac{\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2\,\delta}}\] With \(\sigma^2=2.917,\ \epsilon=0.1,\ \delta=0.05\) this gives \(n\ge5834\).

D3 · Exact normality of \(\bar X\) for a normal population

If \(X_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)\) are independent, then \(\sum a_iX_i\sim N\big(\sum a_i\mu_i,\sum a_i^2\sigma_i^2\big)\). With \(a_i=1/n\), \(\mu_i=\mu\), \(\sigma_i^2=\sigma^2\): \[\text{mean}=\sum\frac1n\mu=\mu,\qquad\text{variance}=\sum\frac1{n^2}\sigma^2=\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.\] So \(\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\) for every \(n\ge1\), and \(Z=(\bar X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\sim N(0,1)\).

D4 · Probabilities for totals via the CLT (Problems 2 and 3)

A total \(S_n=\sum X_i\) exceeds a value \(s\) exactly when \(\bar X_n>s/n\). Standardizing \(\bar X_n\) with mean μ and standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) and using \(Z_n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)\): \[P(\bar X_n>x)\approx P\Big(Z>\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\Big)=1-\Phi\Big(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\Big).\]
