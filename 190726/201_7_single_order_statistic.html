Single Order Statistic

Sort a random sample and look at the value sitting in position r. This page finds the chance distribution of that one value: first the smallest and the largest, then any position, with the Beta-function form and two ways to get its density.

1 · Start with a small example

Five numbers are drawn independently, each equally likely to land anywhere between 0 and 1. Sort them from smallest to largest. The middle one (the 3rd smallest) is called \(X_{(3)}\).

Question: how likely is the middle number to be at most 0.4? What is its density? And how do the largest and smallest numbers compare?

Step 1 · One number. For a number drawn between 0 and 1, the chance of being at most \(x\) is \(F(x)=x\). So each number is at most 0.4 with probability \(p=0.4\).
Step 2 · When is the middle number at most 0.4? Exactly when at least 3 of the 5 numbers are at most 0.4. The count of such numbers follows a binomial distribution with \(n=5,\ p=0.4\): \[P(X_{(3)}\le0.4)=\sum_{j=3}^{5}\binom5j(0.4)^j(0.6)^{5-j}\]

Step 3 · Same answer from the density. The density of the 3rd smallest of 5 is (§6) \[f_{(3)}(x)=\frac{5!}{2!\,2!}\,x^2(1-x)^2=30\,x^2(1-x)^2\] and \[\int_0^{0.4}30\,x^2(1-x)^2\,dx=30\Big(\tfrac{0.4^3}{3}-\tfrac{0.4^4}{2}+\tfrac{0.4^5}{5}\Big)=0.31744\ \checkmark\]
Step 4 · Largest and smallest. The largest is at most 0.4 only if all five are: \(0.4^5=0.01024\). The smallest is at most 0.4 unless all five are above it: \(1-0.6^5=0.92224\).
P(middle ≤ 0.4) = 0.31744 · P(largest ≤ 0.4) = 0.01024 · P(smallest ≤ 0.4) = 0.92224. The middle number is above 0.4 about 68% of the time, the largest almost always (99%), and the smallest is below 0.4 about 92% of the time.

2 · Definition

Let \(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\) be a random sample of size \(n\) from a continuous population with density \(f(x)\) and cumulative distribution function \(F(x)\). That is, the \(X_i\) are independent and all come from the same distribution (i.i.d.). Here \(F(x)\) is the chance that one observation is at most \(x\).

Order statistics The sample values arranged in non-decreasing order. Sorting the sample gives \[X_{(1)}\le X_{(2)}\le\dots\le X_{(n)}\] \(X_{(r)}\), also written \(X_{r:n}\), is the r-th order statistic, for \(r=1,2,\dots,n\).
Sample minimum
\(X_{(1)}=\min(X_1,\dots,X_n)\)
Sample maximum
\(X_{(n)}=\max(X_1,\dots,X_n)\)
Any position
\(X_{(r)}\) is the \(r\)-th smallest value.
  • The general definition needs neither independence, identical distributions, nor continuity. Classical theory works with i.i.d. samples from one common distribution function.
  • When \(F\) is continuous, \(P(X_i=X_j)=0\) for \(i\ne j\), so ties happen with probability zero and the strict inequalities \[X_{(1)}\lt X_{(2)}\lt\dots\lt X_{(n)}\] hold with probability 1.
  • Even when the original \(X_i\) are independent, the order statistics are necessarily dependent, because each one is squeezed between its neighbours.
  • \(X_{(r)}\) is not one fixed original variable: which \(X_i\) lands in position \(r\) changes from sample to sample. Added

3 · The largest and the smallest

(a) Largest, \(X_{(n)}\)

If the largest value is at most \(x\), then every value is at most \(x\), and the other way round.

\[F_n(x)=P(X_{(n)}\le x)=P(X_i\le x\ \text{for all } i)=P(X_1\le x\cap\dots\cap X_n\le x)\]

The \(X_i\) are independent and identically distributed, so the probabilities multiply:

\[=P(X_1\le x)\,P(X_2\le x)\cdots P(X_n\le x)=\big[F(x)\big]^n\]

(b) Smallest, \(X_{(1)}\)

It is easier to ask the opposite question: the smallest value is above \(x\) only if every value is above \(x\).

\[F_1(x)=P(X_{(1)}\le x)=1-P(X_{(1)}\gt x)=1-P(X_1\gt x)\cdots P(X_n\gt x)\] \[=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(X_i\gt x)=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big[1-P(X_i\le x)\big]=1-\big[1-F(x)\big]^n\]
Result \[F_{(n)}(x)=\big[F(x)\big]^n\qquad\qquad F_{(1)}(x)=1-\big[1-F(x)\big]^n\]

4 · The r-th smallest: its distribution function

The \(r\)-th smallest value is at most \(x\) exactly when at least \(r\) of the \(n\) observations are at most \(x\). Call "\(X_i\le x\)" a success; each observation is a success with probability \(p=F(x)\), independently. The number of successes is therefore binomial, and \[F_r(x)=P(X_{(r)}\le x)=P(\text{at least } r\ \text{of the } X_i\ \text{are}\le x)=\sum_{j=r}^{n}P(\text{exactly } j\ \text{of the } n\ X_i\ \text{are}\le x)\] \[\boxed{F_r(x)=\sum_{j=r}^{n}\binom nj\big[F(x)\big]^j\big[1-F(x)\big]^{n-j}}\]

Check Added: with \(r=n\) only the \(j=n\) term remains, giving \([F(x)]^n\). With \(r=1\) the sum is everything except \(j=0\), giving \(1-[1-F(x)]^n\). Both match §3.

5 · The same thing as a Beta-function integral

The binomial sum is awkward to differentiate. It can be rewritten as an integral, which makes the density easy to find.

Incomplete Beta function For \(0\le p\le1\) and parameters \(a,b\gt0\): \[I_p(a,b)=\frac{1}{\beta(a,b)}\int_0^{p}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt,\qquad \beta(a,b)=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}=\frac{(a-1)!\,(b-1)!}{(a+b-1)!}\] Note that the upper limit \(p\) is what makes it "incomplete": at \(p=1\) the integral is the full Beta function and \(I_1(a,b)=1\).
Result \[F_r(x)=I_{F(x)}(r,\ n-r+1)\]

Proof

Put \(p=F(x)\). We need to show that for every \(p\) in \([0,1]\), \[\sum_{j=r}^{n}\binom nj p^j(1-p)^{n-j}=\frac{1}{\beta(r,n-r+1)}\int_0^p t^{r-1}(1-t)^{n-r}\,dt\] The plan: show that both sides have the same derivative in \(p\) and the same starting value.

Step 1 · Differentiate the left side. Using the product rule on each term (the \(j=n\) part of the second sum is zero): \[\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\sum_{j=r}^{n}\binom nj p^j(1-p)^{n-j}=\sum_{j=r}^{n}j\binom nj p^{j-1}(1-p)^{n-j}-\sum_{j=r}^{n-1}(n-j)\binom nj p^{j}(1-p)^{n-j-1}\]
Step 2 · Two identities. \[j\binom nj=\frac{n!}{j!\,(n-j)!}\,j=n\,\frac{(n-1)!}{(j-1)!\,[(n-1)-(j-1)]!}=n\binom{n-1}{j-1}\] \[(n-j)\binom nj=\frac{n!}{(n-j)!\,j!}(n-j)=n\,\frac{(n-1)!}{j!\,[(n-1)-j]!}=n\binom{n-1}{j}\quad(0\le j\le n-1)\] So the derivative becomes \[n\sum_{j=r}^{n}\binom{n-1}{j-1}p^{j-1}(1-p)^{n-j}-n\sum_{j=r}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}p^{j}(1-p)^{n-1-j}\]
Step 3 · Shift the second sum. Let \(k=j+1\), so \(k\) runs from \(r+1\) to \(n\). The index name does not matter, so the second sum equals \(n\sum_{j=r+1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{j-1}p^{j-1}(1-p)^{n-j}\), which is the first sum without its \(j=r\) term. Everything cancels except that term: \[\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\sum_{j=r}^{n}\binom nj p^j(1-p)^{n-j}=n\binom{n-1}{r-1}p^{r-1}(1-p)^{n-r}=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\,p^{r-1}(1-p)^{n-r}\]
Step 4 · Differentiate the right side. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, and since \(\beta(r,n-r+1)=\dfrac{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}{n!}\): \[\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\Big[\frac{1}{\beta(r,n-r+1)}\int_0^p t^{r-1}(1-t)^{n-r}dt\Big]=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\,p^{r-1}(1-p)^{n-r}\]
Step 5 · Conclude. Both sides have identical derivatives for all \(p\in[0,1]\). Both are also 0 at \(p=0\) (every term on the left contains \(p^j\) with \(j\ge r\ge1\); the integral on the right starts at 0), so they are equal everywhere. Substituting \(p=F(x)\) gives \[F_r(x)=I_{F(x)}(r,\ n-r+1)\ \ ∎\]

6 · Density of the r-th smallest

Result \[f_r(x)=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\,\big[F(x)\big]^{r-1}\big[1-F(x)\big]^{n-r}f(x),\qquad -\infty\lt x\lt\infty\]

Two ways to get it.

Method 1 · Differentiate the distribution function

The density is the derivative of the distribution function, and the Beta form from §5 is easy to differentiate. Let \[c=\frac{1}{\beta(r,n-r+1)},\qquad g(t)=t^{r-1}(1-t)^{n-r},\qquad G(u)=\int_0^u g(t)\,dt\]

Step 1. \(\displaystyle f_r(x)=\frac{d}{dx}F_r(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\Big[c\int_0^{F(x)}g(t)\,dt\Big]=\frac{d}{dx}\big[c\,G(F(x))\big]\)
Step 2 · Chain rule. \(G'=g\), so \(\displaystyle f_r(x)=c\,G'(F(x))\,\frac{d}{dx}F(x)=c\,g(F(x))\,f(x)\).

This step can be done directly with the Leibniz integral rule.

Step 3 · Substitute back. \[f_r(x)=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\,[F(x)]^{r-1}\,[1-F(x)]^{n-r}\,f(x)\]

Method 2 · A tiny interval and counting

The idea behind a density. The density at \(x\) is the chance of landing in a very small interval just after \(x\), divided by the interval's length: \[f(x)=\lim_{\delta x\to0}\frac{P(x\lt X\le x+\delta x)}{\delta x}\] Why: \(P(x\lt X\le x+\delta x)=\int_x^{x+\delta x}f(u)\,du\). If \(\delta x\) is tiny, the area under the curve is almost a thin rectangle of height \(f(x)\) and width \(\delta x\), so \(P\approx f(x)\,\delta x\), and the approximation becomes exact as \(\delta x\to0\).

Apply this to \(X_{(r)}\): \[f_r(x)=\lim_{\delta x\to0}\frac{P(x\lt X_{(r)}\le x+\delta x)}{\delta x}\] For the \(r\)-th smallest observation to fall inside the tiny interval \((x,x+\delta x]\), the number line splits into three non-overlapping pieces that together cover everything:

IntervalCondition on \(X_i\)Observations neededChance for one observation
\((-\infty,x]\)\(X_i\le x\)\(r-1\)\(P_1=F(x)\)
\((x,x+\delta x]\)\(x\lt X_i\le x+\delta x\)\(1\) (only one point fits in the tiny interval)\(P_2=F(x+\delta x)-F(x)\)
\((x+\delta x,\infty)\)\(X_i\gt x+\delta x\)\(n-r\)\(P_3=1-F(x+\delta x)\)

The \(n\) observations are independent and identically distributed, so how they are shared among these three intervals follows the multinomial law \(\dfrac{n!}{x_1!\,x_2!\,x_3!}P_1^{x_1}P_2^{x_2}P_3^{x_3}\). With counts \(r-1,\ 1,\ n-r\): \[P(x\lt X_{(r)}\le x+\delta x)=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,1!\,(n-r)!}\,[F(x)]^{r-1}\,[F(x+\delta x)-F(x)]\,[1-F(x+\delta x)]^{n-r}+O\big((\delta x)^2\big)\] The \(O((\delta x)^2)\) term covers two or more observations falling inside the tiny interval; it shrinks faster than \(\delta x\) and vanishes in the limit.

Step 1 · Divide by \(\delta x\) and let \(\delta x\to0\). \[f_r(x)=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\,[F(x)]^{r-1}\Big[\lim_{\delta x\to0}\frac{F(x+\delta x)-F(x)}{\delta x}\Big]\Big[\lim_{\delta x\to0}\big(1-F(x+\delta x)\big)^{n-r}\Big]\]
Step 2 · Evaluate the limits. The first limit is the derivative of \(F\), which is \(f(x)\). Since \(F\) is continuous, \(F(x+\delta x)\to F(x)\), so the second limit is \([1-F(x)]^{n-r}\).
Step 3 · Result. \[f_r(x)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,(r-1)!}\,[F(x)]^{r-1}\,[1-F(x)]^{n-r}\,f(x)\]

In words: \(r-1\) observations fall below \(x\), one sits at \(x\), and \(n-r\) fall above. The factor \(\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r)!}\) counts the ways to choose which observation plays which role.

7 · Special cases Added

These are not in your notes; they follow directly from the results above and make good checks.

Largest and smallest. Setting \(r=n\) and \(r=1\) in the density formula (or differentiating the results of §3): \[f_{(n)}(x)=n\,[F(x)]^{n-1}f(x)\qquad\qquad f_{(1)}(x)=n\,[1-F(x)]^{n-1}f(x)\]

Uniform on (0,1). Here \(F(x)=x\) and \(f(x)=1\), so \[f_r(x)=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\,x^{r-1}(1-x)^{n-r},\qquad 0\lt x\lt1\] This is the Beta\((r,\,n-r+1)\) density, whose mean is \(\dfrac{r}{n+1}\). For the example in §1 (\(n=5,\ r=3\)) this gives \(30x^2(1-x)^2\), with mean \(0.5\).

Exponential with rate \(\lambda\). \(F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}\), so \(1-F(x)=e^{-\lambda x}\) and \[F_{(1)}(x)=1-\big(e^{-\lambda x}\big)^n=1-e^{-n\lambda x}\] The smallest of \(n\) such values is again exponential, with the larger rate \(n\lambda\).

8 · Solved problems

Problem 1 is the example in §1. Two more follow.

Problem 2 · First failure

A system has \(n=4\) components and stops when the first one fails. Each lifetime is independent and exponential with mean 100 hours (rate \(\lambda=0.01\) per hour). Find the chance the system lasts more than 30 hours, and its average lifetime.

Step 1. The system lifetime is \(X_{(1)}\). One component lasts beyond 30 hours with probability \(1-F(30)=e^{-0.01\times30}=e^{-0.3}\).
Step 2. The smallest exceeds 30 only if all four do: \[P(X_{(1)}\gt30)=\big[1-F(30)\big]^4=e^{-1.2}=0.3012\]
Step 3. By §7, \(X_{(1)}\) is exponential with rate \(4\lambda=0.04\), so its mean is \(1/0.04=25\) hours.
P ≈ 0.3012 · average system lifetime = 25 hours (a quarter of one component's 100).

Problem 3 · Second smallest of four

Four independent numbers are drawn between 0 and 1. Find the density of the second smallest, \(X_{(2)}\), and \(P(X_{(2)}\le0.5)\).

Density. With \(n=4,\ r=2\), \(F(x)=x\), \(f(x)=1\): \[f_2(x)=\frac{4!}{1!\,2!}\,x\,(1-x)^2=12\,x(1-x)^2,\qquad 0\lt x\lt1\]
Probability, by the binomial sum (at least 2 of 4 numbers at most 0.5): \[\sum_{j=2}^{4}\binom4j(0.5)^4=\frac{6+4+1}{16}=0.6875\]
Check by integrating. \[12\int_0^{0.5}x(1-x)^2dx=12\Big(\tfrac{0.5^2}{2}-\tfrac{2\cdot0.5^3}{3}+\tfrac{0.5^4}{4}\Big)=12(0.057292)=0.6875\ \checkmark\]
f2(x) = 12x(1 − x)² · P(X(2) ≤ 0.5) = 0.6875.

9 · Extra derivations

D1 · Densities of the largest and smallest from their distribution functions

Differentiate \(F_{(n)}(x)=[F(x)]^n\) with the chain rule: \[f_{(n)}(x)=n\,[F(x)]^{n-1}f(x).\] Differentiate \(F_{(1)}(x)=1-[1-F(x)]^n\): \[f_{(1)}(x)=-n\,[1-F(x)]^{n-1}\cdot\big(-f(x)\big)=n\,[1-F(x)]^{n-1}f(x).\] These agree with the general formula at \(r=n\) and \(r=1\), because \(\frac{n!}{(n-1)!\,0!}=n\).

D2 · Why the constant is \(\dfrac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}\)

It is the number of ways to split \(n\) distinguishable observations into three groups of sizes \(r-1\) (below \(x\)), \(1\) (at \(x\)) and \(n-r\) (above \(x\)). Choose which \(r-1\) are below: \(\binom{n}{r-1}\). From the remaining \(n-r+1\), choose the one at \(x\): \(n-r+1\) ways. The rest go above: \[\binom{n}{r-1}(n-r+1)=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r+1)!}(n-r+1)=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!}.\] It also equals \(1/\beta(r,n-r+1)\), which links the two methods in §6.

D3 · The density integrates to 1

Substitute \(t=F(x)\), \(dt=f(x)\,dx\): \[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_r(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{\beta(r,n-r+1)}\int_0^1t^{r-1}(1-t)^{n-r}\,dt=\frac{\beta(r,n-r+1)}{\beta(r,n-r+1)}=1.\] This is the same as \(I_1(r,n-r+1)=1\), or \(F_r(\infty)=1\).

D4 · The Uniform case reduces to a Beta density

For \(F(x)=x,\ f(x)=1\) on \((0,1)\) the general density becomes \(\dfrac{1}{\beta(r,n-r+1)}x^{r-1}(1-x)^{n-r}\), which is exactly the Beta\((r,n-r+1)\) density. Its mean is \[\frac{\beta(r+1,n-r+1)}{\beta(r,n-r+1)}=\frac{r!\,(n-r)!/(n+1)!}{(r-1)!\,(n-r)!/n!}=\frac{r}{n+1}.\] The \(n\) sample points cut \((0,1)\) into \(n+1\) pieces that are equal on average, so the \(r\)-th point sits about \(r/(n+1)\) of the way along.
