Simple Random Sampling

Selection, estimation of mean, total and proportion, and sample-size determination. Every topic is split into its two designs: SRSWR and SRSWOR.

Sampling basics

Simple random sampling (SRS) selects \(n\) units from a population of \(N\) units so that every unit has an equal chance at each step. Two versions exist:

Without replacement (SRSWOR)

\(n\) distinct units are drawn so that each of the \(\binom{N}{n}\) possible samples is equally likely. A drawn unit is removed, so the draws are dependent.

With replacement (SRSWR)

Units are drawn one by one, each with chance \(1/N\) every time. The same unit can appear again, and the draws are independent.

Key terms

  • Population: all units under study. Sample: the representative part observed. Sampling: drawing the sample. Element: the basic unit measured; sampling unit: what is selected (can be a group); frame: the list of all sampling units.
  • Target population is what we want conclusions about; study population is the part reachable through the frame. Gaps (no phone, refusal) cause coverage and nonresponse bias.
  • Finite populations (fixed \(N\), e.g. households) vs infinite ones (quality control, probability models).
  • Descriptive inference estimates parameters (means, totals, proportions); analytical surveys compare groups or test relationships.
  • Census observes all units: no sampling error, but costly and slow. A sample survey is cheaper, faster, more flexible and can have better data quality, but has sampling error (which can be estimated).
  • Sampling error comes from observing only a sample and falls as \(n\) grows. Non-sampling error (measurement, nonresponse, coverage) is not reduced by larger \(n\).
Selection methods. Draw numbers from a hat, a random number table (read columns, ignore repeats and numbers above \(N\), until \(n\) distinct units), or software such as R or SAS.

Descriptive vs inferential

Descriptive statistics organise and summarise data. Inferential statistics draw conclusions about an unknown parameter from a random sample and measure how reliable they are.

Selection probabilities

SRSWOR

Number of samples \(\binom Nn\); each has probability

\[P(s)=1/\tbinom Nn\]

Inclusion probability (same for every unit):

\[\pi_i=n/N\]

Chance that unit \(u_j\) is picked at draw \(k\):

\[1/N\quad(\text{any }j,k)\]

SRSWR

Number of ordered samples \(N^n\); each has probability

\[P(s)=1/N^n\]

Selection chance at every draw:

\[P(u_i)=1/N\]

Same unit may repeat; draws independent; population stays at \(N\).

where \(N\) = population size, \(n\) = sample size, \(P(s)\) = probability of a particular sample \(s\), \(\pi_i\) = inclusion probability of unit \(i\), \(u_j\) = the \(j\)th unit of the population.

Both give the same marginal chance \(1/N\) per draw. They differ in dependence between draws. SRS is the only design in which every possible sample is equally likely; other designs may give equal inclusion chances without that.

Estimating mean, variance and total

Population: \(\mu=\frac1N\sum y_i\), \(S^2=\frac1{N-1}\sum(y_i-\mu)^2\), \(\sigma^2=\frac1N\sum(y_i-\mu)^2\), so \(S^2=\frac N{N-1}\sigma^2\). Sample: \(\bar y=\frac1n\sum y_i\), \(s^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2\).

SRSWOR

\[\E(\bar y)=\mu\]
\[\V(\bar y)=\frac{N-n}{N}\frac{S^2}{n}=\frac{N-n}{N-1}\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]
\[\E(s^2)=S^2\]
\[\vh(\bar y)=\frac{N-n}{N}\frac{s^2}{n}\]

SRSWR

\[\E(\bar y)=\mu\]
\[\V(\bar y)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}=\frac{N-1}{N}\frac{S^2}{n}\]
\[\E(s^2)=\sigma^2\]
\[\vh(\bar y)=\frac{s^2}{n}\]

where \(\mu,\ \bar y\) = population and sample mean; \(S^2,\ \sigma^2\) = population variance with divisor \(N-1\) and \(N\); \(s^2\) = sample variance; \(\E,\ \V\) = expectation and variance; \(\vh\) = estimated variance; \(\frac{N-n}{N}\) = finite-population correction.

The standard error is \(SE(\bar y)=\sqrt{\V(\bar y)}\), estimated by \(\sqrt{\vh(\bar y)}\). The estimated SE is not unbiased, because the square root is non-linear.

Finite-population correction (FPC)

\((N-n)/N=1-n/N\). If \(n/N\) is small (below 5%, sometimes 10%), FPC is near 1 and \(\V(\bar y)\approx S^2/n\); ignoring it slightly overestimates the variance. For small populations it matters. As \(n\to N\), FPC \(\to0\) and \(\V(\bar y)\to0\) (a census).

Try it: FPC.

SRSWOR is more efficient than SRSWR

\[\V_{WR}(\bar y)-\V_{WOR}(\bar y)=\frac{n-1}{N}\frac{S^2}{n}>0\ (n>1)\]

where \(\V_{WR},\ \V_{WOR}\) = variance of \(\bar y\) under SRSWR and SRSWOR.

The gain grows with \(n\); at \(n=N\), \(\V_{WOR}=0\).

Population total

\[\tau=N\mu,\quad \hat\tau=N\bar y\]

SRSWOR

\[\V(\hat\tau)=N(N-n)\frac{S^2}{n}\]
\[\vh(\hat\tau)=N(N-n)\frac{s^2}{n}\]

SRSWR

\[\V(\hat\tau)=N^2\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]
\[\vh(\hat\tau)=N^2\frac{s^2}{n}\]

where \(\tau=N\mu\) = population total, \(\hat\tau=N\bar y\) = its estimator, \(\V(\hat\tau)\) = variance of \(\hat\tau\), \(\vh(\hat\tau)\) = its unbiased estimator.

The slide prints \(\sigma^2\) in the WOR total variance; the correct quantity is \(S^2\), as above.

Sampling distribution of \(\bar y\)

List all samples, compute the statistic, tabulate its values. Three features matter: mean, variance, functional form. Example: ages 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 (\(N=5\), \(\mu=10\), \(\sigma^2=8\)), \(n=2\).

SRSWR: mean \(10=\mu\), variance \(\sigma^2/n=4\) (uniform-looking, bell shaped). SRSWOR: mean 10, variance \(\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\frac{N-n}{N-1}=3\). The factor \(\frac{N-n}{N-1}\) is the finite-population correction (ignorable if \(n/N\le0.05\)).

Normal population: \(\bar y\) is exactly normal. Non-normal population (finite variance): by the Central Limit Theorem, \(Z=\frac{\bar y-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\to N(0,1)\) as \(n\to\infty\); roughly normal from \(n\approx30\).

Point and interval estimation

An estimator is a random variable built from the sample; its value is an estimate; the process is estimation. A point estimator (e.g. \(\bar Y\) for \(\mu\)) has a sampling distribution; different samples give different estimates.

  • Unbiased: \(\E(\hat\theta)=\theta\); \(\text{Bias}=\E(\hat\theta)-\theta\).
  • Efficient: among unbiased estimators, smaller variance wins; the smallest-variance one is the MVUE.

Bias and mean squared error

\[MSE(\hat\theta)=\V(\hat\theta)+[\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)]^2\]

where \(\hat\theta\) = estimator of the parameter \(\theta\), \(\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)=\E(\hat\theta)-\theta\), \(MSE\) = mean squared error.

Variance is random error; squared bias is systematic error. A biased estimator can be centred on \(m\ne\mu\) and miss the truth even when its spread is small, and confidence intervals around it cover \(\mu\) less often than claimed. Small bias with much smaller variance can beat an unbiased estimator. Bias also comes from data collection (measurement error, nonresponse) and from estimators like ratio estimators. Equal MSE means roughly equally good.

\(\sigma\) known

\[\bar y\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]

Example: \(\sigma=20,n=64,\bar y=75\): \(75\pm1.96(2.5)\), i.e. \(\mathbf{[70.1,\,79.9]}\).

\(\sigma\) unknown

\[\bar y\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt n}\]

Example (24 trucks): \(\bar y=18.68,s=1.695,t_{23,0.05}=1.714\): \(\mathbf{[18.1,\,19.3]}\) at 90%.

where \(\bar y\) = sample mean, \(n\) = sample size, \(\sigma\) = population standard deviation, \(s\) = sample standard deviation, \(1-\alpha\) = confidence level, \(z_{\alpha/2}\) = standard normal critical value, \(t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\) = \(t\) critical value with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

With SRSWOR from a finite population the SE inside these intervals carries the FPC, \(\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N}\frac{s^2}{n}}\).

Estimating a proportion

For a two-class attribute, \(P=A/N\), \(Q=1-P\), estimated by \(p=a/n\), \(q=1-p\). Define the indicator \(Y_i=1\) if unit \(i\) is in class \(C\), else 0. Then \(\sum Y_i=A\), \(\bar Y=P\), \(\bar y=p\), \(\sum y_i^2=a=np\), so \(s^2=\frac{npq}{n-1}\) and \(S^2=\frac{NPQ}{N-1}\). All mean results carry over.

SRSWOR

\[\E(p)=P\]
\[\V(p)=\frac{N-n}{N-1}\frac{PQ}{n}\]
\[\vh(p)=\frac{N-n}{N}\frac{pq}{n-1}\]

SRSWR

\[\E(p)=P\]
\[\V(p)=\frac{PQ}{n}\]
\[\vh(p)=\frac{pq}{n-1}\]

where \(P=A/N\) = population proportion (\(A\) units of the population are in class \(C\)), \(p=a/n\) = sample proportion (\(a\) sampled units are in \(C\)), \(Q=1-P\), \(q=1-p\), \(\V(p)\) = variance of \(p\), \(\vh(p)\) = its unbiased estimator.

Total in class: \(\hat A=Np=Na/n\), \(\V(\hat A)=N^2\V(p)\), \(\vh(\hat A)=N^2\vh(p)\).

Confidence interval for \(P\)

For large \(N,n\), \((p-P)/\sqrt{\V(p)}\approx N(0,1)\):

\[p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\vh(p)}\]

where \(p\) = sample proportion, \(z_{\alpha/2}\) = standard normal critical value for confidence level \(1-\alpha\), \(\sqrt{\vh(p)}\) = estimated standard error of \(p\).

Continuity correction (discrete to continuous, matters for small \(n\) or \(p\) near 0 or 1): limits \(\big(p-z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\vh(p)}-\frac1{2n},\ p+z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\vh(p)}+\frac1{2n}\big)\), i.e. the interval is widened by \(\frac1{2n}\) each side.

The slide prints the correction with the opposite sign on the upper limit; the usual convention is to widen the interval, as above.

More than two classes

Classes \(C_1,\dots,C_k\) (e.g. coffee: very strong, strong, mild, very mild), \(P_i=C_i/N\). The sample counts \(c_1,\dots,c_k\) have probability \(\frac{\binom{C_1}{c_1}\cdots\binom{C_k}{c_k}}{\binom Nn}\) (SRSWOR). Estimate \(p_i=c_i/n\), with \(\E(p_i)=P_i\) and \(\vh(p_i)=\frac{N-n}{N}\frac{p_iq_i}{n-1}\). Class size: \(\hat C_i=Np_i\), \(\vh(\hat C_i)=N^2\vh(p_i)\) (the slide writes \(N\); it should be \(N^2\)).

Inverse sampling (rare attributes)

When the attribute is rare (rare genes, rare cancer cells), ordinary \(n/N\) estimation fails. Draw units one by one, equal probability, without replacement, and stop once a preset number \(m\) of units with the attribute is observed. The sample size \(n\) is then a random variable.

Determining the sample size

The size must be fixed before the survey. Larger \(n\): variance falls, cost rises. Smaller \(n\): the reverse. Balance precision and cost. Criteria: variance, error, CI width, CV, relative error, cost. Each needs \(S\) beforehand, from a pilot survey, past data, past experience, long association with the subject, or prior information. Formulas below assume SRSWOR; SRSWR is similar (drop the FPC).
