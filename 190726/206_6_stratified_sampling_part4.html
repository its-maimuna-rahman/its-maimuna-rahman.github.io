Stratified Sampling: Kish Allocation and Post-stratification

StRS Part 4

a sample-size rule that works without stratum standard deviations, and a way to fix a sample whose composition does not match the population. Every formula comes with a worked example, and the two ideas are kept separate on purpose.

See it first

Three strata, one sample of \(n\) units, one dial. The dial is \(\alpha\), the power in the Kish rule. At \(0\) every stratum gets the same number of units. At \(1\) the sample follows the population shares. In between, big strata get more sample, but not in full proportion.

The variance is the FPC-free formula from Part 3, \(\sum_h w_h^2S_h^2/n_h\). Drag the dial and watch where the sample goes and what it costs in variance.

Why a power rule?

Neyman allocation from Part 2 is the best rule, but it asks for something you often do not have: a number \(S_h\) for every stratum. A pilot survey may give you those numbers, but a first survey cannot.

What you usually know

The size of every stratum, \(N_h\), is normally on the sampling frame, the census, or an administrative list. That part is cheap and reliable.

What you often do not know

The spread \(S_h\) inside each stratum. With no pilot and no earlier survey, guessing \(S_h\) badly is worse than admitting you do not know it.

What Kish does

It uses only \(N_h\), and adds one dial \(\alpha\) that decides how strongly the sample follows stratum size. You do not have to claim knowledge of variability.

The compromise in one line. Equal allocation ignores stratum size and can starve a big stratum. Proportional allocation follows size exactly and can starve a small but important stratum. Kish allocation sits on a dial between the two, so neither extreme wins by default.

The Kish rule

The population is cut into \(H\) strata indexed by \(h\). Population and sample sizes add up over strata.

\[N=\sum_{h=1}^{H}N_h\]
\[n=\sum_{h=1}^{H}n_h\]

Allocation formula

Give each stratum a share proportional to \(N_h^{\alpha}\). The power \(\alpha\) sits between \(0\) and \(1\).

\[n_h=n\,\frac{N_h^{\alpha}}{\sum_{g=1}^{H}N_g^{\alpha}},\qquad 0\le\alpha\le1\]

The denominator is just a normalising constant. It forces the pieces to add up to the total sample \(n\), nothing more.

Question. Three strata with \(N=(10000,\ 5000,\ 1000)\) and a total sample of \(n=300\). Use \(\alpha=0.5\). Find the sample sizes.
Solution.

First take square roots.

\[N_1^{0.5}=100\]
\[N_2^{0.5}=70.7107\]
\[N_3^{0.5}=31.6228\]

Add them to get the normalising constant.

\[\sum_{g}N_g^{0.5}=100+70.7107+31.6228=202.3334\]

Now each stratum's share of the sample.

\[\frac{n_1}{n}=\frac{100}{202.3334}=0.4942\]
\[\frac{n_2}{n}=\frac{70.7107}{202.3334}=0.3495\]
\[\frac{n_3}{n}=\frac{31.6228}{202.3334}=0.1563\]

Multiply by \(n=300\).

\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=(148.27,\ 104.85,\ 46.88)\]

Round to whole units and keep the total at 300.

\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=(148,\ 105,\ 47)\]

Check the total.

\[148+105+47=300\]

Notice what happened to stratum 3. It holds only 6.25% of the population, and proportional allocation would give it about 19 units. Kish gives it 47. That is the whole point of the dial.

The three special cases

The dial does not just sit between two rules. At its two ends it becomes the rules you already know.

Case 1: \(\alpha=0\) gives equal allocation

Any number to the power zero is one, so every stratum contributes the same amount to the denominator.

\[N_h^{0}=1\]
\[\sum_{g=1}^{H}N_g^{0}=H\]
\[n_h=n\frac{1}{H}=\frac{n}{H}\]
Question. Same three strata, \(n=300\), but now \(\alpha=0\). Find the sample sizes.
Solution.
\[n_1=n_2=n_3=\frac{300}{3}=100\]

Stratum sizes are ignored completely. The 10000-unit stratum and the 1000-unit stratum get the same 100 interviews.

Case 2: \(\alpha=1\) gives proportional allocation

To the power one, a number is itself, so the denominator becomes \(N\).

\[N_h^{1}=N_h\]
\[\sum_{g=1}^{H}N_g^{1}=N\]
\[n_h=n\frac{N_h}{N}=nw_h\]
Question. Same data, \(\alpha=1\). Find the sample sizes.
Solution.
\[w=\Big(\frac{10000}{16000},\ \frac{5000}{16000},\ \frac{1000}{16000}\Big)=(0.625,\ 0.3125,\ 0.0625)\]
\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=300(0.625,\ 0.3125,\ 0.0625)=(187.5,\ 93.75,\ 18.75)\]

After rounding: \((188,\ 94,\ 18)\), which adds to 300.

Now stratum 3 gets only 18 or 19 units. If that stratum is small but highly variable or politically important, this is too few.

Case 3: \(0\lt\alpha\lt1\) gives intermediate allocation

Inside the interval, the sample share is a fractional power of the population share.

\[\frac{n_h}{n}\propto N_h^{\alpha}\]

The ratio between two strata is easy to read.

\[\frac{n_h}{n_g}=\Big(\frac{N_h}{N_g}\Big)^{\alpha}\]
Question. Stratum A is four times larger than stratum B. How many times more sample does A get at \(\alpha=0.5\) and at \(\alpha=1\)?
Solution.

At \(\alpha=0.5\):

\[\frac{n_A}{n_B}=4^{0.5}=2\]

At \(\alpha=1\):

\[\frac{n_A}{n_B}=4^{1}=4\]

Being four times bigger buys twice the sample at the square-root setting, not four times. That is the whole design: large strata get more, but less than their full share.

\(\alpha\)RuleSample size \(n_h\)Behaviour
0Equal\(n/H\)size ignored
0.5Square-root\(\propto\sqrt{N_h}\)mild tilt toward large strata
1Proportional\(nN_h/N\)follows size exactly

Choosing the power \(\alpha\)

There is no formula that hands you the right \(\alpha\). It is a judgement call about what the survey is for.

Things to weigh

  • How reliable must the estimate be for the small strata?
  • What do pilot data or an earlier survey say about how variable the strata are?
  • What does each extra interview in a small stratum cost in field time?
  • Is there a minimum sample size you must respect in every stratum?

Things to do

  • Try a short list of values, for example \(0,\ 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.75,\ 1\).
  • Compare them on precision and on cost, not on intuition alone.
  • Check that no important stratum falls below its minimum.
  • Write down the reason for the value you pick, so the next survey can revisit it.
Key message. There is no universally best \(\alpha\). The value \(0.5\) is a useful illustration, not a default that suits every survey. Choose \(\alpha\) from the objectives, the information available and the precision required.

When Kish allocation earns its place

Good situations

  • Stratum sizes \(N_h\) are known from the frame.
  • Reliable \(S_h\) values are missing.
  • Proportional allocation leaves small strata with a handful of units.
  • Equal allocation feels too blunt.
  • You want a defensible middle ground, not a fight between two extremes.

What to keep in mind

  • It is a practical rule, not an optimal one.
  • It cannot beat Neyman when good \(S_h\) values exist.
  • Rounding and minimum sizes still have to be handled by hand.
  • With several study variables, one \(\alpha\) cannot suit all of them.
Equal allocation
\(\alpha=0\)
← Kish →
Proportional allocation
\(\alpha=1\)
One-line summary. Use Kish when size is all you know, and use Neyman when you also know the spread.

Kish against Neyman

Plug the Kish sizes into the Part 3 variance and simplify. The answer has a tidy shape.

Variance under Kish allocation

\[V_K=\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{H}N_g^{\alpha}}{n}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{w_h^2S_h^2}{N_h^{\alpha}}\]

It looks heavier than it is. The first factor is a constant for the whole design, and the second is a per-stratum sum, exactly like the Part 3 formula but with \(N_h^{\alpha}\) standing in for the sample size. The algebra is in derivation D3.

Question. Three strata with \(N=(10000,5000,1000)\) and spreads \(S=(10,15,20)\). Total sample \(n=300\). Find the variance under equal, proportional, Kish (\(\alpha=0.5\)) and Neyman allocation.
Solution.

Weights first.

\[w=(0.625,\ 0.3125,\ 0.0625)\]

The per-stratum numerators \(w_h^2S_h^2\):

\[w_1^2S_1^2=0.390625(100)=39.0625\]
\[w_2^2S_2^2=0.0976563(225)=21.9727\]
\[w_3^2S_3^2=0.0039063(400)=1.5625\]

Equal allocation, \(n_h=100\):

\[V_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{39.0625+21.9727+1.5625}{100}=0.6260\]

Proportional allocation, \(n_h=(187.5,\ 93.75,\ 18.75)\):

\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac{39.0625}{187.5}+\frac{21.9727}{93.75}+\frac{1.5625}{18.75}=0.5260\]

Kish allocation, \(n_h=(148.27,\ 104.85,\ 46.88)\):

\[V_K=\frac{39.0625}{148.27}+\frac{21.9727}{104.85}+\frac{1.5625}{46.88}=0.5064\]

Neyman allocation. First the constant:

\[A=\sum_hw_hS_h=0.625(10)+0.3125(15)+0.0625(20)=12.1875\]
\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{A^2}{n}=\frac{148.5352}{300}=0.4951\]

Putting them in order:

\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}=0.4951\ \lt\ V_K=0.5064\ \lt\ V_{\mathrm{prop}}=0.5260\ \lt\ V_{\mathrm{eq}}=0.6260\]

Two lessons. Neyman is still the smallest, as Part 3 promised. But Kish beats proportional comfortably here, because proportional gave the small, highly variable third stratum only 19 units while Kish gave it 47.

Do not over-read the example. Kish is not guaranteed to beat proportional. In the practice exercises there is a data set where equal allocation beats both. The only guaranteed chain is \(V_{\mathrm{opt}}\le V_K\), because Neyman is the minimum over all allocations and Kish is one allocation. The derivation is D4.

Post-stratification: what it is

Sometimes the sample is already in the field before you learn how the population is composed. Post-stratification is the repair job: split the finished sample into groups, and reweight so the groups match the known population shares.

The idea in three steps

  1. Draw the sample and collect the data.
  2. Sort the sampled units into groups called post-strata. These are mutually exclusive and cover everyone.
  3. Weight each post-stratum by its known population share \(N_h/N\), taken from a census or an administrative register.

The post-stratification weight is

\[W_h=\frac{N_h}{N}\]

where \(N_h\) is the known population size of post-stratum \(h\), and \(n_h\) is how many sampled units happened to land in it.

FeatureStratified samplingPost-stratification
Groups known before samplingYesNot necessarily
When the sample is drawnInside each stratumBefore groups exist
Where \(N_h\) is usedIn the designIn the estimation
Sample sizes \(n_h\)Planned in advanceWhatever happened to appear
Main purposePrecision by designAdjustment, then precision
Why bother? A simple random sample can land with the wrong mix of men and women, young and old, urban and rural. If the true mix is known from the census, reweighting to that mix removes a bias that would otherwise sit in the estimate. It does not change who was interviewed. It changes how much each interviewed unit counts.

The post-stratified estimator

Formula

Let \(s_h\) be the sampled units that fall in post-stratum \(h\), and let \(n_h\) be how many there are. The sample mean inside the post-stratum is

\[\bar y_h=\frac{1}{n_h}\sum_{i\in s_h}y_i\]

The post-stratified estimator replaces the sample share by the known population share.

\[\bar y_{PS}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}W_h\bar y_h=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{N_h}{N}\bar y_h\]

Compare it with the ordinary sample mean, which uses whatever shares the sample happened to produce.

\[\bar y=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\frac{n_h}{n}\bar y_h\]

The only difference is the weight: \(n_h/n\) becomes \(N_h/N\).

Question. A health survey splits into urban and rural post-strata. Urban: \(N_U=6000\), \(n_U=80\), \(\bar y_U=24\). Rural: \(N_R=4000\), \(n_R=120\), \(\bar y_R=30\). Total population \(N=10000\). Find the post-stratified mean and the ordinary mean.
Solution.

Population weights:

\[W_U=\frac{6000}{10000}=0.60\]
\[W_R=\frac{4000}{10000}=0.40\]

Post-stratified mean:

\[\bar y_{PS}=0.60(24)+0.40(30)\]
\[\bar y_{PS}=14.4+12=26.4\]

Ordinary mean. The sample is 40% urban and 60% rural, the reverse of the population:

\[\bar y=\frac{80}{200}(24)+\frac{120}{200}(30)\]
\[\bar y=0.4(24)+0.6(30)=9.6+18=27.6\]

Rural units are over-represented and they score higher, so the ordinary mean is pulled up by 1.2.

\[\bar y_{PS}-\bar y=26.4-27.6=-1.2\]

A three-stratum example

Question. Post-strata with \(N=(6000,\ 3000,\ 1000)\), sample counts \(n=(70,\ 50,\ 80)\), and sample means \(\bar y=(24,\ 30,\ 45)\). Total sample \(n=200\). Find both estimates.
Solution.

Population weights:

\[W=(0.6,\ 0.3,\ 0.1)\]

Post-stratified mean:

\[\bar y_{PS}=0.6(24)+0.3(30)+0.1(45)\]
\[\bar y_{PS}=14.4+9+4.5=27.9\]

Sample shares:

\[\frac{n_h}{n}=(0.35,\ 0.25,\ 0.40)\]

Ordinary mean:

\[\bar y=0.35(24)+0.25(30)+0.40(45)\]
\[\bar y=8.4+7.5+18=33.9\]

The gap is large because the third post-stratum, which has the highest mean, is 40% of the sample but only 10% of the population.

\[\bar y_{PS}-\bar y=27.9-33.9=-6.0\]
Check the gap by hand. The difference is a weighted sum of the weight mismatches.
\[\bar y_{PS}-\bar y=\sum_h\Big(\frac{N_h}{N}-\frac{n_h}{n}\Big)\bar y_h\]

For the three-stratum example:

\[(0.6-0.35)(24)+(0.3-0.25)(30)+(0.1-0.40)(45)\]
\[=6+1.5-13.5=-6.0\]

It matches. The formula is derived in D5.

Post-stratification as a weight adjustment

In practice post-stratification is applied by changing the weight of every sampled unit. Start with the design weight \(d_i\) that each unit already carries.

Formulas

First add up the design weights inside each post-stratum. That is the population size the sample claims to represent there.

\[\hat N_h=\sum_{i\in s_h}d_i\]

Compare that with the true size from the register, and stretch or shrink every weight by the ratio.

\[w_i^{*}=d_i\,\frac{N_h}{\hat N_h},\qquad i\in s_h\]

The adjustment factor is the ratio on its own.

\[A_h=\frac{N_h}{\hat N_h}\]
Question. Two post-strata, urban and rural. \(N_U=6000\), \(N_R=4000\), total \(N=10000\), total sample \(n=200\), with \(n_U=80\) and \(n_R=120\). Every unit starts with the same design weight \(d=N/n=50\). Find \(\hat N_h\), the adjustment factors and the new weights.
Solution.

What the sample represents before adjustment:

\[\hat N_U=80(50)=4000\]
\[\hat N_R=120(50)=6000\]

Adjustment factors:

\[A_U=\frac{6000}{4000}=1.5\]
\[A_R=\frac{4000}{6000}=0.6667\]

Urban is under-represented, so its factor is above 1. Rural is over-represented, so its factor is below 1.

New weights:

\[w_U^{*}=50(1.5)=75\]
\[w_R^{*}=50(0.6667)=33.3333\]

Calibration check, the weights must now add up to the known sizes:

\[80(75)=6000=N_U\]
\[120(33.3333)=4000=N_R\]

And the grand total:

\[6000+4000=10000=N\]

The adjusted weighted mean now reproduces the post-stratified estimate of 26.4 exactly. The algebra is in D7.

Two ideas, one sentence each. Kish fixes the design when variability is unknown. Post-stratification fixes the estimation when the composition turns out wrong.

Exercise 2: Kish variance against the alternatives

Strata \(N=(4000,\ 4000,\ 2000)\), spreads \(S=(5,\ 10,\ 20)\), total sample \(n=200\), \(\alpha=0.5\). Find the variance under equal, proportional, Kish and Neyman allocation, and put them in order.

Show answer

Weights and numerators:

\[w=(0.4,\ 0.4,\ 0.2)\]
\[w_h^2S_h^2=(4,\ 16,\ 16)\]

Kish sizes:

\[N^{0.5}=(63.2456,\ 63.2456,\ 44.7214)\]
\[\sum_gN_g^{0.5}=171.2125\]
\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=(73.88,\ 73.88,\ 52.24)\]
\[V_K=\frac{4}{73.88}+\frac{16}{73.88}+\frac{16}{52.24}=0.5770\]

Equal, \(n_h=66.67\):

\[V_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{4+16+16}{66.67}=0.5400\]

Proportional, \(n_h=(80,\ 80,\ 40)\):

\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac{4}{80}+\frac{16}{80}+\frac{16}{40}=0.6500\]

Neyman:

\[A=0.4(5)+0.4(10)+0.2(20)=10\]
\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{100}{200}=0.5000\]

Order:

\[0.5000\ \lt\ 0.5400\ \lt\ 0.5770\ \lt\ 0.6500\]

Here equal allocation beats both Kish and proportional. The third stratum is tiny but far more variable, so giving it a fixed 100 units through equal allocation pays off. This is exactly why Kish is called a practical rule, not an optimal one.

Exercise 3: post-stratified mean and weights

Post-strata \(N=(5000,\ 3000,\ 2000)\), sample counts \(n=(60,\ 90,\ 50)\), sample means \(\bar y=(30,\ 40,\ 50)\). Find \(\bar y_{PS}\), \(\bar y\), and the adjusted weights when every unit starts with \(d=N/n\).

Show answer

Weights and shares:

\[W=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2)\]
\[\frac{n_h}{n}=(0.3,\ 0.45,\ 0.25)\]

Post-stratified mean:

\[\bar y_{PS}=0.5(30)+0.3(40)+0.2(50)=15+12+10=37\]

Ordinary mean:

\[\bar y=0.3(30)+0.45(40)+0.25(50)=9+18+12.5=39.5\]

Gap:

\[\bar y_{PS}-\bar y=-2.5\]

Design weight:

\[d=\frac{10000}{200}=50\]

Represented sizes and factors:

\[\hat N=(3000,\ 4500,\ 2500)\]
\[A=(1.6667,\ 0.6667,\ 0.8000)\]

Adjusted weights:

\[w^{*}=(83.3333,\ 33.3333,\ 40)\]

Calibration check:

\[60(83.3333)=5000,\qquad 90(33.3333)=3000,\qquad 50(40)=2000\]
