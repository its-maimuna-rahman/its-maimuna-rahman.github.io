Life Annuities

A stream of payments that stops the moment a person dies. This page prices that promise: the survival probabilities, the expected present value, the commutation functions, and every standard formula — with one real case solved first.

1 · A real-life case, solved

Priya, aged exactly 60, retires with \$500,000 of savings. She hands it to an insurer in exchange for a whole-life annuity-due of \$100,000: the insurer pays her \$100,000 at the start of every year, for as long as she lives, and the money is gone when she dies.

The insurer prices at an annual effective interest rate of \(i=5\%\) and uses a mortality basis summarised by these survival probabilities:

\(k\)01234
\({}_{k}p_{60}\)1.0000.9700.9350.8950.850

Question: how much must the insurer set aside today to fund these uncertain payments — and is \$500,000 enough?

Step 1 · One payment at a time. A payment of \$100,000 at time \(k\) is made only if Priya survives \(k\) years. Its present value today is therefore payment × discount factor, but that number is paid with probability \({}_{k}p_{60}\) only. So its expected present value is \[\text{EPV}_k = 100{,}000 \times v^{k} \times {}_{k}p_{60}, \qquad v=\frac{1}{1.05}=0.952381.\]
Step 2 · Compute each term. The first payment is made today with certainty, so it carries no discount and no survival factor.
\(k\)Payment\(v^{k}\)\({}_{k}p_{60}\)EPV of payment

Step 3 · Add them up. Factor out the \$100,000 and define \[\ddot{a}_{60}=\sum_{k=0}^{4} v^{k}\,{}_{k}p_{60}=1+0.92381+0.84807+0.77313+0.69930=4.24431.\] Then \[\text{PV}=100{,}000 \times 4.24431=\$424{,}431.\]
Under the 5-year illustration the insurer needs \$424,431 today. Priya's \$500,000 covers it — with \$75,569 to spare.
Step 4 · The illustration is the catch. The table stopped at 4 years, but a 60-year-old can live to 90. Extending the same life table over the full remaining lifetime gives \[\ddot{a}_{60}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} v^{k}{}_{k}p_{60}\approx 6.86670 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{PV}\approx \$686{,}670.\] Now \$500,000 is not enough — it falls about \$187,000 short.
Lesson: the annuity factor is a sum over the whole remaining lifetime. Truncating the sum understates the cost badly — here by 38%.
What the insurer is really doing It is not guessing Priya's death date. It sells 10,000 such annuities and knows that on average the payments will cost exactly the sum of the expected present values. The law of large numbers turns the random variable into a predictable liability.

2 · Definition

The keywords from the case were series of payments, while the individual is alive, survival probability, discount factor, expected present value and uncertain timing. Combined:

Life annuity A series of payments made to an individual conditional on that individual being alive at each payment date, according to specified payment amounts and timing. The payment at time \(k\) is received only if the person survives to time \(k\).
Annuity-certain
Payments are guaranteed. The only uncertainty is the time value of money. Value \(=a_{\overline{n}|}\) or \(s_{\overline{n}|}\).
Life annuity
Payments depend on survival. Uncertainty is time value of money plus mortality.
Actuarial present value (APV)
The probability-weighted present value of the uncertain payment stream — an expectation, not a certain amount.
The one-line idea \[\text{Expected PV of a payment at time }k \;=\; \underbrace{v^{k}}_{\text{discount}}\times\underbrace{{}_{k}p_{x}}_{\text{survival}}\]

3 · The idea in plain words

Ordinary financial maths asks: what is a series of future payments worth today? Actuarial maths adds a second question: will the person still be alive when the payment is due?

So every single payment gets multiplied by two fractions:

  1. The discount factor \(v^{k}\) — because a dollar in \(k\) years is worth less than a dollar today.
  2. The survival probability \({}_{k}p_{x}\) — because the dollar might never arrive at all.

Both fractions are between 0 and 1, so a life annuity is always worth less than the same payment stream paid with certainty. Add up all the discounted, probability-weighted payments and you have the actuarial present value.

Why the present value is a random variable

Take a whole-life annuity-immediate of \$10,000 on a life aged 60. The number of payments is not known in advance:

  • dies after 2 years → payments at years 1 and 2 only;
  • survives 10 years → 10 payments;
  • survives 20 years → 20 payments.

Write \(T_{x}\) for the future lifetime of a life aged \(x\). Then the present value of the payments is itself a random variable: \[Z = v\,I(T_{x}\ge 1) + v^{2}I(T_{x}\ge 2) + v^{3}I(T_{x}\ge 3)+\cdots\] where \(I(\cdot)\) is an indicator (1 if the event happens, 0 otherwise). Taking expectations, and using \(P(T_{x}\ge k)={}_{k}p_{x}\), \[E(Z)=v\,{}_{1}p_{x}+v^{2}{}_{2}p_{x}+v^{3}{}_{3}p_{x}+\cdots\] So the familiar annuity formula is an expectation. That is why we call it the expected present value.

A two-outcome illustration

Suppose a life can have only two possible future lifetimes:

Future lifetimeProbabilityPV of the annuity
1 year0.4\$952
2 years0.6\$1,860
\[E(Z)=0.4(952)+0.6(1{,}860)=380.80+1{,}116.00=\$1{,}496.80.\]
\$1,496.80 is the probability-weighted average. Nobody ever actually receives \$1,496.80 — the real outcome is either \$952 or \$1,860. The average is what the insurer must hold per policy.

4 · The building blocks

4.1 Survival probabilities from a life table

A life table tracks a cohort from birth. The essential columns:

SymbolMeaning
\(\ell_{x}\)Number alive at exact age \(x\)
\(d_{x}\)Number dying between ages \(x\) and \(x+1\)
\(q_{x}\)Probability a life aged \(x\) dies before age \(x+1\)
\(p_{x}\)Probability a life aged \(x\) survives to age \(x+1\)
\(L_{x}\)Person-years lived between ages \(x\) and \(x+1\)
\(T_{x}\)Total future person-years lived above age \(x\)
\(\mathring{e}_{x}\)Complete expectation of life at age \(x\)

The relationships you must be able to quote instantly:

\[d_{x}=\ell_{x}-\ell_{x+1},\qquad q_{x}=\frac{d_{x}}{\ell_{x}},\qquad p_{x}=\frac{\ell_{x+1}}{\ell_{x}},\qquad p_{x}+q_{x}=1.\]

4.2 Multi-year survival

Surviving \(k\) years means surviving year 1 and year 2 and … — but the chain collapses beautifully: \[{}_{k}p_{x}=\frac{\ell_{x+k}}{\ell_{x}},\qquad {}_{k}q_{x}=1-{}_{k}p_{x}.\] Example. With \(\ell_{60}=100{,}000\) and \(\ell_{63}=89{,}500\): \[{}_{3}p_{60}=\frac{89{,}500}{100{,}000}=0.895,\qquad {}_{3}q_{60}=0.105.\] So \(10.5\%\) of 60-year-olds die before reaching 63.

4.3 The expected present value of one payment

A payment of \$1 at time \(k\) requires two things to happen:

Survive. Probability \(={}_{k}p_{x}=\ell_{x+k}/\ell_{x}\).
Discount. Present value \(=v^{k}\), where \(v=1/(1+i)\).

These are independent, so multiply: \[\boxed{v^{k}\,{}_{k}p_{x}}\] This little product is the atom out of which every life-annuity formula is built.

4.4 The whole-life annuity-due

Payments of 1 are made at times \(0,1,2,\dots\) as long as the person is alive. The first payment is certain, so it contributes exactly 1.

\[\ddot{a}_{x}=1+v\,{}_{1}p_{x}+v^{2}{}_{2}p_{x}+v^{3}{}_{3}p_{x}+\cdots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v^{k}\,{}_{k}p_{x}.\]

Applying it to the scenario above with \(\ell_{60}=100{,}000,\ \ell_{61}=97{,}000,\dots\) and \(i=5\%\) reproduces the 4.24431 we found by hand.

5 · The main types of life annuity

Life annuities are classified by when payments start and how long they last.

TypePayment timesNotationIdea
Whole-life immediate\(1,2,3,\dots\)\(\bar{a}_{x}\)End of each year, for the rest of life
Whole-life due\(0,1,2,\dots\)\(\ddot{a}_{x}\)Start of each year, for the rest of life
Temporary (term)\(1,\dots,n\) (or \(0,\dots,n-1\))\(\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}\), \(\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}\)At most \(n\) payments, and only while alive
Deferredstarts after \(k\) years\({}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x}\)Nothing during the \(k\)-year deferral, then life payments
Deferred temporary\(k+1,\dots,k+n\)\({}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}\)Deferral then a limited term
Due vs. immediate The only difference is a one-period shift. Since the payment at time 0 is certain, \[\ddot{a}_{x}=1+\bar{a}_{x}.\] A due annuity is always worth exactly 1 more than the corresponding immediate annuity.

6 · Commutation functions

A whole-life annuity has infinitely many terms. Rather than sum them one by one, actuaries pre-tabulate two columns that do the summing for us.

The D-function

\[\boxed{D_{x}=v^{x}\ell_{x}}\qquad\text{— the discounted number alive at age }x.\]

Its whole purpose is the ratio

\[\frac{D_{x+k}}{D_{x}}=\frac{v^{x+k}\ell_{x+k}}{v^{x}\ell_{x}}=v^{k}\frac{\ell_{x+k}}{\ell_{x}}=v^{k}\,{}_{k}p_{x}.\]
The bridge \[\frac{D_{x+k}}{D_{x}}=v^{k}\,{}_{k}p_{x}\] Every life-annuity formula follows from substituting this into the sum.

The N-function

\[N_{x}=D_{x}+D_{x+1}+D_{x+2}+\cdots=\sum_{k=x}^{\infty}D_{k}\]

So \(N_{x+1}=D_{x+1}+D_{x+2}+\cdots\) is just \(N_{x}\) with the first term removed, giving the recursion

\[N_{x}=D_{x}+N_{x+1}.\]

Death-related and accumulated functions

FunctionDefinitionInterpretation
\(D_{x}\)\(v^{x}\ell_{x}\)Discounted number alive at age \(x\)
\(N_{x}\)\(\sum_{k\ge x}D_{k}\)Accumulated \(D\)-functions from age \(x\) onward
\(C_{x}\)\(v^{x+1}d_{x}\)Discounted deaths between \(x\) and \(x+1\)
\(M_{x}\)\(\sum_{k\ge x}C_{k}\)Accumulated \(C\)-functions
\(S_{x}\)\(\sum_{k\ge x}N_{k}\)Accumulated \(N\)-functions

For life annuities we only need \(D\) and \(N\). The \(C\), \(M\), \(S\) columns are tabulated alongside them and are used when pricing life insurance benefits, which is outside the annuity scope here.

Practical note \(D_{x}=v^{x}\ell_{x}\) contains a huge constant factor \(v^{x}\). Since every annuity formula uses \(D\)'s only as a ratio with the same base age, that factor cancels — which is why printed tables often use a rescaled version.

7 · Worked examples

Example 1 · Whole-life immediate

A person aged 60 buys a whole-life annuity-immediate paying \$10,000 at the end of each year while alive. The mortality/interest basis gives \[D_{60}=4{,}000,\qquad N_{61}=516{,}000.\] Find the actuarial present value.

Formula. \(\bar{a}_{60}=\dfrac{N_{61}}{D_{60}}\).
Per dollar of annuity. \[\bar{a}_{60}=\frac{516{,}000}{4{,}000}=129.\]
Scale to the actual payment. \[\text{PV}=10{,}000\times129=\$1{,}290{,}000.\]
The insurer must hold \$1.29 million today to fund \$10,000 a year at each year-end for life.

Example 2 · 10-year temporary immediate

A person aged 50 will receive \$20,000 at the end of each year for at most 10 years, provided they are alive at each payment date. Given \[D_{50}=6{,}000,\quad N_{51}=700{,}000,\quad N_{61}=560{,}000.\]

Formula. A term annuity-immediate keeps only the \(D\)-terms from \(x+1\) to \(x+n\): \[\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{N_{x+1}-N_{x+n+1}}{D_{x}}.\] With \(x=50,\ n=10\): \[\bar{a}_{50:\overline{10}|}=\frac{N_{51}-N_{61}}{D_{50}}=\frac{700{,}000-560{,}000}{6{,}000}=\frac{140{,}000}{6{,}000}=23.3333.\]
Scale. \[\text{PV}=20{,}000\times23.3333\approx\$466{,}667.\]
Why subtract? \(N_{51}\) contains every \(D\)-term from age 51 onward; \(N_{61}\) contains everything from 61 onward. The difference leaves exactly \(D_{51}+\cdots+D_{60}\) — the ten years we want.

Example 3 · 5-year deferred whole-life immediate

A person aged 55 will receive \$15,000 at the end of each year, beginning after a 5-year deferral, for as long as they live. Given \[D_{55}=5{,}000,\qquad N_{61}=360{,}000.\]

Payment times. Years 1–5: nothing. First payment at the end of year 6, then 7, 8, …
Formula. A \(k\)-year deferred whole-life immediate starts at \(k+1\): \[{}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x}=\frac{N_{x+k+1}}{D_{x}}.\] With \(x=55,\ k=5\): \[{}_{5|}\bar{a}_{55}=\frac{N_{55+5+1}}{D_{55}}=\frac{N_{61}}{D_{55}}=\frac{360{,}000}{5{,}000}=72.\]
Scale. \[\text{PV}=15{,}000\times72=\$1{,}080{,}000.\]
Deferral makes the annuity cheaper than an immediate one starting at 55 — the insurer collects five years of interest and the person may not survive to collect anything.

9 · Derivations

The five-step recipe Every formula on this page is built the same way: 1 write the payment times · 2 write \(v^{k}{}_{k}p_{x}\) for each · 3 replace it by \(D_{x+k}/D_{x}\) · 4 add the \(D\)-terms · 5 collapse the sum into \(N\)'s.
D1 · The bridge: \(D_{x+k}/D_{x}=v^{k}{}_{k}p_{x}\)

By definition \(D_{x}=v^{x}\ell_{x}\), so \[\frac{D_{x+k}}{D_{x}}=\frac{v^{x+k}\ell_{x+k}}{v^{x}\ell_{x}}=v^{k}\cdot\frac{\ell_{x+k}}{\ell_{x}}=v^{k}\,{}_{k}p_{x}.\] The factor \(v^{x}\) cancels. Everything below is this identity applied to a list of payment times.

D2 · Whole-life annuity-immediate: \(\bar{a}_{x}=N_{x+1}/D_{x}\)

Payment times: \(1,2,3,\dots\)

\[\bar{a}_{x}=v\,{}_{1}p_{x}+v^{2}{}_{2}p_{x}+v^{3}{}_{3}p_{x}+\cdots =\frac{D_{x+1}}{D_{x}}+\frac{D_{x+2}}{D_{x}}+\frac{D_{x+3}}{D_{x}}+\cdots\] Pull out \(1/D_{x}\): \[=\frac{D_{x+1}+D_{x+2}+D_{x+3}+\cdots}{D_{x}}=\frac{N_{x+1}}{D_{x}}.\] Note the numerator starts at \(D_{x+1}\), not \(D_{x}\) — because the first payment is at time 1.

D3 · Whole-life annuity-due: \(\ddot{a}_{x}=N_{x}/D_{x}\)

Payment times: \(0,1,2,\dots\)

\[\ddot{a}_{x}=1+v\,{}_{1}p_{x}+v^{2}{}_{2}p_{x}+\cdots =\frac{D_{x}}{D_{x}}+\frac{D_{x+1}}{D_{x}}+\frac{D_{x+2}}{D_{x}}+\cdots=\frac{N_{x}}{D_{x}}.\] The leading term is \(D_{x}/D_{x}=1\), i.e. the certain payment today.

Corollary. Since \(N_{x}=D_{x}+N_{x+1}\), \[\frac{N_{x}}{D_{x}}=1+\frac{N_{x+1}}{D_{x}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot{a}_{x}=1+\bar{a}_{x}.\]

D4 · Temporary annuities (immediate and due)

Immediate, payments at \(1,\dots,n\):

\[\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{D_{x+1}}{D_{x}}+\cdots+\frac{D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}=\frac{D_{x+1}+\cdots+D_{x+n}}{D_{x}}.\] Now \[N_{x+1}=D_{x+1}+\cdots+D_{x+n}+D_{x+n+1}+\cdots\] \[N_{x+n+1}=D_{x+n+1}+D_{x+n+2}+\cdots\] Subtracting kills the tail and leaves exactly the wanted terms: \[\boxed{\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{N_{x+1}-N_{x+n+1}}{D_{x}}}.\]

Due, payments at \(0,\dots,n-1\): the same argument starting one term earlier gives \[\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}{D_{x}}.\] Check: \(\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=1+\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n-1}|}\).

D5 · Deferred whole-life annuity-immediate

Deferral of \(k\) years: no payments in years \(1,\dots,k\); the first payment is at the end of year \(k+1\).

\[{}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x}=v^{k+1}{}_{k+1}p_{x}+v^{k+2}{}_{k+2}p_{x}+\cdots =\frac{D_{x+k+1}}{D_{x}}+\frac{D_{x+k+2}}{D_{x}}+\cdots\] The numerator is precisely \(N_{x+k+1}\): \[\boxed{{}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x}=\frac{N_{x+k+1}}{D_{x}}}.\]

Equivalently, by subtraction of whole-life annuities, \({}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x}=\bar{a}_{x}-{}_{k}\bar{a}_{x}=\dfrac{N_{x+1}-N_{x+k+1}}{D_{x}}\) — the \(k\)-year temporary piece is exactly what gets removed.

D6 · Deferred temporary annuity: \({}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}\)

Payment times: \(k+1,\ k+2,\ \dots,\ k+n\).

\[{}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{D_{x+k+1}+D_{x+k+2}+\cdots+D_{x+k+n}}{D_{x}}.\] Take the two accumulations \[N_{x+k+1}=D_{x+k+1}+D_{x+k+2}+\cdots\] \[N_{x+k+n+1}=D_{x+k+n+1}+D_{x+k+n+2}+\cdots\] Their difference is exactly the \(n\) terms in the numerator: \[\boxed{{}_{k|}\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{N_{x+k+1}-N_{x+k+n+1}}{D_{x}}}.\]
D7 · Why the formula is an expectation

Let \(T_{x}\) be the future lifetime and \(I(\cdot)\) the indicator function. For an immediate annuity of 1, \[Z=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}I(T_{x}\ge k).\] Linearity of expectation works even for infinite sums, so \[E(Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}\,E[I(T_{x}\ge k)]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}P(T_{x}\ge k)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v^{k}{}_{k}p_{x}.\] Since \(P(T_{x}\ge k)={}_{k}p_{x}\) is the definition of the \(k\)-year survival probability, the actuarial formula is nothing but \(E(Z)\). The randomness of \(T_{x}\) is fully absorbed into the probabilities.
