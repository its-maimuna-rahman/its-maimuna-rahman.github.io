Central Sampling Distributions: Chi-Squared, t and F

A plant that fills bottles, a research station that grows rice and a mill that runs three production lines all share one problem. They hold a small sample and must make a claim about a population they cannot fully see. Three distributions, all built from the normal curve, make that claim precise. This guide builds each one, states its exact properties and shows how the three depend on each other.

1 · The common starting point

Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be independent draws from a normal population \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). Define the sample mean and sample variance

\[ \bar X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i,\qquad S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X_i-\bar X\right)^2 . \]
Three facts that everything below rests on (i) \(\bar X\sim N\!\left(\mu,\ \sigma^2/n\right)\).
(ii) \(\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\).
(iii) \(\bar X\) and \(S^2\) are independent.

Fact (iii) holds only for normal populations, and it is what makes exact small-sample inference possible. Take a plant that fills 500 ml bottles. The machine has two separate settings: where it centres the fill, and how much the fill wobbles. Fact (iii) says a sample that happens to average high tells you nothing about whether the same sample looks too variable.

The unknown \(\sigma\) is the obstacle. The quantity \(\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)/\sigma\) is exactly standard normal, but it cannot be computed without \(\sigma\). Each distribution below is the answer to a specific way of dealing with that obstacle: study \(S^2\) itself (chi-squared), replace \(\sigma\) by \(S\) (t), or compare two variance estimates (F). In each case the goal is a quantity whose distribution is fully known, even though \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are not.

2 · The chi-squared distribution

Let \(Z_1,\dots,Z_k\) be independent standard normal variables. Their sum of squares is chi-squared with \(k\) degrees of freedom, the number of independent squares being added:

\[ V=Z_1^2+\cdots+Z_k^2\sim\chi^2_k,\qquad f(x)=\frac{x^{k/2-1}\,e^{-x/2}}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)},\quad x\gt 0 . \]

This is a gamma distribution with shape \(k/2\) and scale \(2\). Squares cannot be negative, so the density lives on the positive axis. For small \(k\) it piles up near zero (\(\chi^2_2\) is exponential with mean 2). As \(k\) grows the curve moves right and turns nearly symmetric, because it is a sum of \(k\) independent terms, each with mean 1 and variance 2. Independent chi-squared variables add: \(\chi^2_a+\chi^2_b\sim\chi^2_{a+b}\).

In fact (ii), the \(n\) deviations \(X_i-\bar X\) always sum to zero, so only \(n-1\) of them can move freely. That is why the degrees of freedom are \(n-1\) and not \(n\).

Operating on a variance: the bottling plant

The plant draws \(n=10\) bottles and finds \(s^2=4.2\ \text{ml}^2\). Because \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\) has a known distribution, the middle 95% of its probability gives a confidence interval for \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \left[\ \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2,\;n-1}}\ ,\ \ \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1}}\ \right] , \]

where \(\chi^2_{\alpha,k}\) is the point with area \(\alpha\) to its right. With 9 degrees of freedom, \(\chi^2_{0.025,9}=19.023\) and \(\chi^2_{0.975,9}=2.700\).

Lower limit. \(\dfrac{9\times 4.2}{19.023}=\dfrac{37.8}{19.023}\approx 1.99\).
Upper limit. \(\dfrac{37.8}{2.700}=14.0\).
The 95% interval for \(\sigma^2\) is \((1.99,\ 14.0)\), so \(\sigma\) lies between about 1.41 ml and 3.74 ml.

Two features matter. The interval is very wide, because ten bottles say little about spread. It is also not centred on \(s^2\): the upper limit sits much farther away than the lower one because the chi-squared curve is skewed to the right. Variance intervals are the clearest place to see that skewness at work.

The second major use is the Pearson statistic \(\sum (O_i-E_i)^2/E_i\), which compares observed counts with expected counts. A hospital checking whether births are spread evenly across the seven weekdays compares seven observed counts with seven equal expected counts. For large counts the statistic follows \(\chi^2\) with (categories minus 1 minus estimated parameters) degrees of freedom. Only the right tail is used, because any departure from the expected pattern makes the sum larger.

3 · Student’s t distribution

Let \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(V\sim\chi^2_\nu\) be independent. Then

\[ T=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}\sim t_\nu,\qquad f(t)=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\;\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}} . \]

It arises when \(\sigma\) is replaced by \(S\). Divide the numerator and denominator of \((\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\) by \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). The numerator becomes the standard normal \(Z\) from fact (i). The denominator becomes \(\sqrt{V/(n-1)}\) with \(V=(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\) from fact (ii), and fact (iii) supplies the independence:

\[ \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}. \]

The curve is symmetric about zero like the normal, but its tails are heavier. The reason is that \(S\) is itself a random estimate. A sample that happens to have a small \(S\) inflates the ratio, and this produces extreme values more often than a normal curve would. With \(\nu=1\) the distribution is the Cauchy, which has no mean. As \(\nu\to\infty\), \(V/\nu\) settles at 1 and \(t_\nu\) becomes \(N(0,1)\).

Operating on a mean: the rice trial

A research station harvests \(n=10\) plots and records a mean yield of 4.8 tonnes per hectare with \(s=0.6\). The standard error is \(0.6/\sqrt{10}\approx 0.190\). With 9 degrees of freedom the 97.5% point is \(t_{0.025,9}=2.262\), so

\[ 4.8\ \pm\ 2.262\times 0.190\ =\ 4.8\ \pm\ 0.429\ \Rightarrow\ (4.37,\ 5.23). \]
Using the normal value 1.96 would give a margin of only 0.372. That interval is too narrow, because it ignores the uncertainty in \(S\).

The same construction drives the two-sample comparison of means with pooled variance (\(n_1+n_2-2\) degrees of freedom) and the test of a regression coefficient (\(n-p\) degrees of freedom, with \(p\) coefficients fitted). In each case the numerator is a normal estimate and the denominator is an estimated standard error built from a chi-squared quantity.

Moments of order \(\nu\) and above fail because the tails fall only like \(|t|^{-(\nu+1)}\). Low degrees of freedom therefore carry a real cost: with \(\nu\le 2\) even the variance is infinite.

4 · The F distribution

Let \(U\sim\chi^2_{d_1}\) and \(V\sim\chi^2_{d_2}\) be independent. Each divided by its own degrees of freedom is an average squared deviation, so the ratio compares two such averages:

\[ F=\frac{U/d_1}{V/d_2}\sim F_{d_1,d_2},\qquad f(x)=\frac{1}{x\,B\!\left(\frac{d_1}{2},\frac{d_2}{2}\right)}\sqrt{\frac{(d_1x)^{d_1}\,d_2^{\,d_2}}{(d_1x+d_2)^{d_1+d_2}}},\quad x\gt 0 . \]

For two independent normal samples of sizes \(n_1\) and \(n_2\), facts (ii) applied to each give

\[ \frac{S_1^2/\sigma_1^2}{S_2^2/\sigma_2^2}\sim F_{n_1-1,\;n_2-1}. \]

When \(\sigma_1=\sigma_2\) the unknown variance cancels and the ratio \(S_1^2/S_2^2\) alone follows an F curve. A mill comparing the thread-strength variability of two lines tests exactly this: a ratio far from 1 in either direction is evidence of unequal spread.

Operating on several means: the fertiliser trial

Three fertilisers are tried on 10 plots each, so \(N=30\). The between-group sum of squares \(SS_B\) and the within-group sum of squares \(SS_W\) are, after dividing by \(\sigma^2\), independent chi-squared variables when all three true means are equal, with 2 and 27 degrees of freedom. Hence

\[ F=\frac{SS_B/2}{SS_W/27}=\frac{MS_B}{MS_W}\sim F_{2,\,27}\quad\text{when the three means are equal}. \]

The denominator estimates \(\sigma^2\) regardless of what the means are. The numerator also estimates \(\sigma^2\) only when the means are equal, and otherwise grows. So only the right tail is used. The 5% critical value is \(F_{0.05;\,2,27}=3.354\). Suppose the trial gives \(F=5.1\). Since \(5.1\gt 3.354\), the data reject equal means, and a follow-up comparison of pairs is needed to see which fertiliser differs.

The mean of this distribution is above 1 for every \(d_2\), yet close to 1 when \(d_2\) is large: if both estimates target the same variance, their ratio is centred near 1 but, being a ratio, it leans to the right. The variance-ratio test is sensitive to non-normality, so it should be used with checked data.

5 · How the three connect

The normal curve is the raw material. Squaring and adding normals gives chi-squared. Dividing a normal by the root of a scaled chi-squared gives t. Dividing one scaled chi-squared by another gives F. Each step reuses the previous one, so the properties of one distribution constrain the others.

LinkStatement
Normal to chi-squared\(Z\sim N(0,1)\Rightarrow Z^2\sim\chi^2_1\), and \(\chi^2_a+\chi^2_b\sim\chi^2_{a+b}\) for independent terms
Chi-squared to t\(T=Z\big/\sqrt{V/\nu}\) with \(V\sim\chi^2_\nu\) independent of \(Z\)
Chi-squared to F\(F=(U/d_1)\big/(V/d_2)\) with independent \(U\sim\chi^2_{d_1}\), \(V\sim\chi^2_{d_2}\)
t squared is F\(T^2=Z^2\big/(V/\nu)\), so \(t_\nu^{2}\sim F_{1,\nu}\)
Reciprocal rule\(1/F_{d_1,d_2}\sim F_{d_2,d_1}\), hence \(F_{1-\alpha;\,d_1,d_2}=\dfrac{1}{F_{\alpha;\,d_2,d_1}}\) for right-tail points
Limit in the denominator\(t_\nu\to N(0,1)\) and \(d_1F_{d_1,d_2}\to\chi^2_{d_1}\) as \(\nu,d_2\to\infty\)
Special cases\(t_1\) is Cauchy; \(\chi^2_2\) is exponential with mean 2; \(\chi^2_k\) is gamma with shape \(k/2\), scale 2
Check with the rice trial The two-sided 5% point of \(t_9\) is \(2.262\), and \(2.262^2=5.117\), which is the 5% critical value of \(F_{1,9}\). A two-sided t test of one mean and a one-tailed F test on 1 and 9 degrees of freedom always agree.

The limits explain why large samples behave so well. As the degrees of freedom of a denominator grow, the estimated variance stops fluctuating, and the extra randomness that made t heavy-tailed and F wide disappears. This is also why the t critical value 2.262 at 9 degrees of freedom falls toward 1.96 as the sample grows.

6 · Choosing the right distribution

Spread of one population

The statistic \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\) follows \(\chi^2_{n-1}\). Use it for variance intervals and for tests of a stated variance. Use the Pearson sum for count tables.

Mean with \(\sigma\) unknown

The statistic \((\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\) follows \(t_{n-1}\). Use it for one-sample, two-sample and regression-coefficient inference.

Compare spreads or several means

The ratio of two variance estimates follows \(F\). Use it for variance ratios, ANOVA and overall regression tests, always on the right tail for ANOVA.

Assumption to check All exact results above assume normal populations. For means with moderate or large samples, the central limit theorem makes the t procedures dependable even when the data are somewhat non-normal. The chi-squared interval for a variance and the F test for a variance ratio are much less forgiving, so a normal quantile plot of the data should come first.
