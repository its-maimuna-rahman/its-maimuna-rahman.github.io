Sample Size Determination

AST204 Design and Analysis of Experiments I · Lecture 16 · How many replicates \(n\) per treatment? The confidence-interval method, power and OC curves, and designing for a minimum detectable difference.

Big Picture

Before running an experiment you must choose \(n\), the number of replicates per treatment. Too few and a real difference can slip through unnoticed (a Type II error); too many and time and material are wasted. The rule of thumb: small effects need more replicates than large effects, and noisier data need more than clean data.

The CI method asks for \(n\) so that differences are estimated to within ±30 (answer \(n=7\)); the OC method asks for \(n\) so that real differences are detected with 90% probability (answers \(n=4\) to \(6\), depending on the difference). The two answers differ because the two questions differ. Both methods need an advance estimate of \(\sigma^2\), which comes from past experiments, a pilot run, or judgment.

Confidence Interval Method

Here you decide in advance how wide the interval for a difference of two treatment means may be. For equal replicates, the half-width (margin of error) is

\[ E=t_{\alpha/2,\,N-a}\sqrt{\frac{2\,MS_E}{n}},\qquad N-a=a(n-1) \]

Because the \(t\) value depends on \(n\) (through the error degrees of freedom), you cannot solve for \(n\) directly. Instead: put the guessed \(\sigma^2\) in place of \(MS_E\), then try \(n=2,3,4,\dots\) until \(E\) first drops to the target or below.

Etch data. \(a=4\), \(\alpha=0.05\), \(\sigma=25\) so \(\sigma^2=625\), target half-width \(\pm30\).

Power and Operating Characteristic (OC) Curves

An OC curve plots the Type II error probability \(\beta\) of a test (for one fixed sample size) against a quantity that measures how false \(H_0\) is. The power is \(1-\beta\):

\[ \beta=1-P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ false})=1-P\big(F_0\gt F_{\alpha,a-1,N-a}\mid H_0\text{ false}\big) \]

To compute this we need the distribution of \(F_0=MS_{Treatments}/MS_E\) when \(H_0\) is false: it follows a noncentral \(F\) distribution with \(a-1\) and \(N-a\) df and noncentrality parameter \(\delta\). When \(\delta=0\) it is the ordinary \(F\). The bigger \(\delta\), the further the distribution slides to the right, so rejection becomes more likely.

\[ \delta=\frac{n\sum_{i=1}^{a}\tau_i^2}{\sigma^2},\qquad \Phi^2=\frac{n\sum_{i=1}^{a}\tau_i^2}{a\,\sigma^2}=\frac{\delta}{a} \]

The charts (Appendix Chart V) are drawn against \(\Phi\), for \(\alpha=0.05\) and \(0.01\) and various numerator and denominator df. How to use a chart: compute \(\Phi\); pick the curve for \(\nu_1=a-1\) and \(\nu_2=a(n-1)\); read \(\beta\) on the vertical axis; power \(=1-\beta\).

  • Raise \(n\) or spread the means further apart: \(\Phi\) grows, \(\beta\) falls.
  • Raise \(\sigma\): \(\Phi\) shrinks, power falls.
  • You must supply \(\sigma^2\) and the pattern of means, which is the hard part in practice. If unsure about \(\sigma^2\), repeat the calculation for several plausible values (a sensitivity analysis) before committing to \(n\).

Example: Specifying the Treatment Means

Etch experiment, \(\alpha=0.01\). We want power at least 0.90 if the true means are \(\mu=(575,\,600,\,650,\,675)\), and we believe \(\sigma\le25\).

Step 1: effects. \(\bar\mu=\dfrac{575+600+650+675}{4}=\dfrac{2500}{4}=625\), so \(\tau_i=\mu_i-\bar\mu=(-50,\,-25,\,25,\,50)\).
Step 2: \(\sum\tau_i^2=2500+625+625+2500=6250\).
Step 3: \(\Phi^2=\dfrac{n(6250)}{4(25)^2}=\dfrac{6250\,n}{2500}=2.5n\).
Step 4: \(\nu_1=a-1=3\), \(\nu_2=4(n-1)\). Try values of \(n\):
\(n\)\(\Phi^2=2.5n\)\(\Phi\)\(\nu_2\)\(\beta\) (chart)power (chart)power (exact)

With \(n=3\) the power (0.75) is below 0.90, so three replicates are not enough. \(n=4\) already gives 0.96, so \(n=4\) is the smallest sufficient choice; \(n=5\) adds a safety margin. The "exact" column is computed from the noncentral \(F\) distribution rather than read off the chart.

Example: Specifying a Minimum Difference

Choosing a full set of means is awkward. A more natural question: "If any two means differ by at least \(D\), I want \(H_0\) rejected with high probability." In the worst case for power, the smallest possible \(\Phi^2\) is

\[ \Phi^2=\frac{nD^2}{2a\sigma^2} \]

(derivation D3 below). If the true means are spread out more than this worst case, the power is even higher, so the sample size found this way is safe.

Etch data. Detect any two means differing by \(D=75\) Å/min, power \(\ge0.90\), \(\alpha=0.01\), \(\sigma=25\), \(a=4\): \[\Phi^2=\frac{n(75)^2}{2(4)(25)^2}=\frac{5625\,n}{5000}=1.125\,n\] Worst-case means: two means at \(\pm D/2=\pm37.5\) from the grand mean, the other two at the grand mean, so \(\sum\tau_i^2=2(37.5)^2=2812.5=D^2/2\). Check: \(\Phi^2=\dfrac{n(2812.5)}{4(625)}=1.125\,n\).

Derivations & Proofs

Tap a bar to open.

D1The half-width of the interval for \(\mu_i-\mu_j\)▸

The two averages are independent, each with variance \(\sigma^2/n\):

\[V(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{j\cdot})=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\frac{\sigma^2}{n}=\frac{2\sigma^2}{n}\]

Estimating \(\sigma^2\) by \(MS_E\) (\(N-a\) df), the standardised difference has a \(t_{N-a}\) distribution, so

\[P\Big(-t_{\alpha/2,N-a}\le\frac{(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{j\cdot})-(\mu_i-\mu_j)}{\sqrt{2MS_E/n}}\le t_{\alpha/2,N-a}\Big)=1-\alpha\]

Solving for \(\mu_i-\mu_j\) gives \((\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{j\cdot})\pm t_{\alpha/2,N-a}\sqrt{2MS_E/n}\). The \(\pm\) part is the half-width \(E\). Example, \(n=7\): \(E=2.064\sqrt{2(625)/7}=2.064(13.363)=27.58\).

D2Why \(\delta=n\sum\tau_i^2/\sigma^2\) measures how false \(H_0\) is▸

From the one-way ANOVA, \(E(MS_E)=\sigma^2\) and

\[E(MS_{Treatments})=\sigma^2+\frac{n\sum\tau_i^2}{a-1}\]

If \(H_0\) is true all \(\tau_i=0\) and both mean squares estimate \(\sigma^2\). The excess of \(MS_{Treatments}\) over \(\sigma^2\), measured in units of \(\sigma^2\) and multiplied by the numerator df, is

\[(a-1)\,\frac{E(MS_{Tr})-\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}=(a-1)\frac{n\sum\tau_i^2}{(a-1)\sigma^2}=\frac{n\sum\tau_i^2}{\sigma^2}=\delta\]

Dividing by \(a\) gives \(\Phi^2=\delta/a\). Numbers (means 575, 600, 650, 675; \(n=3\); \(\sigma=25\)): \(\delta=\dfrac{3(6250)}{625}=30\), \(\Phi^2=\dfrac{30}{4}=7.5\), \(\Phi=2.74\).

D3Minimum \(\Phi^2\) when two means differ by \(D\)▸

Suppose the largest gap is \(\tau_i-\tau_j=D\). Only the sum of squares matters, and the other \(\tau\)'s add non-negative terms, so the smallest case keeps them at 0. Use the identity

\[\tau_i^2+\tau_j^2=\frac{(\tau_i-\tau_j)^2+(\tau_i+\tau_j)^2}{2}\]

Since \((\tau_i+\tau_j)^2\ge0\), and \(\tau_i-\tau_j=D\):

\[\tau_i^2+\tau_j^2\ge\frac{D^2}{2}\]

Equality needs \(\tau_i+\tau_j=0\), i.e. \(\tau_i=D/2,\ \tau_j=-D/2\), which also keeps \(\sum\tau=0\). Hence \(\sum\tau_i^2\ge D^2/2\) and

\[\Phi^2=\frac{n\sum\tau_i^2}{a\sigma^2}\ge\frac{nD^2}{2a\sigma^2}\]

Etch: \(D=75\) gives \(\tau=\pm37.5\), \(\sum\tau_i^2=2812.5\), \(\Phi^2=\dfrac{n(2812.5)}{4(625)}=1.125\,n\).
