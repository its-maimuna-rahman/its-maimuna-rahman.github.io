Nuisance Factors and Blocking

Some variation in the response comes from things you do not care about, such as which batch of material was used or which part of a field a plot sits in. A nuisance factor is a factor that probably affects the response but whose effect is not the goal of the study.

If the source of this variation is known and can be controlled, we use blocking: group the experimental units so that units within a group are alike, then compare treatments inside each group. The nuisance effect is then taken out of the comparison instead of inflating the error.

Comparing a unit with baseline 10 against one with 21 mixes the 11-unit baseline gap into the “treatment” difference. Comparing 10 vs 11 (same pair) leaves a gap of only 1 plus the treatment effect. Blocking makes the second kind of comparison.

Hardness Testing: CRD vs RCBD

We want to know whether 4 tips give different readings on a hardness tester, with 4 observations per tip.

Completely randomized design (CRD). Assign the \(4\times4=16\) runs at random to 16 metal coupons. Problem: coupons may differ in hardness (for example, coupons cut from ingots made in different heats). That coupon-to-coupon variation lands in the experimental error, which becomes large and hides tip differences.

Randomized complete block design (RCBD). Use only 4 coupons and test every tip once on every coupon. Each coupon is a block. Inside a block the coupon is the same material for all four tips, so the tips are compared fairly; the order of the tips on a coupon is randomized.

What “Randomized Complete Block” Means

  • Block: a group of similar experimental units.
  • Complete: each block holds all the treatments, each once.
  • Randomized: in every block the treatments are assigned to units (or positions in time) at random, independently of other blocks.

Hardness layout with 4 coupons, each row is the run order inside a coupon:

How many layouts are possible? Each block is an independent shuffle of 4 treatments: \(4!=24\) orders. With 4 blocks, \(24^4=331{,}776\) equally likely layouts. In general \((a!)^b\). A CRD has \(16!/(4!)^4\) ways to split 16 runs into 4 groups of 4, but coupon quality can end up unevenly spread over tips; the RCBD makes that impossible by construction.

Because every block contains every treatment exactly once, the design is balanced: each treatment is observed \(b\) times and each block holds \(a\) runs, so \(N=ab\).

Statistical Model for the RCBD

With \(a\) treatments and \(b\) blocks there is one observation per treatment in each block, \(y_{ij}\) = response of treatment \(i\) in block \(j\).

\[ y_{ij}=\mu+\tau_i+\beta_j+\epsilon_{ij},\qquad i=1,\dots,a,\ \ j=1,\dots,b \]
  • \(\mu\): overall mean  ·  \(\tau_i\): effect of treatment \(i\)  ·  \(\beta_j\): effect of block \(j\)
  • \(\epsilon_{ij}\sim NID(0,\sigma^2)\): random error, independent and normal with common variance.

Effects are deviations from the overall mean, so they add to zero:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{a}\tau_i=0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{b}\beta_j=0 \]
Estimating the effects (hardness data). \(\hat\mu=\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}=154.0/16=9.625\).
\(\hat\tau_i=\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}\): \(9.575-9.625=-0.050\), \(9.600-9.625=-0.025\), \(9.450-9.625=-0.175\), \(9.875-9.625=+0.250\). Sum \(=0\).
\(\hat\beta_j=\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}\): \(-0.225,\ -0.200,\ +0.100,\ +0.325\). Sum \(=0\).
Fitted value, tip 1 on coupon 1: \(9.625-0.050-0.225=9.350\); observed \(9.3\), residual \(-0.05\). Tip 4 on coupon 4: \(9.625+0.250+0.325=10.200\); observed \(10.2\), residual \(0\).

The model is additive: the effect of a tip is assumed to be the same on every coupon (no tip×coupon interaction). With one observation per cell, the leftover \(y_{ij}-\hat y_{ij}\) is what we use as error.

Hypotheses in an RCBD

The question is about treatments. Blocks are included only to soak up nuisance variation.

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_a\qquad H_1:\ \mu_i\ne\mu_j\ \text{for at least one pair } i\ne j \]

Since the mean of treatment \(i\) is \(\mu_i=\mu+\tau_i\), the same hypotheses in terms of effects are

\[ H_0:\tau_1=\tau_2=\cdots=\tau_a=0\qquad H_1:\ \tau_i\ne0\ \text{for at least one } i \]
Hardness data. \(H_0:\) all four tips have the same mean reading, i.e. \(\tau_1=\tau_2=\tau_3=\tau_4=0\). The estimates \(-0.050,-0.025,-0.175,+0.250\) are not zero, but is that more than noise? The analysis of variance in the sections below answers that, and “Running the Test” applies it to these data.

Totals and Means

An RCBD has \(a\) treatments and \(b\) blocks, and every treatment appears once in every block. Let \(y_{ij}\) be the observation of treatment \(i\) in block \(j\). The number of observations is \(N=ab\). The data are summarized by a total for each treatment, each block, and everything together:

\[\yi=\sum_{j=1}^{b}y_{ij},\qquad \yj=\sum_{i=1}^{a}y_{ij},\qquad \yy=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{b}y_{ij}\]

The matching averages divide by the number of values that were added:

\[\ybi=\frac{\yi}{b},\qquad \ybj=\frac{\yj}{a},\qquad \ybb=\frac{\yy}{N}\]

Splitting the Total Sum of Squares

The total variation is the sum of squared gaps between each observation and the grand mean. In an RCBD this gap has three parts: how far the treatment mean is from the grand mean, how far the block mean is from the grand mean, and what is left over.

\[\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{b}(y_{ij}-\ybb)^2=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{b}\Big[(\ybi-\ybb)+(\ybj-\ybb)+(y_{ij}-\ybi-\ybj+\ybb)\Big]^2\]

After squaring, the three cross-product terms add up to zero, so only the three squared parts remain (every step is in derivation D3):

\[\SS{T}=\SS{Tr}+\SS{Bl}+\SS{E}\]
\[\SS{Tr}=b\sum_{i=1}^{a}(\ybi-\ybb)^2,\qquad \SS{Bl}=a\sum_{j=1}^{b}(\ybj-\ybb)^2,\qquad \SS{E}=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{b}(y_{ij}-\ybi-\ybj+\ybb)^2\]

The factor \(b\) appears because each treatment gap is shared by its \(b\) observations. The factor \(a\) appears for the same reason in blocks.

Example (\(\ybb=24\)).
\(\SS{T}\): row gaps squared are \(16+0+36+4=56\), \(1+9+16+4=30\), \(1+25+0+36=62\), so \(\SS{T}=148\).
\(\SS{Tr}=4[(21-24)^2+(24-24)^2+(27-24)^2]=4(9+0+9)=72\).
\(\SS{Bl}=3\big[(-\tfrac43)^2+(\tfrac83)^2+(-\tfrac{10}3)^2+2^2\big]=3\cdot\tfrac{16+64+100+36}{9}=3\cdot24=72\).
\(\SS{E}=148-72-72=4\).

Degrees of Freedom

Each sum of squares has its own degrees of freedom (df). The total has \(N-1\). Treatments have \(a-1\) and blocks have \(b-1\). Error gets whatever remains:

\[ab-1-(a-1)-(b-1)=(a-1)(b-1)\]
Example. \(a=3,\ b=4\): total \(=11\), treatments \(=2\), blocks \(=3\), error \(=2\times3=6\). Check: \(2+3+6=11\).

Mean Squares and What They Estimate

A mean square is a sum of squares divided by its df. When treatments and blocks are fixed, their expected values are

\[E(\MS{Tr})=\sigma^2+\frac{b\sum_{i=1}^{a}\tau_i^2}{a-1},\qquad E(\MS{Bl})=\sigma^2+\frac{a\sum_{j=1}^{b}\beta_j^2}{b-1},\qquad E(\MS{E})=\sigma^2\]

\(\MS{E}\) always estimates the error variance \(\sigma^2\). \(\MS{Tr}\) estimates \(\sigma^2\) only when every \(\tau_i=0\); any real treatment effect pushes it above \(\sigma^2\). Blocks work the same way with \(\beta_j\).

Example. \(\MS{Tr}=72/2=36\), \(\MS{Bl}=72/3=24\), \(\MS{E}=4/6=0.667\).
The estimated treatment effects are \(\ybi-\ybb=-3,\ 0,\ 3\), so the effect term is \(\dfrac{4(9+0+9)}{2}=36\), far above \(\hat\sigma^2=0.667\). The estimated block effects are \(-\tfrac43,\tfrac83,-\tfrac{10}3,2\), so \(\sum\hat\beta_j^2=24\) and the effect term is \(\dfrac{3(24)}{3}=24\).

F-tests for Treatments and Blocks

Are the treatments different?

The null hypothesis says all treatment means are equal (all \(\tau_i=0\)). Then \(\MS{Tr}\) and \(\MS{E}\) both estimate \(\sigma^2\), so their ratio should be near 1. A large ratio is evidence of a treatment effect.

\[F_0=\frac{\MS{Tr}}{\MS{E}}\sim F_{a-1,\,(a-1)(b-1)}\ \text{ if } H_0 \text{ is true};\qquad \text{reject } H_0 \text{ if } F_0\gt F_{\alpha,\,a-1,\,(a-1)(b-1)}\]

Do the blocks differ?

If block means barely differ, blocking may not be needed in later experiments, so it is worth checking.

\[H_0:\beta_1=\beta_2=\cdots=\beta_b=0,\qquad F_0=\frac{\MS{Bl}}{\MS{E}},\qquad \text{reject if } F_0\gt F_{\alpha,\,b-1,\,(a-1)(b-1)}\]
Example (\(\alpha=0.05\)).
Treatments: \(F_0=36/0.667=54\) against \(F_{0.05,2,6}=5.14\). Reject \(H_0\); the fertilizers differ.
Blocks: \(F_0=24/0.667=36\) against \(F_{0.05,3,6}=4.76\). Reject \(H_0\); the plots differ, so blocking was useful.

Computing Formulas

For hand calculation, the sums of squares are easier from totals. The term \(y_{\cdot\cdot}^2/N\) is the same in all of them (call it the correction term).

\[\SS{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sum_{j=1}^{b}y_{ij}^2-\frac{y_{\cdot\cdot}^2}{N},\qquad \SS{Tr}=\frac1b\sum_{i=1}^{a}y_{i\cdot}^2-\frac{y_{\cdot\cdot}^2}{N}\]
\[\SS{Bl}=\frac1a\sum_{j=1}^{b}y_{\cdot j}^2-\frac{y_{\cdot\cdot}^2}{N},\qquad \SS{E}=\SS{T}-\SS{Tr}-\SS{Bl}\]
Example. Correction term: \(288^2/12=82944/12=6912\).
\(\sum y_{ij}^2=(400+576+324+484)+(529+729+400+676)+(625+841+576+900)=1784+2334+2942=7060\), so \(\SS{T}=7060-6912=148\).
\(\sum y_{i\cdot}^2=84^2+96^2+108^2=7056+9216+11664=27936\), so \(\SS{Tr}=27936/4-6912=6984-6912=72\).
\(\sum y_{\cdot j}^2=68^2+80^2+62^2+78^2=4624+6400+3844+6084=20952\), so \(\SS{Bl}=20952/3-6912=6984-6912=72\).
\(\SS{E}=148-72-72=4\). Same answers as the definition formulas.

Running the Test on the Hardness Data

Now apply the formulas from the sections above (sums of squares, degrees of freedom, mean squares and the F-test) to the hardness data. Reject \(H_0\) if \(F_0\gt F_{\alpha,\,a-1,\,(a-1)(b-1)}\).

\(F_{0.05,3,9}=3.86\). Since \(14.44\gt3.86\) (\(P\approx0.001\)), reject \(H_0\): the tips do not all give the same mean reading. For blocks, \(F_0=0.2750/0.00889=30.94\gt3.86\), so the coupons differ as well and blocking was worthwhile.

Blocking shrank the error mean square from 0.0754 to 0.00889, about 8.5 times smaller, and turned a missed effect into a clear one. (Red bar: variation that the RCBD removes from the error. Dark bar: the leftover error, 0.080.)

Cost of blocking: it spends \(b-1\) df on blocks. Here error df fell from 12 to 9, which is a small price because the block variation was large. If blocks barely differ, the lost df can make the test slightly weaker.
