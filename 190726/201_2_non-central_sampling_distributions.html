Non-Central Chi-Squared, t and F Distributions

The central chi-squared, t and F curves describe a statistic when the null hypothesis is true. The moment the truth differs from the null, the statistic follows a shifted version of the same family. These shifted curves are the non-central distributions. They answer the question every study planner asks: if the effect is real and of a given size, how likely is the test to catch it?

1 · What non-centrality means

Each central distribution is built from standard normal variables \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) with mean zero. A non-central distribution uses the same construction, but the normal variables have a non-zero mean. Under a true null hypothesis the means are zero. Under an alternative they are not, and the size of the departure is measured by one number, the non-centrality parameter.

Reading the parameter The parameter is always the true effect measured in units of noise. It equals 0 when the null is true, and it grows as the effect grows or as the sample grows. A non-centrality of 0 gives back the central distribution exactly.

Three situations show how the parameter is read from a problem statement. For a mean, it is the true shift divided by the standard error. For several group means, it is the spread of the true means, summed over all observations, divided by \(\sigma^2\). For category counts, it is the sample size times the distance between the true shares and the null shares. The three scenarios below use one example of each.

2 · The non-central chi-squared distribution

Let \(Z_1,\dots,Z_k\) be independent standard normals and let \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k\) be fixed numbers. Then

\[ V=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(Z_i+\mu_i\right)^2\sim\chi^2_k(\lambda),\qquad \lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_i^2 . \]

The distribution depends on the means only through \(\lambda\). Its density is a Poisson-weighted blend of central chi-squared curves. Choose \(J\) from a Poisson law with mean \(\lambda/2\), then use a central chi-squared with \(k+2J\) degrees of freedom:

\[ f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\lambda/2}\left(\lambda/2\right)^{j}}{j!}\;f_{\chi^2_{k+2j}}(x),\qquad x\gt 0 . \]

Pushing the means away from zero moves the curve to the right, widens it and makes it more symmetric. Independent terms add both parts: \(\chi^2_{a}(\lambda_1)+\chi^2_{b}(\lambda_2)\sim\chi^2_{a+b}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\).

Operating on counts: the call centre

A call centre expects its calls to fall equally in six time blocks, each with share \(p_{0}=1/6\). The true shares are \(0.20,\,0.20,\,0.15,\,0.15,\,0.15,\,0.15\). For \(n\) calls the Pearson statistic is approximately \(\chi^2_{5}(\lambda)\) with

\[ \lambda=n\sum_{i=1}^{6}\frac{\left(p_i-p_0\right)^2}{p_0}. \]
Distance. \(\sum (p_i-p_0)^2/p_0=6\left[\,2(0.0333)^2+4(0.0167)^2\right]=0.02\).
Non-centrality. For \(n=300\): \(\lambda=300\times0.02=6\).
Decision line. The 5% critical value of \(\chi^2_5\) is \(11.070\), taken from the central distribution because the line is set under the null.
Power \(=P\!\left(\chi^2_5(6)\gt 11.070\right)\approx 0.433\). The test misses this real pattern more than half the time. Raising \(n\) to about 650 gives \(\lambda=13\) and power near 0.81.

3 · The non-central t distribution

Let \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(V\sim\chi^2_\nu\) be independent, and let \(\delta\) be a fixed real number. Then

\[ T'=\frac{Z+\delta}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}\sim t_\nu(\delta),\qquad f(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{v}{\nu}}\;\varphi\!\left(t\sqrt{\frac{v}{\nu}}-\delta\right)f_{\chi^2_\nu}(v)\,dv, \]

where \(\varphi\) is the standard normal density. Given the denominator, the statistic is normal with mean \(\delta\) divided by the denominator. The integral averages over all possible denominators. The curve is shifted toward the sign of \(\delta\) and is no longer symmetric: the tail on the side away from \(\delta\) is thin, and the tail on the near side is long.

Operating on a mean: the rice trial

A researcher tests \(H_0:\mu=4.5\) tonnes per hectare on \(n=10\) plots. The true mean is 4.8 and \(\sigma=0.6\). The true shift in standard errors is

\[ \delta=\frac{\sqrt n\,(\mu-\mu_0)}{\sigma}=\frac{\sqrt{10}\times 0.3}{0.6}\approx 1.581 . \]

The statistic \((\bar X-\mu_0)/(S/\sqrt n)\) follows \(t_9(1.581)\). The two-sided 5% limits are \(\pm 2.262\), taken from the central \(t_9\).

Power \(=P\!\left(|T'|\gt 2.262\right)\approx 0.293\). The trial finds the real gain in only about three attempts out of ten. Since \(\delta=0.5\sqrt n\) here, reaching 0.80 power needs \(n=34\), where \(\delta\approx 2.92\).

4 · The non-central F distribution

Let \(U\sim\chi^2_{d_1}(\lambda)\) be non-central and \(V\sim\chi^2_{d_2}\) be central and independent of \(U\). Then

\[ F'=\frac{U/d_1}{V/d_2}\sim F_{d_1,d_2}(\lambda),\qquad f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\lambda/2}\left(\lambda/2\right)^{j}}{j!}\;\frac{1}{r_j}\,f_{F_{d_1+2j,\,d_2}}\!\left(\frac{x}{r_j}\right),\quad r_j=\frac{d_1+2j}{d_1}. \]

Only the numerator is shifted. The denominator still estimates \(\sigma^2\) alone, so a real effect makes the ratio larger and pushes the curve to the right, away from the central F curve that sets the critical value.

Operating on several means: the fertiliser trial

Three fertilisers have true mean yields 4.5, 4.8 and 5.1 with \(\sigma=0.6\), and each gets 10 plots. The overall mean is 4.8, so

\[ \lambda=\frac{\sum_i n_i\left(\mu_i-\bar\mu\right)^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{10\left[(-0.3)^2+0^2+0.3^2\right]}{0.36}=5 . \]

The statistic is \(F_{2,27}(5)\) and the 5% line from the central \(F_{2,27}\) is \(3.354\).

Power \(=P\!\left(F_{2,27}(5)\gt 3.354\right)\approx 0.458\). With 21 plots per fertiliser, \(\lambda=10.5\), the degrees of freedom become \((2,60)\) and the power rises to about 0.81.

Check with the rice trial The rice test has \(\delta=1.581\), so \(\delta^2=2.5\). The equivalent F form gives \(F_{1,9}(2.5)\) and the same power, because \(|T'|\gt 2.262\) is the same event as \(T'^{\,2}\gt 5.117\). The non-central t is two-sided by construction, and the non-central F with 1 numerator degree of freedom is the same test written with squares.

The mean of \(t_\nu(\delta)\) is \(\delta\) multiplied by a factor slightly above 1 that shrinks to 1 as \(\nu\) grows. For \(\nu=12\) and \(\delta=1.5\) the mean is 1.603, not 1.5. The estimated \(S\) is occasionally small, and this pulls the ratio outward.

6 · Using them: power and sample size

Every power calculation has the same three steps. Find the critical line from the central distribution, because it must hold when the null is true. Work out the non-centrality from the effect you want to detect. Then read the probability beyond the line from the non-central curve. Increasing the sample raises the non-centrality and also tightens the degrees of freedom, and both help.

Counts

\(\chi^2_{k}(\lambda)\) with \(\lambda=n\sum (p_i-p_{0i})^2/p_{0i}\). The call centre needs about 650 calls for power 0.8.

One or two means

\(t_\nu(\delta)\) with \(\delta\) the true shift in standard errors. The rice trial needs 34 plots for power 0.8.

Several means

\(F_{d_1,d_2}(\lambda)\) with \(\lambda\) the spread of true means over \(\sigma^2\). The fertiliser trial needs 21 plots per group for power 0.8.

Planning values The non-centrality uses a guessed effect and a guessed \(\sigma\), taken from earlier studies or a pilot. A power figure is only as good as these guesses, so check it over a range of plausible values.
