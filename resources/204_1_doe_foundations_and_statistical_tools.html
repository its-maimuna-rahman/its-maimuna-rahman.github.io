Big Picture

A designed experiment has two sides. The design side (Lectures 1–2) tells us how to collect data fairly: what to change, what to measure, how to assign units, and how to plan. The statistics side (Lectures 5–6) gives the tools (sampling distributions, \(\chi^2\), \(t\), \(F\), hypothesis tests) that we use later in ANOVA.

What Is an Experiment?

An experiment is a test or series of tests in which we make purposeful changes to the input variables of a process or system, so that we can observe and identify the reasons for changes in the output response. Experiments are commonly done to study the effect of one or more variables (factors) on another variable (the response).

Why run an experiment?

  • To find the principal causes of variation in a measured response.
  • To find the conditions that give a maximum or minimum response.
  • To compare the responses at different settings of controllable variables.
  • To get a mathematical model that predicts future responses.

Design of experiments (DOE)

DOE is a structured and organised way to conduct and analyse controlled tests for evaluating the factors that affect response variables. It was pioneered by Ronald A. Fisher in the 1920s and early 1930s at Rothamsted Experimental Station, an agricultural research station 25 miles north of London. DOE has a long tradition in statistics, engineering and life sciences.

In most practical problems many variables influence the outcome, and they usually interact in complex ways. A good design lets us estimate and interpret these interactions.

Objectives of DOE

  • Identify the factors that (i) affect the average response, (ii) affect the variability, (iii) do not contribute significantly.
  • Identify the mathematical model relating the response to the factors.
  • Identify the "optimum" settings for the factors.

Three Basic Principles of DOE

Replication
Blocking
Randomization

Replication

Replication means repetition of the treatments under investigation.

Replication

Four subjects are each assigned to a drug and one measurement is taken on each subject. The result is four independent observations on the drug.

Repeated measurements

One subject is assigned to the drug and measured four times. The measurements are not independent, so we call them repeated measurements, not replicates.

Blocking

A block is a group of experimental units that are internally homogeneous with respect to the characteristic affecting the response and externally heterogeneous. To block an experiment we partition the observations into groups (blocks) so that the observations in each block are collected under relatively similar conditions. If blocking is done well, comparisons of two or more treatments are made more precisely than in an unblocked design.

Example: soil type (sandy or clay) is a block in a fertilizer experiment.

Randomization

Randomization means distributing the treatments to the experimental units purely by a chance mechanism, so that any unit is equally likely to receive any treatment. Its purpose is to prevent systematic and personal biases from being introduced into the experiment by the experimenter. (See the interactive at the top.)

Planning an Experiment

The checklist below summarises a very large number of decisions. The steps are not independent: at any stage we may need to go back and revise an earlier decision.

  1. Objectives: define the objectives of the experiment.
  2. Sources of variation: identify them all, including (i) treatment factors and their levels, (ii) experimental units, (iii) blocking factors, noise factors and covariates.
  3. Assignment rule: choose a rule for assigning the experimental units to the treatments.
  4. Measurements and procedure: specify the measurements, the experimental procedure and the anticipated difficulties.
  5. Pilot: run a pilot experiment.
  6. Model: specify the model.
  7. Analysis: outline the analysis.
  8. Sample size: calculate the number of observations that need to be taken.
  9. Review: review the above decisions and revise if necessary.
Three kinds of extra variables. A blocking factor is a known source of variation that we group the units by (soil type). A noise factor is a variable that affects the response but is not controlled (temperature, humidity). A covariate is a measured numerical variable (such as initial height) that can be used to adjust the comparison.

Two worked examples

Does Fertilizer B give a higher mean tomato yield than Fertilizer A?Does a new antihypertensive drug lower systolic blood pressure more than the standard drug?

Population & Sampling Distributions

Can we find the sampling distribution of the sample variance in the same way? Only by computing it for every possible sample, which is usually impossible. So we use known theoretical distributions.

  • Normal population: \(\bar x\) is exactly normal for every \(n\), and it gets narrower as \(n\) grows.
  • Non-normal population: \(\bar x\) is only approximately normal, and the approximation gets better for large \(n\) (central limit theorem).

Under certain assumptions the sampling distributions of common statistics are known theoretically. They form the basis of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. The three most used are the chi-square, Student's t and F distributions.

Chi-square, t and F

Chi-square \((\chi^2)\)

\(Z_1,\dots,Z_n\) independent standard normal \(\Rightarrow\)

\[ \chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_i^2 \]

has \(n\) degrees of freedom. For a normal sample,

\[ \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1} \]

Student's \(t\)

\(Z\sim N(0,1)\), \(U\sim\chi^2_n\), independent \(\Rightarrow\)

\[ T=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{U/n}}\sim t_n \]

Putting \(Z=\dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\) and \(U=\dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\) gives

\[ t=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1} \]

F distribution

\(U_1\sim\chi^2_{n_1}\) and \(U_2\sim\chi^2_{n_2}\) independent \(\Rightarrow\) \(F=\dfrac{U_1/n_1}{U_2/n_2}\sim F_{n_1,n_2}\) (numerator df \(n_1\), denominator df \(n_2\)). For independent samples from two normal populations,

\[ F=\frac{S_1^2/\sigma_1^2}{S_2^2/\sigma_2^2}\sim F_{n_1-1,\,n_2-1} \]

If \(\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2\), then \(F=S_1^2/S_2^2\), the familiar F-test for comparing two variances. Because it is a ratio of two independent estimates of the same variance, it is called the variance ratio.

Why these matter in DOE: they describe the distribution of sample statistics, so they give confidence intervals and tests (t-tests, \(\chi^2\) tests, F-tests). In particular, F is the foundation of ANOVA, the main method for comparing treatment means.

Hypothesis Testing

A hypothesis is a statement about one or more populations. The research hypothesis is the conjecture that motivates the research. In a test we check a statement about the population using a sample from that population.

  • The null hypothesis \(H_0\) is a claim about a population parameter that is assumed true until it is declared false.
  • The alternative hypothesis \(H_1\) is the claim that will be true if \(H_0\) is false.

Like a court case, a test has four possible outcomes. The court assumes the person is not guilty (\(H_0\)) until the evidence says otherwise.

Tails of a test

A two-tailed test has rejection regions in both tails, a left-tailed test in the left tail, a right-tailed test in the right tail. The key phrase in the question tells us which one to use.

TypeKey phraseExample (population assumed normal)Hypotheses

\(H_1:\mu\neq18.4\)

For the left-tailed test we may also write \(H_0:\mu\ge12\). It does not change the result as long as \(H_1\) has the "less than" sign.

Testing \(\mu\) when \(\sigma\) is known

Two cases: Case I, the population is normal; Case II, the population is not normal but \(n\) is large. In both cases \(\bar x\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\), so the test statistic is

\[ Z=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \]

Steps: (1) state \(H_0\) and \(H_1\); (2) compute \(Z\); (3) get the critical value(s) from the Z-table using \(\alpha\); (4) compare; (5) state the conclusion in words. Critical values at \(\alpha=0.05\):

Two-tailedRight-tailedLeft-tailed
\(\pm1.96\) (\(\alpha/2=0.025\) in each tail)\(+1.65\)\(-1.65\)
Customer spending. A retailer claims the mean monthly spending of loyalty-card customers is $500. A random sample of \(n=10\) customers has \(\bar x=470\). Spending is approximately normal and the population variance is known, \(\sigma^2=4000\). This is Case I (normal population, \(\sigma\) known).
\(Z=\dfrac{470-500}{63.25/\sqrt{10}}=-1.50\).
Two-tailed (\(H_1:\mu\ne500\)): \(-1.50\) lies between \(-1.96\) and \(1.96\), so do not reject \(H_0\); no evidence that the mean differs from $500.
Right-tailed (\(H_1:\mu\gt500\)): \(-1.50\lt1.65\), do not reject \(H_0\).
Left-tailed (\(H_1:\mu\lt500\)): \(-1.50\gt-1.65\), do not reject \(H_0\).
Customer spending example, step by step

Given \(\bar x=470\), \(\mu_0=500\), \(n=10\), \(\sigma^2=4000\). First the standard deviation:

\[\sigma=\sqrt{4000}=63.25\]

Standard error of the mean:

\[\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=\frac{63.25}{\sqrt{10}}=\frac{63.25}{3.162}=20.00\]

Test statistic:

\[Z=\frac{470-500}{20.00}=-1.50\]

Compare with the critical values at \(\alpha=0.05\):

\[\text{two-tailed: }\ -1.96\le-1.50\le1.96\ \Rightarrow\ \text{do not reject }H_0\] \[\text{right-tailed: }\ -1.50\le1.65\ \Rightarrow\ \text{do not reject }H_0\] \[\text{left-tailed: }\ -1.50\ge-1.65\ \Rightarrow\ \text{do not reject }H_0\]

"Do not reject \(H_0\)" means there is not enough evidence against it; it does not prove \(H_0\) true.
