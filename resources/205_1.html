Demography & Demographic Data

Every year Bangladesh records roughly 3 million births and 950,000 deaths; the United States records about 3.6 million births and 3.4 million deaths. How do the two countries know these numbers, where do the figures live, and why does the difference in their data machinery matter? This guide follows two families through every major source — census, survey, register — and works through the arithmetic behind the estimates.

1 · Two families, two data systems

On the same morning in March, a baby is born to the Rahman family in Cumilla, Bangladesh, and another to the Miller family in Columbus, Ohio. Both countries will eventually know about these births. But the machinery differs sharply, and the difference shows up in the quality of every statistic the two governments publish.

The Rahman baby. The birth is registered locally under the Birth and Death Registration Act 2004 and entered into the Birth and Death Registration Information System (BDRIS), which feeds the national identity database. The legal record exists. But in 2025, only about 63% of births in Bangladesh were registered at all, and only 9% within the statutory 45-day window. So the register cannot, on its own, tell the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) how many babies were born this year.
Filling the gap with SVRS. BBS runs the Sample Vital Registration System (SVRS): a continuous survey of about 1,000 primary sampling units spread across the country, tracking roughly 250,000 households. SVRS records births, deaths, marriages, migration, disability and contraceptive use every year. It estimates that Bangladesh's crude birth rate is about 18 per 1,000 and crude death rate about 5.5 per 1,000 — which, applied to a population of about 170 million, implies roughly 3.06 million births and 935,000 deaths a year. Those are the numbers the government actually uses.
The Miller baby. The birth is certified on a U.S. Standard Certificate of Live Birth at the hospital. The Ohio Department of Health transmits the record to the CDC's National Vital Statistics System (NVSS), which compiles official birth and death statistics for all 50 states and the District of Columbia. U.S. births in 2022 numbered about 3.66 million; deaths about 3.28 million. Every one of those records is a legal certificate, not a sample estimate.
The census underneath both. Every ten years, both countries enumerate everyone. Bangladesh's Population and Housing Census 2022 counted about 165 million people (later adjusted to roughly 170 million after post-enumeration checks). The U.S. Decennial Census in 2020 counted about 331 million. The census gives the denominator — the total population — that every rate needs.
The Rahman baby is known through a sample survey because the civil register is incomplete. The Miller baby is known through a complete civil registration system. Both are re-counted ten years later by a census. Same event, different machinery, different statistical consequences.
The cost gap in one comparison The U.S. 2020 Census cost about $13.6 billion to count 331 million people — roughly $41 per person. Bangladesh's 2022 Census cost about $140 million to count 165 million — roughly $0.85 per person. The difference reflects labour costs, infrastructure, and how much follow-up enumeration each country can afford. The cheaper census is not worse; it is calibrated to a different budget and a different labour market.

2 · What demography studies

Demography The scientific study of human populations — their size, composition (by age, sex and other characteristics) and change over time through births, deaths and migration. Three questions organise the field: how many people live here, who are they, and how are those numbers changing?
Population size
The total count in a place at a point in time. Censuses are the gold standard: Bangladesh's 2022 Census recorded 165.16 million people; the U.S. 2020 Census recorded 331.45 million.
Composition
Age, sex, household structure, education, ethnicity. Surveys provide this in detail: the BDHS samples roughly 17,000–20,000 households; the ACS samples about 3.5 million addresses a year.
Components of change
Births, deaths, migration. Registers and vital statistics track these: NVSS records about 3.66 million U.S. births a year; SVRS estimates Bangladesh's 3.06 million.
The demographic balancing equation \[\text{Population}_{\text{end}} = \text{Population}_{\text{start}} + \text{Births} - \text{Deaths} + \text{Net Migration}\] With Bangladesh-style numbers — start 170 million, births 3.06 million, deaths 0.935 million, net migration −0.5 million — the end population is 171.625 million. Every data source in this chapter exists to measure one of these terms.

3 · The three tools and their trade-offs

Counting a whole country is not one problem but three, and each has a different tool. The trade-off is between coverage (how many people the tool reaches) and detail (how much it asks each person).

  1. A census reaches everyone, every ten years, and asks a short set of questions. Its strength is exhaustive coverage; its weakness is infrequency and shallowness.
  2. A survey reaches a representative sample, can run every year, and asks many detailed questions. Its strength is depth and frequency; its weakness is sampling error and the risk of bias in hard-to-reach groups.
  3. A register records events continuously as they happen, but only for people who register. Its strength is continuity and legal standing; its weakness is incomplete coverage where registration incentives or infrastructure are weak.

No single tool does all three jobs. Demographers combine them: census for the denominator, survey for the rates and characteristics, register for the event counts.

Why sample size matters more than population size

The standard error of a sample proportion \(\hat p\) is

\[\text{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\times\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}},\]

where \(n\) is the sample size and \(N\) the population size. For \(n\) much smaller than \(N\), the second factor is nearly 1, so precision depends almost entirely on \(n\).

Bangladesh. Suppose SVRS surveys \(n=250{,}000\) households and estimates a proportion \(p=0.5\). Then \[\text{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{250{,}000}}\approx0.0010,\qquad\text{95% margin of error}\approx\pm0.20\text{ percentage points.}\]
United States. The ACS surveys about 3.5 million addresses. With \(p=0.5\), \[\text{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{3{,}500{,}000}}\approx0.00027,\qquad\text{95% margin of error}\approx\pm0.053\text{ percentage points.}\]

Fourteen times the sample buys about four times the precision — the square-root law. A census is still needed for exact small-area counts, but for national rates the sample does the heavy lifting.

Primary vs secondary Primary data are collected and published by the organisation that gathered them: a census, a survey, a vital registration system. Secondary data are compilations or derived estimates built on primary data: a UN World Population Prospects volume, a World Bank indicator, a published analytical report. The distinction is about who produced the number, not about reliability.

5 · Primary sources in detail

5.1 The population census

A census is a complete enumeration of every person in a country at a single point in time, usually every ten years. It is the only source that gives counts for every small area — every district, every neighbourhood — and it creates the sample frame from which surveys draw their samples.

Bangladesh 2022. Conducted by BBS in June 2022 using digital tablets and a geospatial enumeration architecture. About 370,000 enumerators worked for about ten days. The preliminary count of 165.16 million was published within a month; the post-enumeration check adjusted the total to about 170 million. The census also collected housing, household amenities and disability data.
United States 2020. Conducted by the Census Bureau with online, mail and in-person response modes. About 500,000 temporary workers were employed over the enumeration period. The count of 331.45 million determined the apportionment of 435 House seats and guided the distribution of about $1.5 trillion a year in federal funds. Cost: about $13.6 billion, or roughly $41 per person.
What a census cannot do. It is expensive, infrequent, and asks few questions. That is exactly why surveys exist.

5.2 The sample survey

A survey collects data from a representative sample and generalises to the whole population. It can be frequent, detailed and flexible in a way a census cannot.

Bangladesh: SVRS. The Sample Vital Registration System began in 1980 with 150 primary sampling units and 250 households. It now covers about 1,000 PSUs and 250,000 households, recording births, deaths, marriages, migration, disability and contraceptive use. SVRS is the main source of annual fertility and mortality estimates between censuses. It reports crude birth rate around 18/1,000 and crude death rate around 5.5/1,000.
Bangladesh: BDHS, MICS, BMMS. The Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey, conducted by NIPORT with ICF International, samples roughly 17,000–20,000 households per round and covers health, fertility, family planning and child wellbeing. MICS focuses on child indicators. BMMS measures maternal mortality. Together these have produced more than 25 national health and demographic surveys in two decades.
United States: ACS. The American Community Survey contacts about 3.5 million addresses a year and produces annual estimates of social, economic, housing and demographic characteristics for every community. It replaced the long-form census questionnaire after 2000. One-year estimates are available for areas of 65,000+ people; five-year estimates for smaller areas. The response rate is around 85–90%.
United States: CPS and SIPP. The Current Population Survey samples about 60,000 households a month and produces the monthly unemployment rate. The Survey of Income and Program Participation follows the same households for several years to track income and programme participation over time.

5.3 The register (civil registration and vital statistics)

A register records vital events continuously as they occur and produces vital statistics at annual or monthly intervals. In principle it is the best source: complete, continuous, legally grounded. In practice it is only as good as its coverage.

Bangladesh. Civil registration is governed by the Birth and Death Registration Act 2004, administered through local government registrars, and entered into BDRIS linked to the national identity system. In 2025, birth registration reached about 63% and death registration about 59%, with a persistent gender gap in death registration (64% male, 36% female). Only about 9% of births are registered within the statutory 45-day window. Because of these gaps, BBS uses SVRS to produce official vital statistics.
United States. The National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) is a federal–state partnership. Hospitals and funeral directors complete standard certificates; state registrars transmit records; the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics compiles the national files. All 50 states and D.C. participate in the Vital Statistics Cooperative Program. Revised standard certificates were fully implemented for births by 2016 and for deaths by 2018. Birth records are about 3.66 million a year; death records about 3.28 million.
Why the coverage gap matters The U.S. register is the primary source of vital statistics. Bangladesh's register is still supplemented by a sample system. That single difference shapes every demographic estimate either country publishes: one country's rates are grounded in a complete count, the other's in a carefully designed sample.

7 · Why demography matters in both countries

Bangladesh
The demographic dividend. About 46 million people aged 15–29 — roughly 27% of the population — are in the working-age window. Whether this becomes a dividend or a missed opportunity depends on skills, jobs and health investment. The window closes as fertility declines: the total fertility rate has fallen from about 6.0 in the 1970s to roughly 2.0 in 2022.
United States
Population ageing. About 10,000 Americans turn 65 every day, and combined Social Security and Medicare spending is projected to rise from roughly 9% of GDP in 2020 to more than 11% by 2036. The share of the population aged 65+ is about 17.3%; in Bangladesh it is about 5.5%. The gap is the whole fiscal story.
Bangladesh
Health and family planning. SVRS and BDHS data drive maternal health, immunisation and family-planning programmes. Fertility decline is measured, not assumed: the TFR of about 2.0 is a survey-based estimate, and the contraceptive prevalence rate of about 64% is tracked annually.
United States
Resource allocation. ACS estimates support over 300 evidence-based federal uses and guide the distribution of billions in federal funds. The Decennial Census itself determines the apportionment of the House of Representatives.
What the two countries share Bangladesh uses demography to capture a dividend before the window closes; the United States uses it to manage the fiscal consequences of ageing. Both need a reliable census, regular surveys and credible vital statistics — and both are limited by the weakest link in their own chain. For Bangladesh that link is civil registration completeness; for the United States it is the cost and complexity of very large surveys and the gaps for small or hard-to-reach populations.

10 · Conceptual derivations and worked numbers

Derivation 1 · The demographic balancing equation, with numbers

A population at time \(t+1\) is the population at time \(t\), plus everyone who enters (births and immigrants), minus everyone who leaves (deaths and emigrants):

\[P_{t+1}=P_{t}+B-D+IM-OM=P_{t}+B-D+NM,\]

where \(NM=IM-OM\) is net migration. Rearranging gives the growth rate:

\[\frac{P_{t+1}-P_{t}}{P_{t}}=\frac{B-D+NM}{P_{t}}.\]

Worked example (Bangladesh-style figures). Start with \(P=170{,}000{,}000\), \(B=3{,}060{,}000\), \(D=935{,}000\), \(NM=-500{,}000\):

\[P_{t+1}=170{,}000{,}000+3{,}060{,}000-935{,}000-500{,}000=171{,}625{,}000.\] \[\text{Growth rate}=\frac{1{,}625{,}000}{170{,}000{,}000}=0.00956\approx0.96\%\text{ per year.}\]

Every demographic data source measures one or more of these terms: censuses measure \(P\), registers measure \(B\) and \(D\), surveys estimate all of them with sampling error.

Derivation 2 · Why a census is needed even when a register exists

A register records events, not stocks. To turn a count of births into a birth rate you need the denominator — the mid-year population. The census supplies that denominator, and also supplies the sample frame that makes surveys representative. Without a census, neither register-based rates nor sample-based estimates can be trusted.

Numerical illustration. Suppose a register records 60,000 births in a district. Without a census count of the district's population you cannot say whether that is a high or a low fertility rate. If the district has 1.5 million people, the CBR is 40 per 1,000 — high. If it has 3 million, the CBR is 20 per 1,000 — moderate. The register alone tells you the numerator; only the census supplies the denominator.

Derivation 3 · Sample precision, with numbers

A survey observes a sample of size \(n\) from a population of size \(N\). The estimate \(\hat p\) of a proportion has standard error

\[\text{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\times\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}}\ \ \text{(finite population correction)}.\]

For large \(N\) the second factor is close to 1, so precision depends mainly on \(n\).

Numerical example. For \(p=0.5\) and \(n=17{,}000\) (BDHS):

\[\text{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{17{,}000}}\approx0.00383,\qquad95\%\text{ CI}\approx\pm0.75\text{ pp.}\]

For \(n=250{,}000\) (SVRS):

\[\text{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{0.25}{250{,}000}}\approx0.0010,\qquad95\%\text{ CI}\approx\pm0.20\text{ pp.}\]

To halve the margin of error you need four times the sample — the square-root law.

Derivation 4 · Dual-record correction (why SVRS exists)

If the civil register captures only a fraction \(c\) of events, the register count \(R\) understates the true number \(T\) by the factor \(c\): \(T=R/c\). Without an independent estimate of \(c\) the register cannot be corrected. SVRS provides that estimate through a dual-record system (capture–recapture):

\[T=\frac{R\times E}{M},\]

where \(E\) is the count from the independent enumeration and \(M\) is the number found in both.

Numerical example. Suppose the register records \(R=60{,}000\) births, the SVRS enumeration finds \(E=65{,}000\) births in the same area, and \(M=55{,}000\) births are found in both:

\[T=\frac{60{,}000\times65{,}000}{55{,}000}=70{,}909.\]

Estimated register completeness \(c=R/T=60{,}000/70{,}909\approx84.6\%\). The register's raw 60,000 is therefore adjusted upward to about 70,900. That is why BBS uses SVRS, not the raw register, to produce official vital statistics between censuses.

Derivation 5 · From counts to rates — the standard toolkit \[CBR=\frac{B}{P}\times1000,\qquad CDR=\frac{D}{P}\times1000,\qquad RNI=\frac{B-D}{P}\times100.\]

Each rate needs a numerator from a register or survey and a denominator from a census or population estimate. The whole architecture of demographic data exists to supply these two pieces consistently.

Worked example. For Bangladesh-style figures — \(B=3{,}060{,}000\), \(D=935{,}000\), \(P=170{,}000{,}000\):

\[CBR=\frac{3{,}060{,}000}{170{,}000{,}000}\times1000\approx18.0,\quad CDR=\frac{935{,}000}{170{,}000{,}000}\times1000\approx5.5,\] \[RNI=\frac{3{,}060{,}000-935{,}000}{170{,}000{,}000}\times100\approx1.25\%.\]

These are the figures the government publishes as the country's annual demographic profile.
