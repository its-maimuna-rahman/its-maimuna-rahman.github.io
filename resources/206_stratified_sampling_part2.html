Stratified Sampling: Allocation

StRS Part 2

How many units should each stratum get? Equal, proportional, Neyman and cost-based rules, each with a worked question and answer.

Allocation lab

Type your own strata. See how the three basic rules split the sample, and how much variance each one leaves.

Quick recap of symbols

Sizes

  • \(k\) strata, stratum \(i\) has \(N_i\) units.
  • You pick \(n_i\) from it.
  • Totals add up to \(N\) and \(n\).
\[N=\sum_{i=1}^{k}N_i\]
\[n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_i\]

Shares

  • Weight = share of the population in the stratum.
  • Sampling fraction = share of the stratum you actually sample.
\[w_i=\frac{N_i}{N}\]
\[f_i=\frac{n_i}{N_i}\]

The estimator we are trying to make precise

Average the stratum means, but weigh each by how big its stratum is.

\[\bar y_{st}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i\bar y_i\]
Question. Strata sizes are \(500,300,200\) and the sample means are \(40,55,70\). Find \(\bar y_{st}\).
Solution. Weights first:
\[w=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2)\]
\[\bar y_{st}=0.5(40)+0.3(55)+0.2(70)\]
\[\bar y_{st}=20+16.5+14=50.5\]

Variance when sampling with replacement

If every stratum is sampled with replacement, a unit can be picked twice, so there is no finite population correction. Each stratum mean has variance \(S_i^2/n_i\) (a close approximation when \(N_i\) is large).

\[\V(\yb_i)\approx\frac{S_i^2}{n_i}\]

Strata are sampled independently, so variances simply add after squaring the weights.

\[\V(\yb_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{w_i^2S_i^2}{n_i}\]

Real data gives \(s_i^2\) instead of \(S_i^2\), so we estimate:

\[\vh(\yb_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{w_i^2s_i^2}{n_i}\]
Why this form matters. Every allocation rule below minimises exactly this sum. It is also a good approximation for without-replacement sampling when the sampling fractions are small.
Question. Two strata with \(w=(0.6,0.4)\), sample sizes \(n=(60,40)\) and sample variances \(s^2=(100,64)\). Find the estimated variance and the standard error.
Solution. Compute each stratum's contribution:
\[\frac{0.6^2(100)}{60}=0.600\]
\[\frac{0.4^2(64)}{40}=0.256\]
Add them:
\[\vh(\yb_{st})=0.600+0.256=0.856\]
\[SE=\sqrt{0.856}\approx0.925\]

The allocation problem

You have a total of \(n\) units to hand out. Many splits \((n_1,\dots,n_k)\) add up to \(n\), but they do not all give the same precision.

The tension

  • More units in a stratum means lower variance.
  • More units also means higher cost.

Two questions to answer

  • Need a target precision: what is the cheapest split?
  • Have a fixed budget: what split gives the best precision?

The rules you will learn

RuleUsesIdea
Equalnumber of stratasame for everyone
Proportionalstratum sizesbigger stratum, bigger share
Neymansizes and SDsbigger or more variable, bigger share
Cost-optimumsizes, SDs, costsalso give less to expensive strata

Equal allocation

\[n_i=\frac{n}{k}\]

Use it when

  • all strata are equally important;
  • you know nothing about the variances;
  • each group must be represented equally.

Watch out

  • It ignores size and variability.
  • So it is usually not the lowest-variance choice.
Question. A survey has \(n=600\) and \(k=6\) strata. How many per stratum?
Solution.
\[n_i=\frac{600}{6}=100\]
Question. \(n=200\), \(k=3\). Give whole-number sizes.
Solution.
\[\frac{200}{3}=66.67\]
Round and fix the total: \((67,67,66)\), which adds to 200.

Proportional allocation

Give each stratum the same sampling fraction. A stratum holding 20% of the population gets 20% of the sample.

\[n_i=n\,w_i=n\,\frac{N_i}{N}\]
Question. \(N=(500,300,200)\), \(n=200\). Find \(n_i\) and check the sampling fractions.
Solution.
\[n_1=200(0.5)=100\]
\[n_2=200(0.3)=60\]
\[n_3=200(0.2)=40\]
Fractions: \(100/500=60/300=40/200=0.20\). All equal, as promised.

Its variance

Put \(n_i=nw_i\) into \(\sum w_i^2S_i^2/n_i\). One \(w_i\) cancels:

\[V_{prop}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i^2\]

Read it as: (population-weighted average of the stratum variances) divided by \(n\).

Question. \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), \(S=(10,20,30)\), \(n=200\). Find \(V_{prop}\).
Solution. Square the SDs: \(100,400,900\).
\[\sum w_iS_i^2=50+120+180=350\]
\[V_{prop}=\frac{350}{200}=1.75\]

Neyman (optimum) allocation

For a fixed \(n\), this split gives the smallest possible variance. A stratum earns more units if it is bigger or more variable.

\[n_i=n\,\frac{w_iS_i}{\sum_{j}w_jS_j}\]

Since \(w_i=N_i/N\), the factor \(N\) cancels, so the same rule can be written with sizes:

\[n_i=n\,\frac{N_iS_i}{\sum_{j}N_jS_j}\]
Question. \(N=(500,300,200)\), \(S=(10,20,30)\), \(n=200\). Find the Neyman sizes.
Solution. Score each stratum by \(N_iS_i\):
\[N_1S_1=5000\]
\[N_2S_2=6000\]
\[N_3S_3=6000\]
\[\sum N_jS_j=17000\]
\[n_1=200\cdot\frac{5000}{17000}=58.82\]
\[n_2=n_3=200\cdot\frac{6000}{17000}=70.59\]
Rounding gives \(59+71+71=201\), one too many. Take one off: \((59,71,70)\).

Its variance

Substituting the Neyman sizes, almost everything cancels:

\[V_{opt}=\frac1n\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i\Big)^2\]
Question. Same data as above. Find \(V_{opt}\) and compare with \(V_{prop}=1.75\).
Solution.
\[\sum w_iS_i=5+6+6=17\]
\[V_{opt}=\frac{17^2}{200}=\frac{289}{200}=1.445\]
\[\frac{1.75-1.445}{1.75}\approx17.4\%\ \text{lower}\]
Limits of Neyman. You must know each \(S_i\) before sampling. Get them from an old survey, a pilot, records or a past census. If you study several variables, each has its own \(S_i\) pattern and its own best split, so no single allocation suits all of them.

When strata cost different amounts

Interviewing in a remote district costs more than in a city. Let \(C_0\) be the fixed overhead (setup, training, supervision) and \(C_i\) the cost of one unit in stratum \(i\).

\[C=C_0+\sum_{i=1}^{k}C_in_i\]
Question. \(C_0=200\), \(C=(2,4,5)\), sizes \(n=(59,71,70)\). Total cost?
Solution.
\[\sum C_in_i=2(59)+4(71)+5(70)=752\]
\[C=200+752=952\]

The guiding rule

\[n_i\ \propto\ \frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}}\]

More sample for

  • a larger \(w_i\);
  • a larger \(S_i\).

Less sample for

  • a larger \(C_i\).
  • The square root appears because variance falls like \(1/n_i\) while cost rises like \(C_in_i\); the best balance lands on \(\sqrt{C_i}\).

Below, define one handy number used in every cost formula:

\[B=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}\]
Question. \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), \(S=(10,20,30)\), \(C=(2,4,5)\). Find \(B\).
Solution.
\[B=5\sqrt2+6\sqrt4+6\sqrt5\]
\[B=7.071+12+13.416=32.487\]
Cost explorer. Slide the budget and watch the best allocation respond.

Fixed budget: smallest variance

Money available for sampling is \(C^*-C_0\). Spend it so the variance is as small as possible.

\[n_i^*=(C^*-C_0)\,\frac{w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}}{B}\]
\[V_{\min}=\frac{B^2}{C^*-C_0}\]
Question. Use \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), \(S=(10,20,30)\), \(C=(2,4,5)\), \(C_0=200\), \(C^*=1200\). Find \(n_i^*\) and \(V_{\min}\).
Solution. Budget for sampling: \(1200-200=1000\), and \(B=32.487\). The scores \(w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}\) are \(3.536,\ 3.000,\ 2.683\).
\[n_1^*=1000\cdot\frac{3.536}{32.487}=108.8\]
\[n_2^*=1000\cdot\frac{3.000}{32.487}=92.3\]
\[n_3^*=1000\cdot\frac{2.683}{32.487}=82.6\]
\[V_{\min}=\frac{32.487^2}{1000}=1.0554\]
Check the spend: \(2(108.8)+4(92.3)+5(82.6)\approx1000\). ✓

Fixed precision: smallest cost

Now the target variance \(V_0\) is fixed, and you want the cheapest plan that reaches it.

\[n_i^*=\frac{B}{V_0}\cdot\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}}\]
\[C_v^*=\frac{B^2}{V_0}\]
\[C^*=C_0+C_v^*\]
Question. Same strata, \(C_0=200\), target \(V_0=1.2\). Find \(n_i^*\), the sampling cost and the total cost.
Solution. Scale factor:
\[\frac{B}{V_0}=\frac{32.487}{1.2}=27.07\]
\[n_1^*=27.07(3.536)=95.7\]
\[n_2^*=27.07(3.000)=81.2\]
\[n_3^*=27.07(2.683)=72.6\]
\[C_v^*=\frac{1055.4}{1.2}=879.5\]
\[C^*=200+879.5=1079.5\]
Duality. The two problems look different but give the same relative split, \(n_i\propto w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}\). Only the scale changes. Remember it as: weight × spread ÷ √cost.

Proportional allocation with costs

If you stay with \(n_i=nw_i\) but costs differ, the rule ignores the \(S_i\); it only works out how big \(n\) can be.

Fixed budget

Cost is \(C_0+n\sum w_iC_i\). Solve for \(n\):

\[n=\frac{C^*-C_0}{\sum_iw_iC_i}\]
\[n_i=w_i\,\frac{C^*-C_0}{\sum_jw_jC_j}\]
Question. \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), \(C=(2,4,5)\), \(C_0=200\), \(C^*=1200\). Find \(n\) and \(n_i\); then compare variance with the cost-optimum answer \(1.0554\) (use \(S=(10,20,30)\)).
Solution.
\[\sum w_iC_i=1+1.2+1=3.2\]
\[n=\frac{1000}{3.2}=312.5\]
\[n_i=(156.3,\ 93.8,\ 62.5)\]
\[V_{prop}=\frac{350}{312.5}=1.12\]
Same budget, larger variance than \(1.0554\): ignoring variability and cost costs precision.

Fixed variance

From \(V_0=\frac1n\sum w_iS_i^2\):

\[n=\frac{\sum_iw_iS_i^2}{V_0}\]
\[n_i=w_i\,\frac{\sum_jw_jS_j^2}{V_0}\]
Question. Same data, target \(V_0=1.2\). Find \(n\) and \(n_i\).
Solution.
\[n=\frac{350}{1.2}=291.7\]
\[n_i=291.7\,(0.5,0.3,0.2)=(145.8,\ 87.5,\ 58.3)\]

How the rules connect

The cost-optimum rule is the "big" rule. Remove differences and it turns into the simpler ones.

\[\text{Cost-optimum}\ \xrightarrow{\ C_i\ \text{equal}\ }\ \text{Neyman}\ \xrightarrow{\ S_i\ \text{equal}\ }\ \text{Proportional}\]
Question. \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), \(S=(10,20,30)\), \(n=200\). (a) Let all costs be \(3\). (b) Then let all \(S_i=20\). What allocations result?
Solution (a). The common \(\sqrt3\) cancels, so \(n_i\propto w_iS_i=(5,6,6)\), the Neyman split \((58.8,70.6,70.6)\).
Solution (b). Now \(w_iS_i=(10,6,4)\), proportional to \(w\). So \(n_i=(100,60,40)\), the proportional split.
Try it. Switch the simplifications on and watch the cost-optimum split change into Neyman and then proportional.

One full worked example

Question. \(N=(5000,3000,2000)\), \(S=(10,20,15)\), \(n=300\). Compare equal, proportional and Neyman.
Solution. Weights \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\).
Proportional: \(n_i=(150,90,60)\).
\[V_{prop}=\frac{0.5(100)+0.3(400)+0.2(225)}{300}=\frac{215}{300}=0.7167\]
Neyman:
\[\sum w_iS_i=5+6+3=14\]
\[n_i=300\cdot\frac{(5,6,3)}{14}=(107.1,\ 128.6,\ 64.3)\]
\[V_{opt}=\frac{14^2}{300}=0.6533\]
Equal:
\[V_{eq}=\frac{3}{300}(25+36+9)=0.7000\]
Order: \(V_{opt}
Why Neyman never loses to proportional.
\[V_{prop}-V_{opt}=\frac1n\sum_{i}w_i(S_i-S_w)^2\ \ge\ 0\]
where \(S_w=\sum w_iS_i\). The sum is the weighted spread of the \(S_i\). It is zero only when all \(S_i\) match, and then the two rules agree. Here: \(0.7167-0.6533=0.0634\).

Real-life cautions

Rounding and floors

  • Sizes must be whole numbers.
  • Each stratum may need a minimum sample.

Messy fieldwork

  • Nonresponse differs between strata.
  • Costs may not be exactly linear.
  • Several survey variables can pull toward different splits.
  • If sampling fractions are large, bring the finite population correction back.
Take-home. Allocate more to strata that are larger, more variable and cheaper. Treat the formulas as a smart starting point, then adjust for the field.

Practice exercise

Question. Strata: \(N=(500,300,200)\), \(S=(10,20,30)\), \(C=(2,4,5)\), \(n=200\). Find the equal, proportional and Neyman sizes and compare their variances.
Solution.
Equal: \(200/3=66.67\to(67,67,66)\).
Proportional: \((100,60,40)\).
Neyman: \((58.8,70.6,70.6)\to(59,71,70)\).
\[V_{eq}=\frac{3}{200}(25+36+36)=1.455\]
\[V_{prop}=\frac{350}{200}=1.75\]
\[V_{opt}=\frac{17^2}{200}=1.445\]
Neyman is best. Equal beats proportional here because the \(S_i\) differ a lot (the big stratum is calm, the small ones are wild). The costs were not needed for these three rules; use the calculator for the cost-optimum split.
