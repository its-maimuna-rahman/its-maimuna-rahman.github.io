Classical vs. Bayesian philosophy

Classical (Frequentist)

  • Parameter \(\theta\) is unknown but fixed.
  • Data are random: \(X_1,\dots,X_n\sim f(x|\theta)\).
  • Inference is based solely on data.
  • Long-run frequency interpretations.

Bayesian

  • Parameter \(\theta\) is random.
  • Data are fixed (after observation).
  • Prior beliefs \(\pi(\theta)\) quantify uncertainty.
  • Beliefs are updated after seeing data.
  • Probability represents degree of belief.

The key difference: what is random and what is fixed?

The Bayesian framework

Thought process

  1. Prior beliefs. Before data, express uncertainty about \(\theta\) by a prior distribution \(\pi(\theta)\): subjective (expert knowledge), objective (non-informative priors) or conjugate (mathematical convenience).
  2. Observe data. Collect \(x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) from \(f(x|\theta)\).
  3. Update to posterior. Combine prior and data by Bayes' rule to get the posterior distribution \(\pi(\theta|x)\).
  4. Make decisions. Use the posterior for inference, prediction and decision-making.

Bayes' rule: the engine

Three components: the prior \(\pi(\theta)\) (beliefs before data), the likelihood \(f(x|\theta)\) (probability of data given parameter) and the marginal distribution \(m(x)=\int f(x|\theta)\pi(\theta)\,d\theta\) (normalizing constant).

  1. Start from the joint density of the data \(x\) and the parameter \(\theta\). It can be factored in two ways:
    \[f(x,\theta)=f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(x,\theta)=\pi(\theta|x)\,m(x).\]
    The first is likelihood \(\times\) prior. The second is posterior \(\times\) marginal of the data.
  2. Both right-hand sides are the same joint density, so set them equal:
    \[\pi(\theta|x)\,m(x)=f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta).\]
  3. Get \(m(x)\) by integrating \(\theta\) out of the joint density:
    \[m(x)=\int f(x,\theta)\,d\theta=\int f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,d\theta.\]
    After the integral \(\theta\) is gone, so \(m(x)\) depends on \(x\) only. It is a constant as far as \(\theta\) is concerned.
  4. Divide both sides of step 2 by \(m(x)\):
    \[\pi(\theta|x)=\frac{f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{m(x)}.\]
  5. Check that this is a proper density, i.e. it integrates to 1:
    \[\int\pi(\theta|x)\,d\theta=\frac1{m(x)}\int f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac{m(x)}{m(x)}=1.\]
    This is the only job of \(m(x)\): it normalizes.
  6. Because \(m(x)\) has no \(\theta\) in it, we can drop it when we only care about the shape in \(\theta\):
    \[\pi(\theta|x)\propto f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\quad\text{(likelihood}\times\text{prior)}.\]
    In practice: multiply likelihood and prior, keep the terms with \(\theta\), recognize the distribution. The constant then takes care of itself.

The posterior is proportional to likelihood \(\times\) prior. \(m(x)\) only ensures the posterior integrates to 1.

Understanding the posterior

The posterior \(\pi(\theta|x)\) is the updated belief about \(\theta\) after the data. It is a conditional distribution given the observed sample and holds all information about \(\theta\) from prior and data. It gives: point estimates (posterior mean, median, mode), interval estimates (credible intervals) and hypothesis tests (posterior probabilities).

\[\hat\theta_B=E[\theta|x]=\int\theta\,\pi(\theta|x)\,d\theta=\frac{\int\theta\,f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,d\theta}{m(x)}\]

The posterior mean is a common Bayes estimator.

Conjugate priors

A prior \(\pi(\theta)\) is conjugate to a likelihood \(f(x|\theta)\) if the posterior \(\pi(\theta|x)\) belongs to the same family of distributions as the prior.
  • Analytical tractability: closed-form expressions.
  • Sequential updating: the posterior can be the prior for new data.
  • Interpretability: prior parameters have intuitive meanings.
  • Computational simplicity: no numerical integration.

Conjugate priors are to Bayesian statistics what analytical solutions are to differential equations.

Binomial–Beta pair

Estimating a proportion: \(x_1,\dots,x_n\overset{iid}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(p)\). Sufficient statistic \(Y=\sum x_i\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)\).

  1. Each \(x_i\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)\) is 1 (success) or 0 (failure), with probability mass function
    \[f(x_i|p)=p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i},\qquad x_i\in\{0,1\}.\]
  2. The \(x_i\) are independent, so the likelihood of the whole sample is the product. Add the exponents of \(p\), and add the exponents of \(1-p\):
    \[f(x_1,\dots,x_n|p)=\prod_{i=1}^np^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i}=p^{\sum x_i}(1-p)^{\,n-\sum x_i}.\]
  3. Let \(y=\sum_{i=1}^nx_i\), the number of successes. The likelihood depends on the data only through \(y\), so \(Y\) is a sufficient statistic:
    \[f(x_1,\dots,x_n|p)=p^{y}(1-p)^{n-y}.\]
  4. There are \(\binom ny\) orderings of \(y\) successes among \(n\) trials, and each has the same probability \(p^y(1-p)^{n-y}\). So \(Y\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)\):
    \[f(y|p)=\binom ny p^y(1-p)^{n-y},\qquad y=0,1,\dots,n.\]
\[f(y|p)=\binom ny p^y(1-p)^{n-y},\ y=0,\dots,n\qquad\pi(p)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1},\ 0\le p\le1\]

Why Beta? Domain \([0,1]\), flexible shapes, conjugate with the binomial. The fraction \(\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\) in the prior is a constant: it has no \(p\) in it.

Joint, marginal and posterior

  1. Joint density = likelihood \(\times\) prior:
    \[f(y,p)=f(y|p)\,\pi(p)=\binom ny p^y(1-p)^{n-y}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}.\]
  2. Group the constants together and the powers of the same base together:
    \[f(y,p)=\binom ny\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\cdot\big[p^{y}\,p^{\alpha-1}\big]\cdot\big[(1-p)^{n-y}(1-p)^{\beta-1}\big].\]
  3. When you multiply powers of the same base, the exponents add:
    \[p^{y}p^{\alpha-1}=p^{y+\alpha-1},\qquad(1-p)^{n-y}(1-p)^{\beta-1}=(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}.\]
  4. So
    \[f(y,p)=\binom ny\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,p^{y+\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}.\]
    This is a constant (in \(p\)) times \(p^{y+\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}\). That part is the kernel of a Beta density (the kernel is the part that contains the variable).
  1. \(p\) lies in \([0,1]\), so integrate \(p\) out over that range:
    \[f(y)=\int_0^1f(y,p)\,dp.\]
  2. Put in the joint from above. Pull out the constants, since they have no \(p\):
    \[f(y)=\binom ny\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\int_0^1p^{y+\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}\,dp.\]
  3. The leftover integral is a Beta function. Recall the identity
    \[B(m,n)=\frac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}=\int_0^1u^{m-1}(1-u)^{n-1}\,du.\]
    Why it holds: the \(\text{Beta}(m,n)\) density \(\frac{\Gamma(m+n)}{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}u^{m-1}(1-u)^{n-1}\) integrates to 1. Move its constant to the other side.
  4. Match the powers. Here \(u\) is \(p\). From \(m-1=y+\alpha-1\) we get \(m=y+\alpha\). The second parameter of the identity (written \(n\) there) is \(n-y+\beta\) here. The two add up to \(n+\alpha+\beta\), because \(y\) cancels.
  5. So
    \[\int_0^1p^{y+\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}\,dp=\frac{\Gamma(y+\alpha)\Gamma(n-y+\beta)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta)}.\]
  6. Put it back:
    \[f(y)=\binom ny\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(y+\alpha)\Gamma(n-y+\beta)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta)}\quad(\text{Beta-Binomial}).\]
    This \(f(y)\) is the normalizing constant (the marginal) from Bayes' rule.
  1. By definition \(\pi(p|y)=\frac{f(y,p)}{f(y)}\). Write out both pieces:
    \[\pi(p|y)=\frac{\binom ny\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,p^{y+\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}}{\binom ny\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(y+\alpha)\Gamma(n-y+\beta)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta)}}.\]
  2. The factor \(\binom ny\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\) is in the top and in the bottom. Cancel it:
    \[\pi(p|y)=\frac{p^{y+\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}}{\frac{\Gamma(y+\alpha)\Gamma(n-y+\beta)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta)}}.\]
  3. Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal:
    \[\pi(p|y)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(y+\alpha)\Gamma(n-y+\beta)}\,p^{y+\alpha-1}(1-p)^{n-y+\beta-1}.\]
  4. Compare with the \(\text{Beta}(\alpha',\beta')\) density \(\frac{\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta')}{\Gamma(\alpha')\Gamma(\beta')}p^{\alpha'-1}(1-p)^{\beta'-1}\). It matches with
    \[\alpha'=y+\alpha,\qquad\beta'=n-y+\beta,\qquad\alpha'+\beta'=n+\alpha+\beta.\]
  5. So \(p|y\sim\text{Beta}(y+\alpha,\,n-y+\beta)\). Here \(p\) is the variable and \(y\) is fixed (the observed data).
  6. Shortcut: the joint is already a constant times a Beta kernel in \(p\). A density must integrate to 1, so that constant has to be the Beta normalizing constant. You can read off the posterior without computing \(f(y)\).

Bayes estimator and weights

  1. First the mean of a \(\text{Beta}(\alpha',\beta')\). Multiply the density by \(p\) and integrate (\(p\cdot p^{\alpha'-1}=p^{\alpha'}\)):
    \[E[p]=\int_0^1p\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta')}{\Gamma(\alpha')\Gamma(\beta')}p^{\alpha'-1}(1-p)^{\beta'-1}dp=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta')}{\Gamma(\alpha')\Gamma(\beta')}\int_0^1p^{\alpha'}(1-p)^{\beta'-1}dp.\]
  2. The integral is the Beta function with first parameter \(\alpha'+1\) and second parameter \(\beta'\):
    \[\int_0^1p^{\alpha'}(1-p)^{\beta'-1}dp=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha'+1)\Gamma(\beta')}{\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta'+1)}.\]
  3. Use \(\Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z)\), so \(\Gamma(\alpha'+1)=\alpha'\Gamma(\alpha')\) and \(\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta'+1)=(\alpha'+\beta')\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta')\):
    \[E[p]=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta')}{\Gamma(\alpha')\Gamma(\beta')}\cdot\frac{\alpha'\Gamma(\alpha')\Gamma(\beta')}{(\alpha'+\beta')\Gamma(\alpha'+\beta')}=\frac{\alpha'}{\alpha'+\beta'}.\]
  4. Now put in the posterior parameters \(\alpha'=y+\alpha\) and \(\beta'=n-y+\beta\):
    \[\hat p_B=\frac{y+\alpha}{(y+\alpha)+(n-y+\beta)}=\frac{y+\alpha}{n+\alpha+\beta}.\]
    In the denominator \(+y\) and \(-y\) cancel.
  5. Rewrite the numerator as \(y+\alpha=n\cdot\frac yn+(\alpha+\beta)\cdot\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}\). Check: \(n\cdot\frac yn=y\) and \((\alpha+\beta)\cdot\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}=\alpha\). Divide by \(n+\alpha+\beta\):
    \[\hat p_B=\frac{n}{n+\alpha+\beta}\cdot\frac yn+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{n+\alpha+\beta}\cdot\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}.\]
  6. This is a weighted average of the MLE \(\frac yn\) and the prior mean \(\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}\). The weights are positive and add to 1:
    \[\frac{n}{n+\alpha+\beta}+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{n+\alpha+\beta}=\frac{n+\alpha+\beta}{n+\alpha+\beta}=1.\]
  7. Limits of the weights. Divide top and bottom by \(n\):
    \[\frac{n}{n+\alpha+\beta}=\frac1{1+\frac{\alpha+\beta}n}\to1\quad\text{as }n\to\infty\ \text{(data dominates)}.\]
    Divide top and bottom by \(\alpha+\beta\):
    \[\frac{\alpha+\beta}{n+\alpha+\beta}=\frac1{1+\frac n{\alpha+\beta}}\to1\quad\text{as }\alpha+\beta\to\infty\ \text{(prior dominates)}.\]
  8. Shrinkage. Let \(w=\frac n{n+\alpha+\beta}\). Then
    \[\hat p_B=w\,\frac yn+(1-w)\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}=\frac yn+(1-w)\left(\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}-\frac yn\right).\]
    The MLE is moved toward the prior mean by the fraction \(1-w\) of the gap.
  9. Effective prior sample size. \(\hat p_B=\frac{y+\alpha}{n+\alpha+\beta}\) is the success rate you get from \(y+\alpha\) successes in \(n+\alpha+\beta\) trials. So the prior acts like \(\alpha\) extra successes and \(\beta\) extra failures: \(\alpha+\beta\) extra observations.

Normal–Normal pair

Estimating a normal mean: \(X\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2\) known; prior \(\theta\sim N(\mu,\tau^2)\) with \(\mu,\tau^2\) known (data variance \(\sigma^2\), prior variance \(\tau^2\)).

\[f(x|\theta)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{(x-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\qquad\pi(\theta)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau}e^{-\frac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\tau^2}}\]

Posterior derivation (completing the square)

  1. Bayes' rule: \(\pi(\theta|x)\propto f(x|\theta)\,\pi(\theta)\). Write out both densities:
    \[\pi(\theta|x)\propto\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]\cdot\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\tau^2}\right].\]
  2. The factors \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\) and \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau}\) have no \(\theta\), so drop them. Multiplying exponentials adds the exponents:
    \[\pi(\theta|x)\propto\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}-\frac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\tau^2}\right].\]
    Now work on the exponent (the expression in the brackets).
  3. Expand the squares: \((x-\theta)^2=x^2-2x\theta+\theta^2\) and \((\theta-\mu)^2=\theta^2-2\mu\theta+\mu^2\).
    \[\text{exponent}=-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\left(x^2-2x\theta+\theta^2\right)-\frac1{2\tau^2}\left(\theta^2-2\mu\theta+\mu^2\right).\]
  4. The terms without \(\theta\), \(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2}\) and \(-\frac{\mu^2}{2\tau^2}\), only multiply the result by a constant once we take \(\exp\). Move them into "const":
    \[\text{exponent}=-\frac{\theta^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{x\theta}{\sigma^2}-\frac{\theta^2}{2\tau^2}+\frac{\mu\theta}{\tau^2}+\text{const}.\]
  5. Collect the \(\theta^2\) terms and the \(\theta\) terms, and factor out \(-\frac12\):
    \[\text{exponent}=-\frac12\left[\left(\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}\right)\theta^2-2\left(\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}\right)\theta\right]+\text{const}.\]
    Check the sign: \(-\frac12\cdot(-2)\cdot\frac x{\sigma^2}\theta=+\frac{x\theta}{\sigma^2}\), as in the previous step.
  6. Use the posterior variance \(\tau_p^2\) from the table of conjugate families, defined by
    \[\frac1{\tau_p^2}=\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}.\]
    Then
    \[\text{exponent}=-\frac12\left[\frac1{\tau_p^2}\,\theta^2-2\left(\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}\right)\theta\right]+\text{const}.\]
  7. Complete the square. Define the posterior mean \(\mu_p\) by \(\mu_p=\tau_p^2\left(\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}\right)\), so that \(\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}=\frac{\mu_p}{\tau_p^2}\). Factor \(\frac1{\tau_p^2}\) out of the bracket, then add and subtract \(\mu_p^2\):
    \[\frac1{\tau_p^2}\theta^2-2\,\frac{\mu_p}{\tau_p^2}\,\theta=\frac1{\tau_p^2}\left[\theta^2-2\mu_p\theta\right]=\frac1{\tau_p^2}\left[(\theta-\mu_p)^2-\mu_p^2\right]=\frac{(\theta-\mu_p)^2}{\tau_p^2}-\frac{\mu_p^2}{\tau_p^2}.\]
  8. \(\frac{\mu_p^2}{\tau_p^2}\) has no \(\theta\), so it also goes into the constant:
    \[\text{exponent}=-\frac{(\theta-\mu_p)^2}{2\tau_p^2}+\text{const}.\]
  9. Exponentiate:
    \[\pi(\theta|x)\propto\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\theta-\mu_p)^2}{2\tau_p^2}\right].\]
    Compare with the prior kernel \(\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\tau^2}\right]\). It has the same form, with mean \(\mu_p\) and variance \(\tau_p^2\): a Normal kernel.
  10. So \(\theta|x\sim N(\mu_p,\tau_p^2)\) with
    \[\mu_p=\tau_p^2\left(\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}\right)=\frac{\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}}{\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}}=E(\theta|x),\qquad\tau_p^2=\frac1{\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}}=\text{Var}(\theta|x).\]
    The constant is fixed because a density integrates to 1. The next steps simplify these two formulas.

Simplifying the mean and variance

  1. Simplify the mean. Multiply top and bottom of \(E(\theta|x)\) by \(\sigma^2\tau^2\). Top:
    \[\left(\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}\right)\sigma^2\tau^2=x\tau^2+\mu\sigma^2.\]
    Bottom:
    \[\left(\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}\right)\sigma^2\tau^2=\tau^2+\sigma^2.\]
  2. So
    \[E(\theta|x)=\frac{\tau^2x+\sigma^2\mu}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}\,x+\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}\,\mu.\]
    The last step splits one fraction into two with the same denominator.
  3. Simplify the variance. Put \(\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}\) over a common denominator:
    \[\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}=\frac{\tau^2}{\sigma^2\tau^2}+\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2\tau^2}=\frac{\tau^2+\sigma^2}{\sigma^2\tau^2}.\]
    The variance is the reciprocal:
    \[\text{Var}(\theta|x)=\frac1{\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}}=\frac{\sigma^2\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}.\]
\[E(\theta|x)=\frac{\frac x{\sigma^2}+\frac\mu{\tau^2}}{\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}}=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}x+\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}\mu\qquad\text{Var}(\theta|x)=\frac1{\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}}=\frac{\tau^2\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}\]

Interpretation

  1. Precision is 1/variance. Data precision is \(\frac1{\sigma^2}\) and prior precision is \(\frac1{\tau^2}\). The posterior precision is their sum:
    \[\frac1{\tau_p^2}=\frac1{\text{Var}(\theta|x)}=\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}.\]
  2. Precision weights. Total precision is \(\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}=\frac{\tau^2+\sigma^2}{\sigma^2\tau^2}\). Data weight:
    \[\frac{1/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma^2+1/\tau^2}=\frac1{\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}.\]
    Prior weight:
    \[\frac{1/\tau^2}{1/\sigma^2+1/\tau^2}=\frac1{\tau^2}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}.\]
  3. These are the same weights found when simplifying the mean, and they add to 1: \(\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}+\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}=1\). So
    \[E(\theta|x)=\frac{\text{data precision}}{\text{total precision}}\,x+\frac{\text{prior precision}}{\text{total precision}}\,\mu.\]
    The source with more precision (smaller variance) gets more weight.
  1. Vague prior, \(\tau^2\to\infty\). Divide top and bottom by \(\tau^2\):
    \[\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}=\frac1{1+\sigma^2/\tau^2}\to1,\qquad\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}\to0,\qquad\text{Var}(\theta|x)=\frac{\sigma^2}{1+\sigma^2/\tau^2}\to\sigma^2.\]
    So \(E(\theta|x)\to x\) and \(\text{Var}(\theta|x)\to\sigma^2\). Only the data matters.
  2. Very noisy data, \(\sigma^2\to\infty\). Divide top and bottom by \(\sigma^2\):
    \[\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}=\frac{\tau^2/\sigma^2}{\tau^2/\sigma^2+1}\to0,\qquad\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}=\frac1{\tau^2/\sigma^2+1}\to1,\qquad\text{Var}(\theta|x)=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2/\sigma^2+1}\to\tau^2.\]
    So \(E(\theta|x)\to\mu\) and \(\text{Var}(\theta|x)\to\tau^2\). The data is ignored and the prior stays.

Poisson–Gamma pair

Estimating a Poisson rate: \(X_1,\dots,X_n\overset{iid}{\sim}\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\), prior \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)\) (shape \(\alpha\), rate \(\beta\)).

  1. Each \(X_i\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) has probability \(\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!}\). The sample is independent, so multiply:
    \[f(\mathbf x|\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!}.\]
  2. Collect like terms. \(n\) copies of \(e^{-\lambda}\) give \(e^{-n\lambda}\). The powers combine as \(\lambda^{x_1}\lambda^{x_2}\cdots\lambda^{x_n}=\lambda^{x_1+\cdots+x_n}\). The denominators multiply:
    \[f(\mathbf x|\lambda)=\frac{e^{-n\lambda}\,\lambda^{\sum x_i}}{\prod x_i!}.\]
\[f(\mathbf x|\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!}=\frac{e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^{\sum x_i}}{\prod x_i!}\qquad\pi(\lambda)=\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\lambda},\ \lambda,\alpha,\beta>0\]

Why Gamma? Support \((0,\infty)\), flexible shapes, conjugate with Poisson.

Posterior

  1. Bayes: \(\pi(\lambda|\mathbf x)\propto f(\mathbf x|\lambda)\,\pi(\lambda)\). The factors \(\frac1{\prod x_i!}\) and \(\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\) have no \(\lambda\), so drop them:
    \[\pi(\lambda|\mathbf x)\propto\left(e^{-n\lambda}\lambda^{\sum x_i}\right)\left(\lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\lambda}\right).\]
  2. Combine powers of the same base by adding exponents:
    \[\lambda^{\sum x_i}\lambda^{\alpha-1}=\lambda^{\sum x_i+\alpha-1},\qquad e^{-n\lambda}e^{-\beta\lambda}=e^{-(n+\beta)\lambda}.\]
    So
    \[\pi(\lambda|\mathbf x)\propto\lambda^{(\sum x_i)+\alpha-1}e^{-(n+\beta)\lambda}.\]
  3. Compare with the \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha',\beta')\) kernel \(\lambda^{\alpha'-1}e^{-\beta'\lambda}\). Match \(\alpha'-1=\sum x_i+\alpha-1\), so the shape is \(\alpha'=\sum x_i+\alpha\). Match \(\beta'=n+\beta\), the rate.
  4. The missing constant is forced. Since \(\int_0^\infty\lambda^{\alpha'-1}e^{-\beta'\lambda}d\lambda=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha')}{\beta'^{\,\alpha'}}\), the density must be \(\frac{\beta'^{\,\alpha'}}{\Gamma(\alpha')}\lambda^{\alpha'-1}e^{-\beta'\lambda}\). Therefore
    \[\lambda|\mathbf x\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(\sum x_i+\alpha,\ n+\beta\Big).\]

Bayes estimator

  1. First the mean of a \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha',\beta')\) (shape \(\alpha'\), rate \(\beta'\)). Use \(\int_0^\infty\lambda^{\alpha'-1}e^{-\beta'\lambda}d\lambda=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha')}{\beta'^{\,\alpha'}}\), with \(\alpha'+1\) in place of \(\alpha'\) for the integral below:
    \[E[\lambda]=\int_0^\infty\lambda\cdot\frac{\beta'^{\,\alpha'}}{\Gamma(\alpha')}\lambda^{\alpha'-1}e^{-\beta'\lambda}d\lambda=\frac{\beta'^{\,\alpha'}}{\Gamma(\alpha')}\int_0^\infty\lambda^{\alpha'}e^{-\beta'\lambda}d\lambda=\frac{\beta'^{\,\alpha'}}{\Gamma(\alpha')}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\alpha'+1)}{\beta'^{\,\alpha'+1}}.\]
  2. Use \(\Gamma(\alpha'+1)=\alpha'\,\Gamma(\alpha')\):
    \[E[\lambda]=\frac{\beta'^{\,\alpha'}}{\Gamma(\alpha')}\cdot\frac{\alpha'\,\Gamma(\alpha')}{\beta'^{\,\alpha'+1}}=\frac{\alpha'}{\beta'}.\]
  3. Put in the posterior parameters \(\alpha'=\sum x_i+\alpha\) and \(\beta'=n+\beta\):
    \[\hat\lambda_B=\frac{\sum x_i+\alpha}{n+\beta}.\]
  4. Rewrite the numerator as \(\sum x_i+\alpha=n\cdot\frac{\sum x_i}n+\beta\cdot\frac\alpha\beta\). Check: \(n\cdot\frac{\sum x_i}n=\sum x_i\) and \(\beta\cdot\frac\alpha\beta=\alpha\). Divide by \(n+\beta\):
    \[\hat\lambda_B=\frac n{n+\beta}\cdot\frac{\sum x_i}n+\frac\beta{n+\beta}\cdot\frac\alpha\beta.\]
    This is a weighted average of the MLE \(\bar x=\frac{\sum x_i}n\) and the prior mean \(\frac\alpha\beta\). The weights add to \(\frac{n+\beta}{n+\beta}=1\), and \(\beta\) acts as a prior "sample size".
Example.
  1. Prior \(\alpha=2\), \(\beta=1\), so the prior mean is \(\frac\alpha\beta=2\). Data \(n=5\), \(x=(3,5,2,4,6)\):
    \[\sum x_i=3+5+2+4+6=20,\qquad\bar x=\frac{20}5=4=\hat\lambda_{\text{MLE}}.\]
  2. Posterior: \(\text{Gamma}(\sum x_i+\alpha,\ n+\beta)=\text{Gamma}(20+2,\ 5+1)=\text{Gamma}(22,\,6)\). Posterior mean:
    \[\hat\lambda_B=\frac{20+2}{5+1}=\frac{22}6=3.67.\]
  3. Check with the weighted form. The weights are \(\frac n{n+\beta}=\frac56=0.833\) and \(\frac\beta{n+\beta}=\frac16=0.167\):
    \[\hat\lambda_B=\frac56(4)+\frac16(2)=0.833\times4+0.167\times2=3.67.\]
    Exactly: \(\frac{20}6+\frac26=\frac{22}6\).
  4. \(3.67\) lies between the prior mean \(2\) and the sample mean \(4\). The MLE is shrunk toward the prior mean.

Key properties of Bayes estimators

  1. Weighted average: \(\hat\theta_B=w\cdot(\text{sample estimate})+(1-w)\cdot(\text{prior mean})\), with \(0\lt w\lt1\) and a different \(w\) for each pair:
    \[\begin{aligned}\text{Binomial-Beta:}\quad&w=\frac n{n+\alpha+\beta}\\[1.6ex]\text{Poisson-Gamma:}\quad&w=\frac n{n+\beta}\\[1.6ex]\text{Normal-Normal:}\quad&w=\frac{1/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma^2+1/\tau^2}=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}\end{aligned}\]
  2. Shrinkage: pulls extreme sample estimates toward the prior mean; reduces variance at the cost of some bias; most useful for small samples. Since \(0\lt w\lt1\), \(\hat\theta_B\) lies between the sample estimate and the prior mean. Rewrite to see the shift:
    \[\begin{aligned}\hat\theta_B&=w\,(\text{sample estimate})+(1-w)\,(\text{prior mean})\\[1.6ex]&=(\text{sample estimate})+(1-w)\Big[(\text{prior mean})-(\text{sample estimate})\Big].\end{aligned}\]
    The estimate moves from the sample estimate toward the prior mean by the fraction \(1-w\) of the gap. This is shrinkage. Bias and variance. Treat the prior mean as a fixed number and the sample estimate (the MLE) as unbiased, \(E[\text{MLE}]=\theta\). Then
    \[\begin{aligned}E[\hat\theta_B]&=w\,\theta+(1-w)\,(\text{prior mean})\\[1.6ex]\text{Bias}=E[\hat\theta_B]-\theta&=(1-w)\big[(\text{prior mean})-\theta\big]\\[1.6ex]\text{Var}(\hat\theta_B)&=w^2\,\text{Var}(\text{MLE})\end{aligned}\]
    Since \(w\lt1\), \(w^2\lt1\), so the variance goes down. The cost is the bias \((1-w)\big[(\text{prior mean})-\theta\big]\). For small samples \(\text{Var}(\text{MLE})\) is large (for example \(\text{Var}(y/n)=\frac{p(1-p)}n\)), so cutting it by the factor \(w^2\) helps most there.
  3. Consistency: as \(n\to\infty\), \(\hat\theta_B\to\) MLE; the prior's influence fades with more data. The weights tend to 1:
    \[\frac n{n+\alpha+\beta}=\frac1{1+\frac{\alpha+\beta}n}\to1,\qquad\frac n{n+\beta}=\frac1{1+\frac\beta n}\to1.\]
    For the normal pair \(w\to1\) when the data precision is much larger than the prior precision. With the mean of \(n\) observations the data precision is \(\frac n{\sigma^2}\), which grows with \(n\). With \(w\to1\) the shift from step 2 vanishes:
    \[\hat\theta_B-(\text{sample estimate})=(1-w)\big[(\text{prior mean})-(\text{sample estimate})\big]\to0.\]
    So \(\hat\theta_B\to\) MLE: the prior fades as the data grows.

Bayesian workflow

  1. Specify the model: likelihood \(f(x|\theta)\) and prior \(\pi(\theta)\).
  2. Compute the posterior \(\pi(\theta|x)\propto f(x|\theta)\pi(\theta)\).
  3. Check conjugacy: if conjugate, the posterior has a closed form.
  4. Summarize: point estimates (mean, median, mode), credible intervals, probabilities of hypotheses.
  5. Make decisions: prediction and decision-making.

Pairs studied: Binomial + Beta \(\to\) Beta; Normal + Normal \(\to\) Normal; Poisson + Gamma \(\to\) Gamma. Further: non-conjugate priors and MCMC, hierarchical models, model selection, empirical Bayes (AST501). Reading: Casella and Berger (2002); Gelman et al. (2013); Carlin and Louis (2008).
