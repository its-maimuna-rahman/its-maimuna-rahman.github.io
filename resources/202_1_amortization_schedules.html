Amortization Schedules

Repaying a loan by instalments: the outstanding balance (prospective and retrospective), the amortization schedule, and all worked examples (6.1–6.4) from the lecture slides, plus a quiz, formula sheet and calculator.

1 · Two ways to repay a loan

Amortization methodThe borrower repays the lender by installment payments at periodic intervals. This is called “amortization of the loan”.
Sinking fund methodThe borrower repays by one lump sum at the end of the term, pays interest on the loan in instalments meanwhile, and also makes periodic payments into a sinking fund that accumulates to the amount of the loan at the end of the term.

This guide covers the amortization method.

2 · Finding the outstanding loan balance

Key factIf a loan is repaid by amortization, the instalment payments form an annuity whose present value equals the original amount of the loan.

Why it matters

Example. A family buys a home with a 30-year mortgage. After making payments for 12 years, how much must they pay in one lump sum to pay off the mortgage completely? That lump sum is the outstanding loan balance.

There are two approaches: the prospective and the retrospective method.

Prospective (looking into the future)The balance at any time is the present value, at that date, of the remaining payments. \[\text{Outstanding balance}=\text{PV of future payments}\]
Retrospective (looking into the past)The balance is the original loan accumulated to that date, less the accumulated value of all payments already made. \[\text{Outstanding balance}=\text{AV of loan}-\text{AV of past payments}\]

Why the two methods agree

At inception: PV of payments = amount of loan. Accumulate both sides to the date where the balance is wanted: \[\text{Current value of payments}=\text{accumulated value of loan}.\] Split the payments into past and future ones: \[\text{AV of past payments}+\text{PV of future payments}=\text{AV of loan}.\] Rearranging: \[\text{PV of future payments}=\text{AV of loan}-\text{AV of past payments}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{Prospective}=\text{Retrospective}.\]

Notation and the basic case

  • \(B_t\): outstanding loan balance at time \(t\) (computed after making the \(t\)-th payment)
  • \(B_t^{p}\): prospective form; \(B_t^{r}\): retrospective form

Consider a loan of \(a_{\overline{n}|}\) at rate \(i\) per period, repaid by payments of 1 at the end of each period for \(n\) periods, with balance wanted \(t\) periods after inception, \(0 \[B_t^{p}=a_{\overline{n-t}|},\qquad B_t^{r}=a_{\overline{n}|}(1+i)^t-s_{\overline{t}|}.\]

Proof that the retrospective form equals the prospective form \[\begin{aligned}a_{\overline{n}|}(1+i)^t-s_{\overline{t}|}&=\frac{1-v^n}{i}(1+i)^t-\frac{(1+i)^t-1}{i}\\&=\frac{(1+i)^t-v^{n-t}-(1+i)^t+1}{i}\\&=\frac{1-v^{n-t}}{i}=a_{\overline{n-t}|}.\end{aligned}\]

The step uses \(v^n(1+i)^t=v^{n-t}\).

3 · Prospective vs retrospective: which is easier?

In any given problem, either method may be more efficient.

ProspectiveIf the size and number of the payments are known, the prospective method is usually more efficient.
RetrospectiveIf the number of payments or the size of a final irregular payment is not known, the retrospective method is usually more efficient.

4 · Example 6.1 · Both methods

A loan is repaid with 10 payments of \$2000 followed by 10 payments of \$1000 at the end of each half-year. The nominal rate convertible semiannually is 10%. Find the outstanding balance immediately after five payments, by both methods.

Given. Per half-year \(i=\dfrac{10\%}{2}=0.05\). We are at \(t=5\), right after the 5th payment of \$2000.

Step 1 · Prospective. Remaining: payments 6–10 (five of \$2000) and payments 11–20 (ten of \$1000, i.e. at \(t=11,\dots,20\)). Discount the second block 5 periods back to time 5: \[B_5^{p}=2000\,a_{\overline{5}|0.05}+1000\,a_{\overline{10}|0.05}\,v^{5}\] \[a_{\overline{5}|0.05}=\frac{1-1.05^{-5}}{0.05}=4.329477,\quad a_{\overline{10}|0.05}=7.721735,\quad v^5=0.783526\] \[B_5^{p}=8{,}658.95+6{,}050.18=\$14{,}709.13\]
Step 2 · Retrospective. First the original loan, the PV of all 20 payments: \[L=2000\,a_{\overline{10}|0.05}+1000\,a_{\overline{10}|0.05}\,v^{10}=15{,}443.47+4{,}740.48=\$20{,}183.95\quad(v^{10}=0.613913)\] Accumulate 5 periods: \(L(1.05)^5=20{,}183.95\times1.276282=25{,}760.40\).
Step 3 · Accumulate the first five payments (made at \(t=1,\dots,5\)) to time 5: \[2000\,[1.05^4+1.05^3+1.05^2+1.05+1]=2000\,s_{\overline{5}|0.05},\quad s_{\overline{5}|0.05}=\frac{1.05^5-1}{0.05}=5.525631\] \[2000\times5.525631=11{,}051.26\] \[B_5^{r}=25{,}760.40-11{,}051.26=\$14{,}709.13\ \checkmark\]
Both methods give \(B_5\approx\$14{,}709.13\).
Note on the slidesThe slides show 14,708.55 (prospective) and 14,706.01 (retrospective). Small arithmetic slips in the intermediate products (6,049.6 should be 6,050.18; 4,740.8 should be 4,740.48) cause that gap. Done accurately, both methods agree exactly, as theory requires.

5 · Example 6.2 · Extra payment, revised instalment

A loan is repaid with 20 annual payments of \$1000. At the time of the fifth payment the borrower pays an extra \$2000 and then repays the balance over 12 years with a revised annual payment. The effective rate is 9%. Find the revised payment.

Step 1 · Balance after 5 years (prospective; 15 payments remain). \[B_5^{p}=1000\,a_{\overline{15}|0.09}=1000(8.0607)=\$8{,}060.70\]
Step 2 · Apply the extra \$2000. New balance \(=8{,}060.70-2{,}000=\$6{,}060.70\).
Step 3 · Equation of value for the revised payment \(X\). \[X\,a_{\overline{12}|0.09}=6{,}060.70\;\Rightarrow\;X=\frac{6{,}060.70}{7.1607}=\$846.38\]
Revised annual payment ≈ \$846.38.

6 · The amortization schedule

Each payment is partly repayment of interest and partly repayment of principal.

DefinitionAn amortization schedule is a table showing the division of each payment into principal and interest, together with the outstanding loan balance after each payment.

The basic loan \(a_{\overline{n}|}\), payments of 1

At the end of period 1, the interest due on the opening balance is \(i\,a_{\overline{n}|}=1-v^n\). The rest of the payment, \(v^n\), is principal repaid. The balance at the end of the period is the opening balance less principal repaid: \[a_{\overline{n}|}-v^n=a_{\overline{n-1}|}.\]

Notation and key observations

\(I_t\): interest paid in the \(t\)-th instalment; \(P_t\): principal repaid in the \(t\)-th instalment (the slide labels \(P_t\) “interest repaid”, a typo). Thus \[I_t=1-v^{n-t+1},\qquad P_t=v^{n-t+1}.\]

  1. The sum of the principal repayments equals the original amount of the loan.
  2. The sum of the interest payments equals the difference between total payments and total principal repaid: \(n-a_{\overline{n}|}\) here.
  3. The principal repayments form a geometric progression with common ratio \(1+i\), so any one principal repayment can be found from any other and the rate: \[\frac{P_{t+1}}{P_t}=\frac{v^{n-t}}{v^{n-t+1}}=1+i,\qquad P_{t+k}=P_t(1+i)^k.\]

Check of 1: \(\sum_{t=1}^{n}v^{n-t+1}=v+v^2+\dots+v^n=a_{\overline{n}|}\).

Assumptions

  1. Constant rate of interest.
  2. Payment period and interest conversion period are equal.
  3. Annuity payments are level.
RoundingRound-off error accumulates, so standard practice is to adjust the last payment to equal the final period’s interest plus the opening balance of the final period. This brings the balance exactly to zero.

Payment. \[R=\frac{1000}{a_{\overline{4}|}}=\frac{1000}{3.3121}=\$301.92\]

7 · Example 6.3 · Interest and principal in a given payment

A \$1000 loan is repaid by payments of \$100 at the end of each quarter for as long as necessary, plus a smaller final payment. The nominal rate convertible quarterly is 16%. Find the principal and interest in the fourth payment.

Here the number of payments is not known, so use the retrospective method. Per quarter \(i=0.04\).

Step 1 · Balance at the end of the 3rd quarter (start of the 4th): \[B_3^{r}=1000(1.04)^3-100\,s_{\overline{3}|0.04}=1124.86-312.16=\$812.70\]
Step 2 · Interest. \(I_4=0.04(812.70)=\$32.51\)
Step 3 · Principal. \(P_4=100.00-32.51=\$67.49\)
Fourth payment: interest \$32.51, principal \$67.49. (The slide writes \(B_4^r\) for this balance; strictly it is \(B_3\), the balance after the 3rd payment.)

8 · Example 6.4 · Selling the right to future payments

A borrows \$10,000 from B, repaying with equal quarterly instalments of principal and interest at 8% convertible quarterly over six years. At the end of two years B sells the right to the remaining payments to C at a price giving C a yield of 10% convertible quarterly. Find the total interest received (1) by C and (2) by B.

Given. Loan \$10,000; per quarter \(0.08/4=0.02\); \(n=6\times4=24\) payments (an annuity).

Step 1 · Quarterly payment. \[10{,}000=R\,a_{\overline{24}|0.02},\quad a_{\overline{24}|0.02}=18.9139,\quad R=\frac{10{,}000}{18.9139}=\$528.71\]
Step 2 · Balance after 2 years (8 payments made, 16 remain), at the original rate 2%: \[B_8=R\,a_{\overline{16}|0.02}=528.71\times13.5777\approx\$7{,}178.68\] (The slide rounds \(a_{\overline{16}|0.02}\) to 13.58, giving 7,180.68; this balance is not needed again below.)
Step 3 · Price C pays. C’s yield is \(0.10/4=0.025\) per quarter. C receives the 16 remaining payments, so \[P_C=528.71\,a_{\overline{16}|0.025}=528.71\times13.0550=\$6{,}902.32\] (Slide: 6,901.41, using \(a_{\overline{16}|0.025}\) rounded to 13.056.) C pays less than the balance because C demands a higher yield than 2% per quarter.
Step 4 · Interest received by C. C collects \(16\times528.71=8{,}459.36\) and paid \$6,902.32: \[\text{Interest}_C=8{,}459.36-6{,}902.32=\$1{,}557.04\]
Step 5 · Interest received by B. B collects 8 payments, \(8\times528.71=4{,}229.68\), then sells for \$6,902.32, against an outlay of \$10,000: \[\text{Interest}_B=4{,}229.68+6{,}902.32-10{,}000=\$1{,}132.00\]
C receives ≈ \$1,557 of interest; B receives ≈ \$1,132. (Slide: 1,557.95 and 1,131.09, from the rounded price 6,901.41.)
  • B collects 8 payments, then sells the rest to C.
  • C pays a discounted price (higher yield) and earns the difference between what is collected and what is paid.
  • B earns interest from the first 8 payments plus the profit or loss from the sale of the loan.
