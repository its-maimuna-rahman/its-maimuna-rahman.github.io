Stratified Random Sampling

Procedure, estimation, variance, allocation rules (equal, proportional, Neyman, cost-optimum), precision gains, proportions and post-stratification. Short forms are shown in bold brackets.

Basics: idea, procedure, advantages

Stratified random sampling (StRS) divides a population of \(N\) units into \(k\) non-overlapping groups called strata, then draws an independent SRSWOR (simple random sample without replacement) from each stratum and combines the results.

\[N=\sum_{i=1}^{k}N_i,\qquad n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_i\]

where \(N_i\) = population size and \(n_i\) = sample size of stratum \(i\); \(k\) = number of strata.

Key idea. Instead of one SRS from the whole population, take separate random samples from each stratum and combine the information.

Example (students' height). Height differs by age/class, so form 4 strata: Classes 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12. Draw an SRS from each and combine. Why stratify? Students within a suitably defined stratum are more similar in the characteristic of interest.

Procedure of selection

  1. Define the target population.
  2. Divide it into \(k\) mutually exclusive strata.
  3. Determine \(N_i\) for each stratum.
  4. Decide the sample size \(n_i\) for each stratum (the allocation problem).
  5. Select an SRSWOR of \(n_i\) units from stratum \(i\).
  6. Repeat for all strata.
  7. Combine the selected units into the final sample.

Advantages

  1. Known precision for important subpopulations: more intensive study of chosen strata.
  2. Different sampling problems: parts of the population can be sampled differently.
  3. Administrative convenience: districts etc. can serve as strata.
  4. Representation: segments are deliberately represented.
  5. Efficiency: well-formed strata reduce sampling variability.

Further advantages

  1. Skewed populations: very large or influential units get proper attention.
  2. Subpopulation estimates: useful when both population and subgroup estimates are needed.
  3. Sampling frames: separate frames may be easier to obtain.
  4. Large populations: separate sampling is administratively convenient.
  5. Precision: suitable weights improve estimates of means and totals.
How should strata be built? Since smaller \(S_i^2\) gives smaller variance, good strata are homogeneous within and meaningfully different between strata.

Notation

  • \(N\): population size; \(k\): number of strata; \(N_i,\ n_i\): population and sample size in stratum \(i\).
  • Stratum weight \(w_i=W_i=N_i/N\), with \(\sum_i w_i=1\) (Part 1 writes \(W_i\), Parts 2–3 write \(w_i\)). Sampling fraction \(f_i=n_i/N_i\).
  • \(y_{ij}\): value of the \(j\)th unit in stratum \(i\), \(i=1,\dots,k\), \(j=1,\dots,n_i\) (population values \(Y_{ij}\), \(j=1,\dots,N_i\)).
\[\bar Y_i=\frac1{N_i}\sum_{j=1}^{N_i}Y_{ij},\quad \bar y_i=\frac1{n_i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}y_{ij},\quad S_i^2=\frac1{N_i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N_i}(Y_{ij}-\bar Y_i)^2,\quad s_i^2=\frac1{n_i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}(y_{ij}-\bar y_i)^2\]

where \(\bar Y_i,\bar y_i\) = population and sample mean of stratum \(i\); \(S_i^2,s_i^2\) = population and sample variance of stratum \(i\). Under SRSWOR within strata, \(\E(\bar y_i)=\bar Y_i\).

The population mean is a weighted mean of stratum means (not their simple average):

\[\bar Y=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^kN_i\bar Y_i=\sum_{i=1}^kw_i\bar Y_i\]

Estimation of the population mean

Replace each unknown \(\bar Y_i\) by \(\bar y_i\) to get the stratified mean (\(\bar y_{st}\)):

\[\bar y_{st}=\sum_{i=1}^kw_i\bar y_i=\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{N_i}{N}\bar y_i\]
Worked example. \(N_i=500,300,200\) (\(N=1000\)), \(\bar y_i=40,55,70\): \(\bar y_{st}=0.5(40)+0.3(55)+0.2(70)=20+16.5+14=50.5.\)
Unbiased. \(\E(\bar y_{st})=\bar Y\) (proof D1), because each \(\E(\bar y_i)=\bar Y_i\).

Population weights versus sample weights

The tempting estimator \(\bar y_{naive}=\sum_i\frac{n_i}{n}\bar y_i\) has \(\E(\bar y_{naive})=\sum_i\frac{n_i}n\bar Y_i\ne\bar Y\) when sampling fractions differ (proof D2). Always use \(w_i=N_i/N\), not \(n_i/n\). The two agree under proportional allocation.

Variance of the stratified mean

For SRSWOR in stratum \(i\), with the finite population correction (FPC) \(1-n_i/N_i\):

\[\V(\bar y_i)=\Big(1-\frac{n_i}{N_i}\Big)\frac{S_i^2}{n_i}\]

Samples from different strata are independent, so \(\mathrm{Cov}(\bar y_i,\bar y_l)=0\) for \(i\ne l\) and the variances add with squared weights (proof D3):

\[\V(\bar y_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^kw_i^2\Big(1-\frac{n_i}{N_i}\Big)\frac{S_i^2}{n_i}\]

With replacement (SRSWR) inside strata: \(\V(\bar y_i)=\sigma_i^2/n_i\), so \(\V(\bar y_{st})=\sum w_i^2\sigma_i^2/n_i\), where \(\sigma_i^2=\frac{N_i-1}{N_i}S_i^2\approx S_i^2\). The allocation derivations use this form, \(\sum w_i^2S_i^2/n_i\), which is also the SRSWOR variance when the FPC is ignored (small sampling fractions).

Estimating the variance

\(S_i^2\) is unknown; use \(s_i^2\) (unbiased, proof D4). The standard error (SE) is the square root:

\[\vh(\bar y_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^kw_i^2\Big(1-\frac{n_i}{N_i}\Big)\frac{s_i^2}{n_i},\qquad SE(\bar y_{st})=\sqrt{\vh(\bar y_{st})}\]
Worked example (corrected). \(w=(0.6,0.4)\), \(N=(600,400)\), \(n=(60,40)\), \(s^2=(100,64)\): \[\vh=0.36(0.9)\tfrac{100}{60}+0.16(0.9)\tfrac{64}{40}=0.5400+0.2304=0.7704,\qquad SE=\sqrt{0.7704}\approx0.878.\]

The slide's answer \(1.1904\) cannot be right: even with no FPC the value is \(0.856\).

Allocation problem and the three basic rules

The same total \(n=\sum n_i\) can be split among strata in many ways \((n_1,\dots,n_k)\). Cost rises with \(n_i\) while variance falls with \(n_i\). Two questions: (1) for a required precision, how to allocate resources? (2) for a fixed cost, how to allocate to get the best precision?

All derivations minimise \(V=\sum_i w_i^2S_i^2/n_i\) (FPC ignored, handy when sampling fractions are small).

Equal (EA)

\[n_i=\frac nk\]

Same \(n_i\) everywhere (D5). Useful when strata are equally important, variances unknown, or equal representation is wanted. Ignores size and variability, so not variance-optimal.

e.g. \(n=600,k=6\): \(n_i=100\).

Proportional (PA)

\[n_i=nw_i=n\frac{N_i}{N}\]

\(n_i\propto N_i\), so every stratum has the same sampling fraction \(n_i/N_i=n/N\) (D6). A stratum with 20% of the population gets about 20% of the sample. Needs no knowledge of \(S_i\).

Neyman (optimum) allocation (NA)

\[n_i=n\,\frac{w_iS_i}{\sum_jw_jS_j}=n\,\frac{N_iS_i}{\sum_jN_jS_j}\]

Derived by Lagrange multipliers (D8). The sample is proportional to \(N_iS_i\): larger and more variable strata get more observations. Using \(A=\sum w_iS_i\): \(n_i=nw_iS_i/A\).

RuleFormulaInformation needed
Equal\(n/k\)number of strata
Proportional\(nw_i\)stratum sizes
Neyman\(nw_iS_i/\sum w_jS_j\)sizes and stratum SDs
Cost-optimum\(\propto w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}\)sizes, SDs and unit costs
Examples. Proportional: \(N=(500,300,200)\), \(n=200\): \(n_i=(100,60,40)\). Neyman: \(S=(10,20,30)\): \(N_iS_i=5000,6000,6000\), total \(17000\), so \(n_i\approx(58.8,70.6,70.6)\to(59,71,70)\) (round, then adjust so \(\sum n_i=n\)).
Limitations of Neyman allocation. It needs prior \(S_1,\dots,S_k\) (previous survey, pilot, administrative data, past census). With several study variables, each has its own \(S_i\) pattern and hence its own optimum, so no single allocation is best for all.

Unequal survey costs

Let \(C_0\) = overhead (office setup, questionnaire development, training, common supervision) and \(C_i\) = cost per sampled unit in stratum \(i\) (travel, interviewer time, hard field conditions, different modes). Total cost:

\[C=C_0+\sum_{i=1}^kC_in_i\]

Now both \(n_i\) and \(C_i\) matter. The principle is

\[n_i\propto\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}}\]

larger \(w_i\) ⇒ more sample; larger \(S_i\) ⇒ more sample; larger \(C_i\) ⇒ less sample. The square root appears because the optimisation balances a term \(\propto1/n_i\) against a term \(\propto C_in_i\).

Fixed total cost \(C^*\): minimise variance

Constraint \(\sum C_in_i=C^*-C_0\). Lagrange method (D11) with \(B=\sum_iw_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}\):

\[n_i^*=(C^*-C_0)\frac{w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}}{\sum_jw_jS_j\sqrt{C_j}},\qquad V_{\min}=\frac{\big(\sum_iw_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}\big)^2}{C^*-C_0}\]

Fixed variance \(V_0\): minimise cost

Minimise \(C_v=\sum C_in_i\) subject to \(\sum w_i^2S_i^2/n_i=V_0\) (D12):

\[n_i^*=\frac{B}{V_0}\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}},\qquad C_v^*=\frac{B^2}{V_0}=\frac{\big(\sum w_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}\big)^2}{V_0},\qquad C^*=C_0+C_v^*\]
Duality. "Minimise variance for fixed cost" and "minimise cost for fixed variance" give the same relative allocation \(n_i\propto w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}\); only the scaling constant differs. Memory phrase: population weight × variability ÷ √unit cost.

Proportional allocation with unequal costs

Fixed cost (D14): \(C=C_0+n\sum w_iC_i\), so \(n=\dfrac{C^*-C_0}{\sum_iw_iC_i}\) and \(n_i=w_i\dfrac{C^*-C_0}{\sum_jw_jC_j}\). It ignores differences in \(S_i\); it only spreads the affordable sample by size.

Fixed variance (D15): \(V_0=\frac1n\sum w_iS_i^2\), so \(n=\dfrac{\sum_iw_iS_i^2}{V_0}\) and \(n_i=w_i\dfrac{\sum_jw_jS_j^2}{V_0}\). The slides call this fixed-variance proportional result Bowley's allocation.

Special cases: one rule contains the others (D13)

If all \(C_i=C\): \(n_i\propto w_iS_i\), which is Neyman. If in addition all \(S_i=S\): \(n_i\propto w_i\), which is proportional.

\[\text{Cost-optimum}\ \xrightarrow{C_i\ \text{equal}}\ \text{Neyman}\ \xrightarrow{S_i\ \text{equal}}\ \text{Proportional}\]

Practical implementation and cautions

Workflow: population frame → strata → \((N_i,S_i,C_i)\) → allocation → \((n_i)\) → sample selection. Preliminary information comes from previous surveys, pilots, administrative records, census data, expert or field-cost information.

  • \(n_i\) must be an integer; each stratum may need a minimum sample size.
  • Nonresponse may differ across strata; costs may not be linear in \(n_i\).
  • Several survey variables may imply conflicting optima.
  • FPC matters when sampling fractions are large.

So allocation formulas are a principled starting point, not an automatic fieldwork prescription. Take-home: allocate more sample to strata that are larger, more variable, and cheaper to sample.

Variance under different allocations

Assumption. FPC is ignored: \(1-n_i/N_i\approx1\), so \(\V(\bar y_i)\approx S_i^2/n_i\). Allocation rules from the earlier sections are used as given.

General variance

From \(\bar y_{st}=\sum w_i\bar y_i\) and the rule for a linear combination (D3): \(\V=\sum w_i^2\V(\bar y_i)+2\sum_{i<l}w_iw_l\mathrm{Cov}(\bar y_i,\bar y_l)\). Independence kills the covariances:

\[V\equiv\V(\bar y_{st})\approx\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{w_i^2S_i^2}{n_i}\]

Larger \(w_i\) or \(S_i\) raises the contribution; larger \(n_i\) lowers it. Strategy: final allocation formula → substitute into \(V\) → simplify.

AllocationSubstitutionResult
Equal (D10)\(n_i=n/k\)\(V_{eq}=\dfrac kn\sum w_i^2S_i^2\)
Proportional (D7)\(n_i=nw_i\)\(V_{prop}=\dfrac1n\sum w_iS_i^2\)
Neyman (D9)\(n_i=nw_iS_i/A\)\(V_{opt}=\dfrac{A}{n}\sum w_iS_i=\dfrac{A^2}n=\dfrac1n\Big(\sum w_iS_i\Big)^2\)
Unequal cost (D13)\(n_i^*=C_v\frac{w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}}B\)\(V_{\min}=\dfrac{B^2}{C_v}=\dfrac{(\sum w_iS_i\sqrt{C_i})^2}{C_v}\)

Equal allocation ignores size and variability, so it is useful when strata are of similar importance but not generally optimal. In \(V_{prop}\), \(\sum w_iS_i^2\) is the population-weighted average of within-stratum variances, so variance \(=\) (weighted average variance)\(/n\). \(V_{opt}\) is the minimum variance under Neyman allocation.

Proportional versus Neyman

\[V_{prop}-V_{opt}=\frac1n\Big[\sum w_iS_i^2-\Big(\sum w_iS_i\Big)^2\Big]=\frac1n\sum w_i(S_i-S_w)^2\ge0,\quad S_w=\sum w_iS_i\]

The bracket is the weighted variance of the \(S_i\). Hence \(V_{opt}\le V_{prop}\) (proof D16), with equality iff \(S_1=\dots=S_k\). Neyman is never less efficient: it shifts observations toward the more variable strata; if all \(S_i\) are equal it reduces to proportional.

Numerical example. \(N=(5000,3000,2000)\), \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\), \(S=(10,20,15)\), \(n=300\).
Proportional: \(n_i=(150,90,60)\); \(V_{prop}=\frac{0.5(100)+0.3(400)+0.2(225)}{300}=\frac{215}{300}=0.7167\).
Neyman: \(\sum w_iS_i=5+6+3=14\), \(n_i=300(\tfrac5{14},\tfrac6{14},\tfrac3{14})\approx(107.1,128.6,64.3)\to(107,129,64)\); \(V_{opt}=\frac{14^2}{300}=0.6533\).
Equal (extra check): \(V_{eq}=\frac3{300}(25+36+9)=0.7000\).
Reduction: \(0.7167-0.6533=0.0634\), i.e. \(\frac{19}{215}\approx8.8\%\) less variance than proportional.
Special case. With equal costs \(C_i=C\): \(B=\sqrt C\,A\), \(C_v=nC\), so \(V_{\min}=\frac{CA^2}{nC}=\frac{A^2}n=V_{opt}\), consistent with Neyman.

Precision comparison and confidence interval

SRSWOR as benchmark

For the same \(n\), \(\V(\bar y_{SRS})=(1-\frac nN)\frac{S^2}{n}\approx\dfrac{S^2}n=V_{SRS}\), where \(S^2\) is the variance of the whole population.

Within and between variation

\[S^2\approx\sum_{i=1}^kw_iS_i^2+\sum_{i=1}^kw_i(\bar Y_i-\bar Y)^2\ \ \ge\ \ \sum_{i=1}^kw_iS_i^2\]

Exact form (D17): \((N-1)S^2=\sum(N_i-1)S_i^2+\sum N_i(\bar Y_i-\bar Y)^2\). First term: within-stratum variation; second: between-stratum variation. Good stratification removes the between part from the sampling variance.

Chain of comparisons (D18)

\[V_{SRS}-V_{prop}\approx\frac1n\Big[S^2-\sum w_iS_i^2\Big]=\frac1n\sum w_i(\bar Y_i-\bar Y)^2\ge0\ \Rightarrow\ V_{opt}\le V_{prop}\le V_{SRS}\]

For the same total \(n\), optimum allocation gives the smallest variance of the three designs.

Relative gain in precision \((G)\) versus variance reduction \((RV)\) (D19)

\[G_{st}=\frac{V_{SRS}-V_{st}}{V_{st}}\times100\%=\Big(\frac{V_{SRS}}{V_{st}}-1\Big)100\%,\qquad RV=\frac{V_{SRS}-V_{st}}{V_{SRS}}\times100\%\]
\[G_{prop}=\Big[\frac{S^2}{\sum_iw_iS_i^2}-1\Big]100\%,\qquad G_{opt}=\Big[\frac{S^2}{(\sum_iw_iS_i)^2}-1\Big]100\%\]

Precision is \(\propto1/V\), so \(G\) is the percentage increase in precision over SRS. Link: \(G=\frac{RV}{1-RV}\). Example: \(RV=20\%\Rightarrow G=\frac{0.20}{0.80}=25\%\). In the numerical example, \(G_{opt}\) over proportional is \(\frac{215-196}{196}\approx9.7\%\) while the variance reduction is \(8.8\%\).

Confidence interval (CI)

Let \(\hat V_{st}=\sum w_i^2s_i^2/n_i\) (FPC ignored). For large samples \(\bar y_{st}\approx N(\bar Y,\hat V_{st})\) (D20), so an approximate \((1-\alpha)100\%\) CI is

\[\bar y_{st}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat V_{st}}=\bar y_{st}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\,SE(\bar y_{st}),\qquad z_{0.025}=1.96\ (95\%)\]

Because \(V_{opt}\le V_{prop}\le V_{SRS}\), \(SE_{opt}\le SE_{prop}\le SE_{SRS}\): optimum allocation gives the shortest CI, proportional an intermediate one, SRSWOR the widest.

\(V_{prop}\) versus \(V_{SRS}\). Large when stratum means differ a lot and within-stratum variances are small. Comes from how the population is divided.

Stratified estimator of a population proportion

For a qualitative trait let \(Y_{ij}=1\) if unit \(j\) is in the class of interest, else \(0\). With \(A_i\) = class count in the stratum population and \(a_i\) in the sample:

\[P_i=\frac{A_i}{N_i},\quad p_i=\frac{a_i}{n_i},\quad Q_i=1-P_i,\qquad P=\sum w_iP_i,\qquad \hat P_{st}=\sum_{i=1}^kw_ip_i\]

A proportion is the mean of a binary variable, so all mean results carry over; \(\hat P_{st}\) is unbiased for \(P\).

Variance. For binary data \(\V(Y_{ij})=P_iQ_i\) (exactly \(S_i^2=\frac{N_i}{N_i-1}P_iQ_i\)), so ignoring FPC (D21):

\[\V(p_i)\approx\frac{P_iQ_i}{n_i},\qquad \V(\hat P_{st})\approx\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{w_i^2P_iQ_i}{n_i}\]

Proportional allocation \(n_i=nw_i\):

\[\V_{prop}(\hat P_{st})\approx\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^kw_iP_iQ_i\]

Neyman allocation: since \(S_i=\sqrt{P_iQ_i}\), \(n_i\propto w_iS_i\) gives

\[n_i\propto w_i\sqrt{P_iQ_i}\quad\Big(\text{so } n_i=n\frac{w_i\sqrt{P_iQ_i}}{\sum_jw_j\sqrt{P_jQ_j}}\Big)\]

It depends on stratum size (\(w_i\)) and on the variability of the binary trait (\(P_iQ_i\), largest at \(P_i=0.5\)). Substitution also gives \(V_{opt}(\hat P_{st})\approx\frac1n(\sum w_i\sqrt{P_iQ_i})^2\) (extension, same method as D9). In practice \(\hat V=\sum w_i^2(1-f_i)\frac{p_iq_i}{n_i-1}\).

Kish allocation

Kish allocation (KA) is a practical rule for stratified sampling when the relative variability \(S_h\) of the strata is not known but the stratum sizes are. Part 4 writes \(H\) strata indexed by \(h\) (the same roles as \(k\) and \(i\) before): \(N=\sum_{h=1}^HN_h\), \(n=\sum_{h=1}^Hn_h\). The idea: the sample size should increase with stratum size, but not necessarily in direct proportion. A commonly used form is (D22)

\[n_h=n\,\frac{N_h^{\alpha}}{\sum_{g=1}^{H}N_g^{\alpha}},\qquad 0\le\alpha\le1\]

where \(N_h\) = size of stratum \(h\), \(n\) = total sample size, \(\alpha\) = power parameter controlling how strongly the sample depends on stratum size.

Special cases: a continuum

\(\alpha\)RuleResult
\(0\)Equal allocation\(N_h^0=1\Rightarrow n_h=n/H\)
\(0.5\)Intermediate (square-root)\(n_h\propto\sqrt{N_h}\)
\(0<\alpha<1\)Intermediate Kish\(n_h/n\propto N_h^{\alpha}\): large strata get more, but less than their proportional share
\(1\)Proportional allocation\(N_h^1=N_h\Rightarrow n_h=nN_h/N\)
\[\text{Equal allocation}\ \longleftarrow\ \text{Kish allocation}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{Proportional allocation},\qquad 0\le\alpha\le1\]
Example. \(N_h=10000,5000,1000\), \(n=300\), \(\alpha=0.5\): \(N_h^{0.5}=100,\ 70.71,\ 31.62\), sum \(=202.33\). So \(n_1=300\frac{100}{202.33}\approx148\), \(n_2=300\frac{70.71}{202.33}\approx105\), \(n_3=300\frac{31.62}{202.33}\approx47\), giving \((148,105,47)\) with \(148+105+47=300\).
Comparison (computed): proportional gives \((187.5,93.8,18.8)\) and equal gives \((100,100,100)\); Kish gives the small stratum 47 units instead of 19.

Choosing \(\alpha\)

\(\alpha\) controls how strongly the allocation depends on \(N_h\). There is no universally optimal \(\alpha\); \(\alpha=0.5\) is only a useful illustration. To choose it:

  • consider the survey objectives and the need for reliable estimates for small strata;
  • use pilot data or previous surveys to judge stratum variability and the precision of estimates;
  • compare candidate values, e.g. \(\alpha=0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\), in terms of precision and cost;
  • ensure a sufficient minimum sample size in every important stratum.

Why use Kish allocation?

  • stratum sizes \(N_h\) are known;
  • reliable estimates of \(S_h\) are unavailable;
  • proportional allocation gives very small samples to small strata;
  • equal allocation is considered too extreme;
  • a compromise between equal and proportional allocation is wanted.

Sampling fractions (D22). \(n_h/N_h\propto N_h^{\alpha-1}\): for \(\alpha<1\) small strata are sampled at a higher rate than large ones; for \(\alpha=1\) the rate is the same everywhere.

Variance under Kish allocation (D22): \(V_K=\sum_hw_h^2S_h^2/n_h=\dfrac{\sum_gN_g^{\alpha}}{n}\sum_h\dfrac{w_h^2S_h^2}{N_h^{\alpha}}\) (FPC ignored).

Important. Kish allocation is a practical rule; it is not generally the variance-minimising allocation. If reliable \(S_h\) are available, Neyman allocation \(n_h=n\dfrac{N_hS_h}{\sum_gN_gS_g}\) is at least as efficient (D8, D22). Naming note: the slides call this power form Kish allocation and also a "generalized power allocation"; in the literature the same \(N_h^{\alpha}\) idea is also known as Bankier's power allocation, with \(\alpha=0.5\) the square-root allocation. Use the slide's name in exams. After rounding, adjust so that \(\sum n_h=n\).

Post-stratification

Sometimes the grouping variable is not known before sampling. Procedure: (1) draw a sample, (2) collect the survey information, (3) classify sampled units into post-strata, (4) use the known post-stratum population sizes in estimation. In post-stratification (PS) the grouping is applied after selection.

Post-stratification is used when the sample is already selected but information about the population composition becomes available afterwards. The post-strata are mutually exclusive and exhaustive (\(h=1,\dots,H\)); their population sizes \(N_h\) come from an external source (census, administrative data, another reliable source); \(n_h\) is the number of sampled units in post-stratum \(h\). The post-stratification weight is \(W_h=w_h=N_h/N\).

Why use it?

When the sample lacks the desired composition for important characteristics. Example: a health survey records sex, age group, urban/rural residence, region and education, and their population distribution is known from the census. Post-stratification adjusts the sample so that the weighted distribution agrees with the known population distribution.

Post-stratified estimator of a population mean

\[\bar y_h=\frac1{n_h}\sum_{i\in s_h}y_i,\qquad \bar y_{PS}=\sum_{h=1}^HW_h\bar y_h=\sum_{h=1}^H\frac{N_h}{N}\bar y_h,\qquad \bar y=\sum_{h=1}^H\frac{n_h}{n}\bar y_h\]

where \(s_h\) = set of sampled units in post-stratum \(h\), \(\bar y_{PS}\) = post-stratified estimator, \(\bar y\) = ordinary sample mean (D23).

Key difference. The sample share \(n_h/n\) is replaced by the population share \(N_h/N\): the known population composition determines the final weights. Given the realised composition \(\bar y_{PS}\) is unbiased, whereas \(\bar y\) is biased unless the composition matches (D24).
Example. Urban: \(N_h=6000\), \(n_h=80\), \(\bar y_h=24\); Rural: \(N_h=4000\), \(n_h=120\), \(\bar y_h=30\); \(N=10000\). \(W_U=0.60\), \(W_R=0.40\), so \[\bar y_{PS}=0.60(24)+0.40(30)=14.4+12=26.4.\] The ordinary mean is \(\bar y=0.4(24)+0.6(30)=27.6\): rural units are 60% of the sample but only 40% of the population, so \(\bar y\) is pulled up. (The slide stops before giving the value 26.4.)

Post-stratification as a weight adjustment

Let every sampled unit have an initial design weight \(d_i\) (the slide writes \(w_i\), which clashes with the stratum weight, so \(d_i\) is used here). Then (D25)

\[\hat N_h=\sum_{i\in s_h}d_i,\qquad w_i^*=d_i\frac{N_h}{\hat N_h}\ \ (i\in s_h),\qquad A_h=\frac{N_h}{\hat N_h}\]

where \(\hat N_h\) = population size represented by the sample in post-stratum \(h\), \(w_i^*\) = adjusted weight, \(A_h\) = adjustment factor.

  • Under-represented: \(N_h>\hat N_h\Rightarrow A_h>1\), so its sampled units get larger weights.
  • Over-represented: \(N_h<\hat N_h\Rightarrow A_h<1\).
Example. Equal initial weights \(d_i=N/n=50\): \(\hat N_U=80(50)=4000\), \(\hat N_R=120(50)=6000\). So \(A_U=6000/4000=1.5\), \(A_R=4000/6000\approx0.667\); new weights \(75\) (urban) and \(33.3\) (rural), which add up to \(6000\) and \(4000\).

Key points

  • It uses known population information after the sample has been selected.
  • The sample is split into mutually exclusive, exhaustive post-strata, and known population proportions give \(\bar y_{PS}=\sum_hW_h\bar y_h\).
  • It is most useful when the sample composition differs from the known population composition.
  • It can reduce bias and may improve precision when the post-stratification variables are related to the outcome.
  • It does not change which units were selected; it changes how the sampled units contribute to the estimate.

Extension (standard result, not on the slides): the estimator is \(\bar y_{PS}=\sum w_i\bar y_i\) with realised \(n_i\), and approximately \(\V(\bar y_{PS})\approx\frac{1-f}{n}\sum w_iS_i^2+\frac1{n^2}\sum(1-w_i)S_i^2\).

Summary: calculation pattern

  1. Write the general variance. 2. Write the allocation formula. 3. Substitute. 4. Cancel and simplify. 5. Interpret.

Main message: final allocation formula → substitution → variance and precision. Also: the stratified estimator is a weighted sum of stratum means; ignoring FPC, \(\V(\bar y_{st})\approx\sum w_i^2S_i^2/n_i\).
