Premiums

The price of a promise you cannot date. This page takes one policy and prices it three different ways, then shows the single rule behind every premium in the syllabus — the equivalence principle — and what happens when expenses are added on top.

1 · A real-life case, solved

Daniel, aged exactly 40, wants whole-life assurance with a sum assured of \$100,000: whenever he dies, the insurer pays \$100,000 at the end of that year, and the policy then ends.

The insurer prices at \(i=5\%\) with a mortality basis whose commutation functions at age 40 are

\[D_{40}=8{,}000,\qquad M_{40}=1{,}200,\qquad N_{40}=24{,}000.\]

Question: what does the policy cost Daniel — and does the answer change if he pays once, or every year for life, or every year for only 20 years?

Step 1 · What the insurer must pay (the benefit side). From the assurance chapter, \[A_{40}=\frac{M_{40}}{D_{40}}=\frac{1{,}200}{8{,}000}=0.15.\] So the expected present value of the benefit on \$100,000 of cover is \[\text{EPV of benefits}=100{,}000\times0.15=\$15{,}000.\] That \$15,000 is what the insurer must have in hand today. Everything else is about how Daniel hands it over.
Step 2 · Option A — pay it all now (single premium). \[\text{NSP}=\$15{,}000.\] One payment, no future premium, no lapse risk. Done.
Step 3 · Option B — pay every year for life. Premiums are payable annually in advance while Daniel is alive, so they form a whole-life annuity-due. Its EPV is \[\ddot{a}_{40}=\frac{N_{40}}{D_{40}}=\frac{24{,}000}{8{,}000}=3.00.\] If \(P\) is the annual premium, the EPV of what the insurer receives is \(P\times3.00\). Set it equal to the EPV of the benefit: \[P\times3.00=15{,}000\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P=\$5{,}000\ \text{per year for life}.\]
Step 4 · Option C — pay every year for 20 years only, cover continues for life. The premium annuity is now temporary: \[\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{20}|}=\frac{N_{40}-N_{60}}{D_{40}},\qquad\text{with }N_{60}=1{,}500\ \text{say},\] \[=\frac{24{,}000-1{,}500}{8{,}000}=\frac{22{,}500}{8{,}000}=2.8125.\] \[P\times2.8125=15{,}000\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P=\$5{,}333.33\ \text{per year for 20 years}.\]
Way to payFactorPremiumPayments
Single premium\(A_{40}=0.15\)\$15,000 once1
Annual, whole life\(A_{40}/\ddot{a}_{40}=0.05\)\$5,000 / yrwhile alive
Annual, 20 years only\(A_{40}/\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{20}|}=0.0533\)\$5,333.33 / yrat most 20
Step 5 · The check that matters. In every row the insurer collects the same present value today: \[5{,}000\times3.00=15{,}000\quad\checkmark\qquad 5{,}333.33\times2.8125=15{,}000\quad\checkmark\] This is the equivalence principle: at issue, EPV of premiums \(=\) EPV of benefits. The premiums differ only because the payment streams differ.
Daniel pays \$15,000 once, or \$5,000 a year for life, or \$5,333.33 a year for 20 years — three prices, one promise. Paying over fewer years means a bigger annual premium, because the same \$15,000 has to be collected in less time.
Why the insurer is not gambling If Daniel dies at the end of year 3 he has paid 3 premiums of \$5,000 \(=\) \$15,000 and the insurer pays \$100,000 — a nominal loss of \$85,000 on that policy. But across thousands of policies, most people pay for far longer than their share, and the \$15,000 collected at issue on every policy, grown at 5%, funds the claims. The premium is the price of the insurer's certainty that it will pay, not a guess about Daniel.

2 · Definition

The keywords from the case were amount paid by the policyholder, in exchange for protection, once / annually / periodically, net vs gross, and expected present value of premiums = expected present value of benefits. Combined:

Premium The amount paid by the policyholder to the insurer in exchange for insurance protection. It is calculated from mortality, interest, the benefit amount and the timing of payments, and set so that at the issue date the expected present value of the premiums equals the expected present value of the benefits — the principle of equivalence.
Net premium
Uses only mortality, interest and benefits. Excludes expenses, commission and profit. It is the pure cost of the risk.
Gross (office) premium
The premium actually charged: net premium plus loadings for expenses, commission, taxes and profit.
Equivalence principle
At issue: \(\text{EPV}(\text{premiums})=\text{EPV}(\text{benefits})\). With expenses: \(\text{EPV}(\text{office premiums})=\text{EPV}(\text{benefits})+\text{EPV}(\text{expenses})\).
The one-line idea \[\boxed{P=\frac{\text{EPV of benefits}}{\text{EPV of the premium-payment stream}}}\]

3 · The idea in plain words

An insurer does not know when Daniel dies, whether death falls inside a term, or when the benefit will have to be paid. So it cannot simply divide the sum assured by the number of premiums. Instead it does the same thing it did when pricing the benefit — it computes an expected present value — and then balances two such quantities:

  • the EPV of what it will pay out (the benefit), and
  • the EPV of what it will take in (the premium stream).

Set them equal and solve for the premium. That is the whole method. Everything in this chapter is a special case of it.

Three friends, three premiums

Picture three 40-year-olds buying \$100,000 of cover on the same day:

  • Amara chooses whole life — the insurer will certainly pay, but at an unknown, probably distant, date. Premium: moderate.
  • Kofi chooses a 10-year term — the insurer probably pays nothing at all, because most 40-year-olds survive ten years. Premium: small.
  • Desta chooses a 10-year endowment — the insurer is certain to pay, and likely sooner rather than later. Premium: largest.

The lesson: the premium is the price of the insurer's certainty that it will pay. The more certain the payment, and the sooner it comes, the higher the price today.

Why \(D_{x}\) vanishes

Both the benefit and the premium annuity are expressed with commutation functions over the same denominator \(D_{x}\): \[A_{x}=\frac{M_{x}}{D_{x}},\qquad \ddot{a}_{x}=\frac{N_{x}}{D_{x}}.\] Dividing one by the other, \[P=\frac{A_{x}}{\ddot{a}_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}/D_{x}}{N_{x}/D_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}}{N_{x}}.\] The \(D_{x}\) cancels because both sides of the equivalence are measured per person alive at age \(x\). So the premium is just discounted deaths divided by discounted premium payments. Look for this cancellation in every premium formula — it is why the answers come out as clean ratios like \(1{,}200/24{,}000=0.05\).

A sanity check to use every time An annual premium should be a small fraction of the sum assured — typically a few percent. If you ever compute an annual premium that is 20%, 50% or 76% of the sum assured, you have almost certainly divided by a premium annuity that is far too small. Recheck the \(N\)-values.

4 · Classifying premiums

ClassificationTypeMeaning
By frequencySingle premiumOne payment at the start of the policy
Annual premiumOne payment each year while the policy is in force
Periodic premiumMonthly, quarterly or semi-annual payments
By contentNet premiumMortality + interest + benefits only
Gross (office) premiumNet premium + expenses + commission + profit loading
By timingIn advancePaid at the start of each period — an annuity-due, \(\ddot{a}_{x}\)
In arrearsPaid at the end of each period — an annuity-immediate, \(a_{x}\)

Traditional annual life insurance premiums are modelled as payable in advance while the policyholder is alive — which is exactly why the premium annuity is \(\ddot{a}\), not \(a\). If premiums were payable in arrears, the equivalence would read \(P\,a_{x}=A_{x}\) instead.

Limited-payment whole life A variant worth naming separately: the benefit lasts for life, but the premiums stop after \(n\) years. The benefit side stays \(A_{x}\); only the premium annuity shrinks to \(\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}\). Fewer payments \(\Rightarrow\) larger each. That is Option C in the scenario.

5 · Single premium

A single premium is paid once, at the beginning of the policy. Because there are no future premiums to discount or to depend on survival, the single premium is simply the EPV of the benefit itself:

\[\boxed{\text{NSP}=S\cdot A_{x}}\]

for a whole-life assurance with sum assured \(S\). For other products, replace \(A_{x}\) by the appropriate benefit factor:

ProductNet single premium for sum assured \(S\)
Whole-life assurance\(S\,A_{x}=S\,M_{x}/D_{x}\)
\(n\)-year term assurance\(S\,A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=S\,(M_{x}-M_{x+n})/D_{x}\)
\(n\)-year pure endowment\(S\,{}_{n}E_{x}=S\,D_{x+n}/D_{x}\)
\(n\)-year endowment assurance\(S\,A_{x:\overline{n}|}=S\,(M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n})/D_{x}\)

Advantage: the policyholder never has to make another payment, and there is no lapse risk for the insurer. Disadvantage: it is a large lump sum, and it gives up the mortality cross-subsidy that makes annual premiums attractive to the buyer.

6 · Annual premium and the equivalence principle

Let \(P\) be the annual net premium, payable in advance while the policyholder is alive. Then

\[\text{EPV of premiums}=P\times(\text{EPV of the premium-payment annuity}).\]

Equivalence sets this equal to the EPV of benefits, so

\[\boxed{P=\frac{\text{EPV of benefits}}{\text{EPV of the premium-payment annuity}}}\]

and in commutation form the \(D_{x}\) in numerator and denominator cancels, leaving a ratio of \(M\)'s and \(N\)'s. The table below is the whole chapter on one screen.

Whole life\(A_{x}=M_{x}/D_{x}\)\(P\,\ddot{a}_{x}=P\,N_{x}/D_{x}\)\(M_{x}/N_{x}\)
\(n\)-year term\(A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=(M_{x}-M_{x+n})/D_{x}\)\(P\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=P\,(N_{x}-N_{x+n})/D_{x}\)\(\dfrac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}}{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}\)
\(n\)-year endowment\(A_{x:\overline{n}|}=(M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n})/D_{x}\)\(P\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=P\,(N_{x}-N_{x+n})/D_{x}\)\(\dfrac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n}}{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}\)
Limited-payment whole life (\(n\) yrs)\(A_{x}=M_{x}/D_{x}\)\(P\,\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=P\,(N_{x}-N_{x+n})/D_{x}\)\(\dfrac{M_{x}}{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}\)
Reading the table The numerator is always the benefit side — deaths (\(M\)) and, for the endowment, the survivors (\(D_{x+n}\)). The denominator is always the premium side — the \(N\)-terms covering the years in which premiums are actually collected. Change either one and the premium changes, exactly as you would expect.

7 · Gross (office) premium

The net premium pays for benefits only. A real insurer also has to fund:

Expense typeWhen incurredExamples
InitialAt policy issueMedical examination, underwriting, policy preparation, initial commission
RenewalThroughout the policyAdministration, premium collection, policy servicing
ClaimWhen a claim is settledClaim investigation and settlement costs

Adding these to the benefit gives the gross or office premium. The equivalence principle simply gains a term:

\[\boxed{\text{EPV of office premiums}=\text{EPV of benefits}+\text{EPV of expenses}}\]

It is not generally correct to say "gross \(=\) net \(+\) a fixed amount", because expenses may be proportional to the premium, to the sum assured, or to the number of policies.

The simplest loading

If the net annual premium is \$5,000 and the insurer adds a loading of \(20\%\), then \[\text{loading}=0.20\times5{,}000=\$1{,}000,\qquad \text{gross premium}=\$6{,}000.\] This is a simplification: it ignores the timing of expenses and assumes they are proportional to premium.

Expenses proportional to premium — the premium appears on both sides

Suppose a proportional expense of \(10\%\) of each premium is charged. If \(P^{g}\) is the office premium and \(\ddot{a}\) the EPV of one dollar of premiums, then:

Received: \(P^{g}\ddot{a}\).
Proportional expenses: \(0.10\times P^{g}\ddot{a}\).
Equivalence: \[P^{g}\ddot{a}=(\text{EPV benefits})+(\text{EPV fixed expenses})+0.10\,P^{g}\ddot{a}.\]
Solve. Collect the \(P^{g}\) terms: \[P^{g}\ddot{a}(1-0.10)=\text{EPV benefits}+\text{EPV fixed expenses}\] \[\boxed{P^{g}=\frac{\text{EPV benefits}+\text{EPV fixed expenses}}{\ddot{a}\,(1-c)}}\] where \(c\) is the proportion of each premium consumed by expenses.

Numerical version. With EPV benefits \$15,000, EPV fixed expenses \$1,000, \(\ddot{a}=3.00\) and \(c=0.10\): \[P^{g}=\frac{15{,}000+1{,}000}{3.00\times0.90}=\frac{16{,}000}{2.70}=\$5{,}925.93\ \text{per year}.\] Compare with the net premium of \$5,000: the loading is \$925.93 per year — more than the naive \(10\%\) of \$5,000, because the commission is charged on the larger gross premium.

8 · Worked examples

All examples use the same age-40 basis: \(D_{40}=8{,}000\), \(M_{40}=1{,}200\), \(N_{40}=24{,}000\), \(M_{50}=720\), \(N_{50}=6{,}000\), \(D_{50}=3{,}000\), \(N_{60}=1{,}500\).

Example 1 · Net single premium

Whole-life assurance of \$100,000 issued at age 40. Find the net single premium.

\[\text{NSP}=100{,}000\,A_{40}=100{,}000\times\frac{1{,}200}{8{,}000}=100{,}000\times0.15=\$15{,}000.\]
\$15,000 once, and the policy is fully paid up.

Example 2 · Whole-life annual premium

\[\ddot{a}_{40}=\frac{N_{40}}{D_{40}}=\frac{24{,}000}{8{,}000}=3.00,\qquad P=\frac{A_{40}}{\ddot{a}_{40}}=\frac{0.15}{3.00}=0.05.\] \[\text{Annual premium}=100{,}000\times0.05=\$5{,}000.\]
Commutation shortcut. \[P=\frac{M_{40}}{N_{40}}=\frac{1{,}200}{24{,}000}=0.05\quad\checkmark\]
The \(D_{40}\) has vanished. Note \(5{,}000\times3.00=15{,}000\): the EPVs balance.

Example 3 · Limited-payment whole life (premiums for 20 years)

\[\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{20}|}=\frac{N_{40}-N_{60}}{D_{40}}=\frac{24{,}000-1{,}500}{8{,}000}=2.8125.\] \[P=\frac{A_{40}}{\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{20}|}}=\frac{0.15}{2.8125}=0.053333\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\$5{,}333.33\ \text{per year for 20 years}.\]
Commutation shortcut. \(P=\dfrac{M_{40}}{N_{40}-N_{60}}=\dfrac{1{,}200}{22{,}500}=0.053333\). ✓
Higher than \$5,000 because the same \$15,000 of EPV is collected over fewer payment years. Total nominal outlay if Daniel lives past 60: \(20\times5{,}333.33=\$106{,}666.67\) — but only \$15,000 in present-value terms.

Example 4 · 10-year term assurance premium

\[A^{1}_{40:\overline{10}|}=\frac{M_{40}-M_{50}}{D_{40}}=\frac{1{,}200-720}{8{,}000}=\frac{480}{8{,}000}=0.06.\] \[\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{10}|}=\frac{N_{40}-N_{50}}{D_{40}}=\frac{24{,}000-6{,}000}{8{,}000}=\frac{18{,}000}{8{,}000}=2.25.\] \[P=\frac{0.06}{2.25}=0.026667\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\$2{,}666.67\ \text{per year for 10 years}.\]
Shortcut. \(P=\dfrac{M_{40}-M_{50}}{N_{40}-N_{50}}=\dfrac{480}{18{,}000}=0.026667\). ✓
A 10-year term premium of \$2,667 for \$100,000 of cover is far below the whole-life figure — exactly Kofi's "cheap" policy from the story. The insurer probably never pays.

Example 5 · 10-year endowment assurance premium

\[A_{40:\overline{10}|}=\frac{M_{40}-M_{50}+D_{50}}{D_{40}}=\frac{1{,}200-720+3{,}000}{8{,}000}=\frac{3{,}480}{8{,}000}=0.435.\] \[P=\frac{A_{40:\overline{10}|}}{\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{10}|}}=\frac{0.435}{2.25}=0.193333\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\$19{,}333.33\ \text{per year for 10 years}.\]
Shortcut. \(P=\dfrac{M_{40}-M_{50}+D_{50}}{N_{40}-N_{50}}=\dfrac{3{,}480}{18{,}000}=0.193333\). ✓
Desta's "certain" policy is by far the dearest: \$19,333 a year against \$2,667 for the term policy. The extra \$16,667 a year is the price of the guaranteed payout at age 50.

Example 6 · Gross premium with a proportional loading

The whole-life policy of Example 2 has EPV benefits \$15,000. Fixed expenses have EPV \$1,000; commission and premium-related expenses absorb \(10\%\) of every premium; \(\ddot{a}_{40}=3.00\). Find the office premium.

Set up the equivalence. \[3P^{g}=15{,}000+1{,}000+0.10(3P^{g})\]
Collect terms. \[3P^{g}-0.3P^{g}=16{,}000\quad\Longrightarrow\quad2.7P^{g}=16{,}000\]
Solve. \[P^{g}=\$5{,}925.93\ \text{per year}.\]
Net \$5,000 → gross \$5,925.93. The implied loading is \(18.5\%\) of the net premium, not \(10\%\) — because the \(10\%\) is charged on the gross, which is itself being solved for.

10 · Derivations

D1 · The equivalence principle and the general premium formula

At the issue date the insurer's expected present outflow must equal its expected present inflow. If \(P\) is the premium per payment and \(\ddot{a}\) (or \(a\)) is the EPV of the premium-payment stream per unit of premium, then

\[\text{EPV of premiums}=P\,\ddot{a},\qquad\text{EPV of benefits}=A,\]

and equivalence gives \(P\ddot{a}=A\), i.e.

\[\boxed{P=\frac{A}{\ddot{a}}}.\]

This is an expectation on both sides: the benefit is a random variable whose expectation is \(A\), the premium stream is a random variable (it stops at death) whose expectation is \(P\ddot{a}\).

D2 · Whole life: \(P=M_{x}/N_{x}\) and why \(D_{x}\) cancels

Substitute the commutation forms:

\[P=\frac{A_{x}}{\ddot{a}_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}/D_{x}}{N_{x}/D_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}}{D_{x}}\cdot\frac{D_{x}}{N_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}}{N_{x}}.\]

The cancellation is not a coincidence: both quantities are "per life aged \(x\)", so the same scaling factor \(D_{x}\) sits in both. The premium is the ratio of discounted deaths to discounted premium payments.

D3 · Limited-payment whole life

Benefit unchanged (\(A_{x}=M_{x}/D_{x}\)); premiums stop after \(n\) years, so the premium annuity is temporary:

\[\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}{D_{x}}.\] \[P=\frac{M_{x}/D_{x}}{(N_{x}-N_{x+n})/D_{x}}=\boxed{\frac{M_{x}}{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}}.\]

As \(n\to\infty\), \(N_{x+n}\to0\) and we recover \(M_{x}/N_{x}\). Since \(N_{x}-N_{x+n}larger than the whole-life one.

D4 · Term assurance

Benefit \(A^{1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=(M_{x}-M_{x+n})/D_{x}\); premiums are paid for at most \(n\) years while alive, so the premium annuity is the same temporary one:

\[\ddot{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\frac{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}{D_{x}}.\] \[P=\frac{(M_{x}-M_{x+n})/D_{x}}{(N_{x}-N_{x+n})/D_{x}}=\boxed{\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}}{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}}.\]

Note there is no \(D_{x+n}\) term: a term assurance pays nothing on survival.

D5 · Endowment assurance

Benefit \(A_{x:\overline{n}|}=(M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n})/D_{x}\); premiums paid for at most \(n\) years:

\[P=\frac{(M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n})/D_{x}}{(N_{x}-N_{x+n})/D_{x}}=\boxed{\frac{M_{x}-M_{x+n}+D_{x+n}}{N_{x}-N_{x+n}}}.\]

It exceeds the term premium by \(D_{x+n}/(N_{x}-N_{x+n})\) — the price of the guaranteed payout on survival.

D6 · Gross premium with expenses proportional to premium

Let \(P^{g}\) be the office premium, \(A\) the EPV of benefits, \(F\) the EPV of fixed expenses, and \(c\) the fraction of each premium consumed by premium-related expenses. Then

\[P^{g}\ddot{a}=A+F+c\,P^{g}\ddot{a}.\]

Move the \(c\)-term to the left:

\[P^{g}\ddot{a}(1-c)=A+F\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{P^{g}=\frac{A+F}{\ddot{a}(1-c)}}.\]

The premium appears on both sides whenever an expense is proportional to it — which is exactly why office premiums cannot be found by adding a fixed amount to the net premium.

D7 · Premiums in advance vs in arrears

In advance (the usual convention): premiums fall at times \(0,1,2,\dots\) while the life is alive, so their EPV is \(P\,\ddot{a}_{x}=P\,N_{x}/D_{x}\). Equivalence: \(P\ddot{a}_{x}=A_{x}\Rightarrow P=M_{x}/N_{x}\).

In arrears: premiums fall at times \(1,2,\dots\) while alive, so their EPV is \(P\,a_{x}=P\,N_{x+1}/D_{x}\). Then

\[P=\frac{A_{x}}{a_{x}}=\frac{M_{x}}{N_{x+1}},\]

which is larger than \(M_{x}/N_{x}\), because the first premium is deferred a year while the benefit is not.

D8 · Why premium calculation is an expected-value problem
IngredientRandom?Handled by
Future benefitYes — paid only on death (or survival)\(A_{x}\), the EPV of the benefit
Number of premiums paidYes — stops at death\(\ddot{a}_{x}\), the EPV of the premium stream
Time valueNo — fixed \(v\)Discount factor inside both

Both sides of the equivalence are expectations of random variables driven by the same future lifetime \(T_{x}\). Setting them equal gives a premium that is fair in expectation; the law of large numbers then makes the aggregate outcome predictable even though each individual policy is not.
