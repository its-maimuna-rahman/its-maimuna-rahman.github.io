Contrasts & Multiple Comparisons

AST204 Design and Analysis of Experiments I · Lectures 12–15 · Contrasts (t test, F test, confidence interval), orthogonal contrasts, Scheffé's method, Tukey's test, Fisher's LSD, Dunnett's test, one-tailed versions. Corrections to the slides are marked in red boxes.

Big Picture

Suppose the ANOVA F test rejects \(H_0\). We now know that some treatment means differ, but not which. Further comparisons among treatment means are called multiple comparison methods. They are made using treatment totals or averages, and many of them use the idea of a contrast. The right method depends on the question.

What do you want to compare?Method
One or a few comparisons planned before the experiment (e.g. \(\mu_3=\mu_4\))Contrast t-test or F-test; a set of \(a-1\) orthogonal contrasts
Any and all contrasts, even ones chosen after looking at the dataScheffé
All pairs of meansTukey (preferred) or Fisher's LSD
Each treatment against a controlDunnett

Contrasts

A contrast is a linear combination of treatment means

\[ \Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{a}c_i\mu_i,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{a}c_i=0 \]

The contrast coefficients \(c_1,\dots,c_a\) must sum to zero. Hypotheses about contrasts have the form \(H_0:\sum c_i\mu_i=0\) versus \(H_1:\sum c_i\mu_i\neq0\).

Plasma etching. The ANOVA rejected \(H_0\), so some power settings give different mean etch rates. Which ones?
We may suspect from the start that 200 W and 220 W give the same etch rate: \(H_0:\mu_3=\mu_4\), or \(H_0:\mu_3-\mu_4=0\). The coefficients are \(c_1=c_2=0,\ c_3=+1,\ c_4=-1\).
We may also suspect that the average of the lowest two powers equals the average of the highest two: \(H_0:\mu_1+\mu_2=\mu_3+\mu_4\), or \(H_0:\mu_1+\mu_2-\mu_3-\mu_4=0\). The coefficients are \(c_1=c_2=+1,\ c_3=c_4=-1\).

To compare treatment 1 with the average of treatments 2 and 3, use \(\Gamma=-\mu_1+0.5\mu_2+0.5\mu_3\), coefficients \((-1,\,0.5,\,0.5)\). The coefficients \((1,-0.5,-0.5)\) or \((-6,3,3)\) give the same information, only rescaled.

Testing and Estimating a Contrast

Write the contrast in terms of the treatment averages:

\[ C=\sum_{i=1}^{a}c_i\bar y_{i\cdot},\qquad V(C)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{a}c_i^2 \]

(equal sample sizes). If \(H_0\) is true, \(C\big/\sqrt{\tfrac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum c_i^2}\) has the \(N(0,1)\) distribution. Replacing \(\sigma^2\) by \(MS_E\) gives the t-test:

\[ t_0=\frac{\sum c_i\bar y_{i\cdot}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{MS_E}{n}\sum c_i^2}},\qquad\text{reject }H_0\text{ if }|t_0|\gt t_{\alpha/2,\,N-a} \]

The square of a \(t\) variable with \(\nu\) df is an \(F\) variable with 1 and \(\nu\) df, so the F-test is

\[ F_0=t_0^2=\frac{\Big(\sum c_i\bar y_{i\cdot}\Big)^2}{\dfrac{MS_E}{n}\sum c_i^2},\qquad\text{reject }H_0\text{ if }F_0\gt F_{\alpha,1,N-a} \]

This can also be written \(F_0=\dfrac{MS_C}{MS_E}=\dfrac{SS_C/1}{MS_E}\), where the single-degree-of-freedom contrast sum of squares is

\[ SS_C=\frac{\Big(\sum c_i\bar y_{i\cdot}\Big)^2}{\dfrac1n\sum c_i^2} \]

Confidence interval for a contrast

Since \(E(C)=\sum c_i\mu_i\) and \(V(C)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum c_i^2\), the \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) confidence interval for \(\Gamma\) is

\[ C\pm t_{\alpha/2,\,N-a}\sqrt{\frac{MS_E}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{a}c_i^2} \]

If the interval includes zero, we cannot reject \(H_0:\sum c_i\mu_i=0\). (For unequal sample sizes replace \(\frac1n\sum c_i^2\) by \(\sum c_i^2/n_i\).)

Worked with the formulas: \(H_0:\mu_3=\mu_4\) in the etch data (\(c=(0,0,1,-1)\), \(\bar y_{3\cdot}=625.4\), \(\bar y_{4\cdot}=707.0\), \(MS_E=333.70\), \(n=5\)).
\(C=625.4-707.0=-81.6\); standard error \(\sqrt{\dfrac{333.70\,(2)}{5}}=11.55\).
\(t_0=\dfrac{-81.6}{11.55}=-7.06\), and \(|t_0|\gt t_{0.025,16}=2.120\), so reject \(H_0\). Also \(F_0=t_0^2=49.88\).
95% CI: \(-81.6\pm2.120(11.55)=-81.6\pm24.49\), that is \((-106.09,\,-57.11)\). It does not include 0.

Orthogonal Contrasts

Two contrasts with coefficients \(\{c_i\}\) and \(\{d_i\}\) are orthogonal if

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{a}c_id_i=0 \]

For \(a\) treatments, a set of \(a-1\) mutually orthogonal contrasts partitions the treatment sum of squares into \(a-1\) independent, single-degree-of-freedom pieces, so the tests on them are independent:

\[ SS_{Treatments}=SS_{C_1}+SS_{C_2}+\cdots+SS_{C_{a-1}} \]
  • There are many possible contrasts, but at most \(a-1\) can form a complete mutually orthogonal set. After that, no further contrast is orthogonal to all of them.
  • The set is not unique. The nature of the experiment should decide which comparisons are of interest, and they should answer scientifically meaningful questions.
Control example. \(a=3\): treatment 1 is the control, treatments 2 and 3 are two levels of the factor.

Etch contrasts: values and sums of squares

Means: \(\bar y_{1\cdot}=551.2,\ \bar y_{2\cdot}=587.4,\ \bar y_{3\cdot}=625.4,\ \bar y_{4\cdot}=707.0\); \(n=5\); \(MS_E=333.70\).

\[C_1=551.2-587.4=-36.2\] \[SS_{C_1}=\frac{(-36.2)^2}{\frac15(2)}=\frac{1310.44}{0.4}=3276.10\]

\[C_3=625.4-707.0=-81.6\] \[SS_{C_3}=\frac{(-81.6)^2}{\frac15(2)}=\frac{6658.56}{0.4}=16646.40\]

They partition the treatment sum of squares:

\[3276.10+46948.05+16646.40=66870.55=SS_{Treatments}\]

Each \(F_0=SS_C/MS_E\):

\[F_0(C_1)=\frac{3276.10}{333.70}=9.82,\quad F_0(C_2)=\frac{46948.05}{333.70}=140.69\] \[F_0(C_3)=\frac{16646.40}{333.70}=49.88\]
Checking that the etch contrasts are orthogonal

Coefficients: \(C_1:(1,-1,0,0)\), \(C_2:(1,1,-1,-1)\), \(C_3:(0,0,1,-1)\). Orthogonal means the sum of products is zero for every pair:

\[C_1\cdot C_2=(1)(1)+(-1)(1)+(0)(-1)+(0)(-1)=0\] \[C_1\cdot C_3=(1)(0)+(-1)(0)+(0)(1)+(0)(-1)=0\] \[C_2\cdot C_3=(1)(0)+(1)(0)+(-1)(1)+(-1)(-1)=0\]

Three mutually orthogonal contrasts with \(a-1=3\): a complete set, which is why their sums of squares add up exactly to \(SS_{Treatments}\).

Scheffé's Method for All Contrasts

Often we do not know in advance which contrasts we want, or we want more than \(a-1\) of them, which orthogonal contrasts cannot handle. Scheffé (1953) proposed a method for comparing any and all contrasts, including ones chosen after looking at the data.

For contrasts \(\Gamma_u=c_{1u}\mu_1+\cdots+c_{au}\mu_a\), estimated by \(C_u=c_{1u}\bar y_{1\cdot}+\cdots+c_{au}\bar y_{a\cdot}\), \(u=1,\dots,m\):

\[ S_{C_u}=\sqrt{MS_E\sum_{i=1}^{a}\frac{c_{iu}^2}{n_i}} \]
\[ S_{\alpha,u}=S_{C_u}\sqrt{(a-1)F_{\alpha,\,a-1,\,N-a}} \]

To test \(H_0:\Gamma_u=0\), reject if \(|C_u|\gt S_{\alpha,u}\). The simultaneous confidence intervals are

\[ C_u-S_{\alpha,u}\le\Gamma_u\le C_u+S_{\alpha,u} \]

They are simultaneous because \(P(\text{all intervals contain their true contrasts})\ge1-\alpha\). The \(1-\alpha\) is already the family-wise level, so you do not multiply \(\alpha\) by the number of contrasts.

The price of freedom. When many contrasts are looked at, some look large just by chance. Scheffé therefore uses the larger critical value \(\sqrt{(a-1)F_{\alpha,a-1,N-a}}\), which comes from the overall ANOVA F distribution and covers the most extreme contrast that could arise. This is the "price" for being free to examine any contrast while keeping the family-wise Type I error rate at \(\alpha\).
Example (etch data, \(\alpha=0.01\)). \(\Gamma_1=\mu_1+\mu_2-\mu_3-\mu_4\) and \(\Gamma_2=\mu_1-\mu_4\).
\(C_1=551.2+587.4-625.4-707.0=-193.80\), \(C_2=551.2-707.0=-155.8\).
\(S_{C_1}=\sqrt{\dfrac{333.70(1+1+1+1)}{5}}=16.34\), \(S_{C_2}=\sqrt{\dfrac{333.70(1+1)}{5}}=11.55\), and \(F_{0.01,3,16}=5.29\).
\(S_{0.01,1}=16.34\sqrt{3(5.29)}=65.09\), \(S_{0.01,2}=11.55\sqrt{3(5.29)}\approx46.0\).
\(|C_1|=193.80\gt65.09\): reject \(H_0:\Gamma_1=0\); settings 1 and 2 as a group differ from settings 3 and 4 as a group.
\(|C_2|=155.8\gt46.0\): reject \(H_0:\Gamma_2=0\); settings 1 and 4 differ.
Note: the slide prints \(S_{0.01,2}=45.97\). Computing \(11.55\times\sqrt{3(5.29)}=11.55\times3.984\) gives about \(46.0\). The decision does not change.
Scheffé example, step by step

Etch data, \(\alpha=0.01\), \(a=4\), \(N-a=16\), \(F_{0.01,3,16}=5.29\).

\[\sqrt{(a-1)F}=\sqrt{3(5.29)}=\sqrt{15.87}=3.984\]

Contrast \(\Gamma_1=\mu_1+\mu_2-\mu_3-\mu_4\), \(\sum c_i^2/n_i=4/5\):

\[S_{C_1}=\sqrt{\frac{333.70(1+1+1+1)}{5}}=\sqrt{266.96}=16.34\] \[S_{0.01,1}=16.34\times3.984=65.09\]

Contrast \(\Gamma_2=\mu_1-\mu_4\), \(\sum c_i^2/n_i=2/5\):

\[S_{C_2}=\sqrt{\frac{333.70(1+1)}{5}}=\sqrt{133.48}=11.55\] \[S_{0.01,2}=11.55\times3.984\approx46.0\]

Decisions: \(|C_1|=193.80\gt65.09\) reject; \(|C_2|=155.8\gt46.0\) reject.

Why Scheffé instead of the usual t or F test?

For a single preplanned contrast, the usual t or F test is appropriate. But if many contrasts are considered, especially after examining the data, testing each one separately increases the chance of a Type I error. Scheffé controls the family-wise error rate over all possible contrasts, though this protection makes it more conservative.

All Pairwise Comparisons: Tukey and Fisher's LSD

Why Tukey instead of Scheffé for pairs?

Scheffé can compare all pairs of means, but it protects all possible contrasts, so it is conservative. Tukey's test looks only at pairwise comparisons, so it uses a smaller critical difference and has more power to detect differences between individual means, while still controlling the family-wise Type I error rate. When we only want every pair of means, Tukey is generally preferable.

Tukey's test

Tukey (1953) gave a procedure whose overall significance level is exactly \(\alpha\) when the sample sizes are equal. It can also give confidence intervals for all pairs of means with simultaneous level \(100(1-\alpha)\%\). It controls the experimentwise ("family") error rate. After an ANOVA that rejects \(H_0\), test \(H_0:\mu_i=\mu_j\) versus \(H_1:\mu_i\neq\mu_j\) for all \(i\neq j\), using the studentized range statistic

\[ q=\frac{\bar y_{\max}-\bar y_{\min}}{\sqrt{MS_E/n}} \]

where \(\bar y_{\max}\) and \(\bar y_{\min}\) are the largest and smallest of the \(p\) sample means. Appendix Table VII gives \(q_\alpha(p,f)\), the upper \(\alpha\) points of \(q\) (\(f\) = error df). Two means differ significantly if their difference exceeds

\[ T_\alpha=q_\alpha(a,f)\sqrt{\frac{MS_E}{n}} \]

Equivalent simultaneous intervals: \(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{j\cdot}\pm q_\alpha(a,f)\sqrt{MS_E/n}\) for all \(i\neq j\). For unequal sample sizes (the Tukey–Kramer procedure):

\[ T_\alpha=\frac{q_\alpha(a,f)}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{MS_E\Big(\frac1{n_i}+\frac1{n_j}\Big)} \]
Example (etch data, \(\alpha=0.05\)). \(f=16\), \(q_{0.05}(4,16)=4.05\), so \(T_{0.05}=4.05\sqrt{\dfrac{333.70}{5}}=33.09\). Any two means differing by more than 33.09 are significantly different.
\(\bar y_{1\cdot}-\bar y_{2\cdot}=-36.20^*\), \(\bar y_{1\cdot}-\bar y_{3\cdot}=-74.20^*\), \(\bar y_{1\cdot}-\bar y_{4\cdot}=-155.8^*\), \(\bar y_{2\cdot}-\bar y_{3\cdot}=-38.0^*\), \(\bar y_{2\cdot}-\bar y_{4\cdot}=-119.6^*\), \(\bar y_{3\cdot}-\bar y_{4\cdot}=-81.60^*\) (\(^*\) = significant).
All differences exceed 33.09, so every power setting has a mean etch rate that differs from every other.
Tukey's critical difference

Etch data: \(a=4\), \(f=N-a=16\), \(q_{0.05}(4,16)=4.05\), \(MS_E=333.70\), \(n=5\):

\[\sqrt{\frac{MS_E}{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{333.70}{5}}=\sqrt{66.74}=8.169\] \[T_{0.05}=q_{0.05}(4,16)\sqrt{\frac{MS_E}{n}}=4.05\times8.169=33.09\]

Tukey–Kramer for unequal sizes. When \(n_i=n_j=n\) it reduces to the equal-size formula:

\[\frac{q}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{MS_E\Big(\frac1n+\frac1n\Big)}=\frac{q}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{\frac{2MS_E}{n}}=q\sqrt{\frac{MS_E}{n}}\]

The studentized range is largest when the two means are the largest and smallest, so using \(q_\alpha(a,f)\) protects all pairs at once.

Fisher's Least Significant Difference (LSD)

Fisher's method controls the error rate \(\alpha\) for each individual comparison, but not the experimentwise (family) error rate. It uses the t statistic for \(H_0:\mu_i=\mu_j\):

\[ t_0=\frac{\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{j\cdot}}{\sqrt{MS_E\Big(\dfrac1{n_i}+\dfrac1{n_j}\Big)}} \]

For a two-sided alternative, \(\mu_i\) and \(\mu_j\) differ significantly if \(|\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{j\cdot}|\gt LSD\), where

\[ LSD=t_{\alpha/2,\,N-a}\sqrt{MS_E\Big(\frac1{n_i}+\frac1{n_j}\Big)}\quad\xrightarrow{\ \text{balanced}\ }\quad LSD=t_{\alpha/2,\,N-a}\sqrt{\frac{2MS_E}{n}} \]
Example 3.8. \(LSD=t_{0.025,16}\sqrt{\dfrac{2(333.70)}{5}}=2.120\sqrt{133.48}=24.49\). The six differences (36.2, 74.2, 155.8, 38.0, 119.6, 81.6) all exceed 24.49, so all pairs of means differ significantly.

Which pairwise method?

There is no clear-cut answer, and statisticians disagree. Carmer and Swanson (1973), using Monte Carlo studies, reported that Fisher's LSD is effective at detecting true differences when used after a significant ANOVA F test at the 5% level, but it does not generally control the family-wise error rate. Tukey's method does control it, so many statisticians prefer Tukey for all pairwise comparisons.

Comparing Treatments with a Control: Dunnett

Sometimes one treatment is a control and we only compare each of the other \(a-1\) treatments with it, so only \(a-1\) comparisons are needed. Dunnett (1964) modified the usual t-test for this. With treatment \(a\) as the control, test \(H_0:\mu_i=\mu_a\) versus \(H_1:\mu_i\neq\mu_a\), \(i=1,\dots,a-1\). Reject \(H_0\) if

\[ |\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{a\cdot}|\gt d_\alpha(a-1,f)\sqrt{MS_E\Big(\frac1{n_i}+\frac1{n_a}\Big)} \]

\(d_\alpha(a-1,f)\) is Dunnett's critical value (Appendix Table VIII) and \(f=N-a\). \(\alpha\) is the joint significance level of all \(a-1\) tests: Dunnett controls the family-wise error rate for the comparisons with the control, not for all pairwise comparisons.

Example 3.9. Etch data with treatment 4 as the control: \(a=4\), \(a-1=3\), \(f=16\), \(n=5\). At 5%, \(d_{0.05}(3,16)=2.59\), so the critical difference is \(D=2.59\sqrt{\dfrac{2(333.70)}{5}}=29.92\).
1 vs 4: \(551.2-707.0=-155.8\); 2 vs 4: \(587.4-707.0=-119.6\); 3 vs 4: \(625.4-707.0=-81.6\).
All exceed 29.92, so all three comparisons are significant: every power setting differs from the control.
Dunnett's critical difference

Etch data, treatment 4 is the control: \(a-1=3\), \(f=16\), \(d_{0.05}(3,16)=2.59\):

\[D=d_{0.05}(3,16)\sqrt{\frac{2MS_E}{n}}=2.59\sqrt{\frac{2(333.70)}{5}}\] \[=2.59\times11.553=29.92\]

Compare with the observed differences from the control:

\[|551.2-707.0|=155.8\gt29.92\] \[|587.4-707.0|=119.6\gt29.92\] \[|625.4-707.0|=81.6\gt29.92\]

All three are significant. Note \(29.92\) is larger than the LSD \(24.49\): the extra width pays for making three comparisons at once.

Why not Fisher's LSD here?

We could use LSD to compare treatments 1, 2, 3 with the control. The important difference is that Dunnett accounts for making three comparisons at once, while LSD does not generally control the family-wise error rate.

For a right-tailed test the sign is "reject if the difference is larger than the critical value"; for a left-tailed test it is "reject if smaller than minus the critical value".
