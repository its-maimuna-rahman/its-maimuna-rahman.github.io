The Chi-Squared Distribution: Derivations from First Principles

Every property of \(\chi^2_k\) is derived here step by step: the density, the raw moments, the moment generating function, the cumulant generating function, the characteristic function, the mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis and mode. Nothing is quoted without proof, apart from three standard analytic facts listed in Section 1. Each quantity is obtained by at least two independent routes, so every result is cross-checked.

1 · Tools used throughout

1.1 The gamma function

For \(s\gt 0\) define

\[ \Gamma(s)=\int_0^\infty t^{\,s-1}e^{-t}\,dt . \]
Recurrence. Integrate by parts with \(u=t^{s}\) and \(dv=e^{-t}dt\): \[ \Gamma(s+1)=\Big[-t^{s}e^{-t}\Big]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty t^{\,s-1}e^{-t}\,dt=0+s\,\Gamma(s). \] The boundary term vanishes at both ends for \(s\gt 0\). Hence \(\Gamma(s+1)=s\,\Gamma(s)\), and since \(\Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}dt=1\), we get \(\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!\) for positive integers \(n\).
Value at one half. Substitute \(t=u^2\), \(dt=2u\,du\): \[ \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac12\right)=\int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}e^{-t}\,dt=\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{u}\,e^{-u^2}\,2u\,du=2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}du=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2}du=\sqrt{\pi}. \] The last step is the Gaussian integral.

1.2 Two integrals

Scaled gamma integral. For \(c\gt 0\) and \(s\gt 0\), substitute \(u=cx\): \[ \int_0^\infty x^{\,s-1}e^{-cx}\,dx=\frac{1}{c^{\,s}}\int_0^\infty u^{\,s-1}e^{-u}\,du=\frac{\Gamma(s)}{c^{\,s}}. \tag{G} \]
Beta integral (standard result). For \(p,q\gt 0\), \[ B(p,q)=\int_0^1 u^{\,p-1}(1-u)^{\,q-1}\,du=\frac{\Gamma(p)\,\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}. \tag{B} \]
Gaussian integral with a scale. For \(a\gt 0\), \[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-az^2/2}\,dz=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{a}}. \tag{N} \] This follows from the substitution \(z=y/\sqrt a\) and \(\int e^{-y^2/2}dy=\sqrt{2\pi}\).

1.3 Generating functions

For a random variable \(X\) define

\[ M_X(t)=E\!\left[e^{tX}\right],\qquad K_X(t)=\ln M_X(t),\qquad \varphi_X(t)=E\!\left[e^{itX}\right], \]

the moment generating function (MGF), the cumulant generating function (CGF) and the characteristic function (CF). When \(M_X\) exists in a neighbourhood of 0, expanding \(e^{tX}=\sum_n t^nX^n/n!\) and taking expectations term by term gives

\[ M_X(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu'_n\,\frac{t^n}{n!}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu'_n=E[X^n]=M_X^{(n)}(0). \]

In the same way \(\kappa_n=K_X^{(n)}(0)\) defines the \(n\)-th cumulant, and \(\mu'_n=i^{-n}\varphi_X^{(n)}(0)\). The characteristic function exists for every distribution. The uniqueness theorem says that two random variables with the same MGF on an open interval around 0, or with the same CF, have the same distribution.

Plan Section 2 derives the density from the definition \(\chi^2_k=Z_1^2+\cdots+Z_k^2\). Section 3 computes all raw moments directly from the density. Sections 4 to 6 compute the MGF, CGF and CF and recover the moments a second way. Sections 7 to 10 assemble mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis and mode.

2 · Deriving the density

Let \(Z_1,\dots,Z_k\) be independent \(N(0,1)\) variables and put \(V=Z_1^2+\cdots+Z_k^2\). We derive the density of \(V\) in two stages: first \(k=1\), then the sum.

2.1 One degree of freedom

The standard normal density is \(\phi(z)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}\) with distribution function \(\Phi\). For \(x\gt 0\), the event \(Z^2\le x\) is the event \(-\sqrt x\le Z\le\sqrt x\), so

\[ F(x)=P(Z^2\le x)=\Phi(\sqrt x)-\Phi(-\sqrt x)=2\,\Phi(\sqrt x)-1, \]

where \(\Phi(-a)=1-\Phi(a)\) was used by symmetry. Differentiate with the chain rule, using \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt x=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\):

\[ f_1(x)=F'(x)=2\,\phi(\sqrt x)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\;x^{-1/2}e^{-x/2},\qquad x\gt 0. \]

Compare this with the proposed general form at \(k=1\). Since \(2^{1/2}\,\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\pi}=\sqrt{2\pi}\),

\[ \frac{x^{1/2-1}e^{-x/2}}{2^{1/2}\,\Gamma(1/2)}=\frac{x^{-1/2}e^{-x/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}, \]

which is exactly \(f_1\). So the claimed formula holds for \(k=1\).

2.2 Adding independent chi-squared variables

Let \(U\) and \(W\) be independent with densities \(f_a\) and \(f_b\) of the proposed form, with \(a\) and \(b\) degrees of freedom. The density of the sum is the convolution

\[ f_{U+W}(x)=\int_0^x f_a(y)\,f_b(x-y)\,dy . \]
Step 1: substitute the densities. \[ f_{U+W}(x)=\frac{1}{2^{(a+b)/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac a2\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac b2\right)}\int_0^x y^{\frac a2-1}(x-y)^{\frac b2-1}\,e^{-y/2}\,e^{-(x-y)/2}\,dy . \] The two exponentials combine into \(e^{-x/2}\), which does not depend on \(y\) and leaves the integral.
Step 2: rescale the integration variable. Put \(y=xu\) with \(0\le u\le 1\) and \(dy=x\,du\). Then \(y^{a/2-1}=x^{a/2-1}u^{a/2-1}\) and \((x-y)^{b/2-1}=x^{b/2-1}(1-u)^{b/2-1}\). Collecting the powers of \(x\), including the factor \(x\) from \(dy\): \[ x^{\frac a2-1}\cdot x^{\frac b2-1}\cdot x=x^{\frac{a+b}{2}-1}. \] So \[ f_{U+W}(x)=\frac{x^{\frac{a+b}{2}-1}e^{-x/2}}{2^{(a+b)/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac a2\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac b2\right)}\int_0^1 u^{\frac a2-1}(1-u)^{\frac b2-1}\,du . \]
Step 3: recognise the Beta integral. By (B) the remaining integral equals \(B\!\left(\frac a2,\frac b2\right)=\dfrac{\Gamma(a/2)\,\Gamma(b/2)}{\Gamma((a+b)/2)}\). The gamma functions \(\Gamma(a/2)\Gamma(b/2)\) cancel: \[ f_{U+W}(x)=\frac{x^{\frac{a+b}{2}-1}e^{-x/2}}{2^{(a+b)/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)} . \]

This is the proposed form with \(a+b\) degrees of freedom. Now induct: \(f_1\) is correct, and adding one more independent \(Z_{k}^2\) (with \(b=1\)) to a sum of \(k-1\) squares (with \(a=k-1\)) gives the form with \(k\) degrees of freedom. Therefore

\[ f_k(x)=\frac{x^{\,k/2-1}\,e^{-x/2}}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)},\qquad x\gt 0,\qquad V\sim\chi^2_k . \] In particular \(\chi^2_a+\chi^2_b\sim\chi^2_{a+b}\) for independent summands.

2.3 Check that the density integrates to one

Apply (G) with \(s=k/2\) and \(c=1/2\):

\[ \int_0^\infty x^{\frac k2-1}e^{-x/2}\,dx=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{(1/2)^{k/2}}=2^{k/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac k2\right). \tag{1} \]

Dividing by the constant \(2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)\) gives total probability 1. Identity (1) is the engine behind every moment calculation that follows. It also shows that \(\chi^2_k\) is the gamma distribution with shape \(k/2\) and scale 2.

3 · Raw moments

For \(V\sim\chi^2_k\) and any real \(r\) with \(r\gt -k/2\), write out the expectation:

\[ E\!\left[V^{\,r}\right]=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty x^{\,r}\,x^{\frac k2-1}e^{-x/2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty x^{\,\left(\frac k2+r\right)-1}e^{-x/2}\,dx . \]

The integral is again of type (G), now with \(s=k/2+r\) and \(c=1/2\), so it equals \(2^{k/2+r}\,\Gamma(k/2+r)\). The condition \(r\gt -k/2\) is needed so that \(s\gt 0\). Hence

\[ E\!\left[V^{\,r}\right]=\frac{2^{k/2+r}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2+r\right)}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2\right)}=2^{\,r}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2+r\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2\right)} . \tag{2} \]

3.1 Positive integer moments as a product

For \(r=n\) a positive integer, apply the recurrence \(\Gamma(s+1)=s\,\Gamma(s)\) repeatedly, \(n\) times:

\[ \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac k2+n\right)=\left(\tfrac k2+n-1\right)\left(\tfrac k2+n-2\right)\cdots\left(\tfrac k2\right)\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac k2\right). \]

Substituting into (2) and distributing one factor 2 to each of the \(n\) brackets, since \(2\left(\frac k2+j\right)=k+2j\):

\[ \mu'_n=E[V^n]=2^{n}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac k2+j\right)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\left(k+2j\right)=k(k+2)(k+4)\cdots(k+2n-2). \tag{3} \]

3.2 The first four raw moments

\(n\)\(\mu'_n\) in product formExpanded
1\(k\)\(k\)
2\(k(k+2)\)\(k^2+2k\)
3\(k(k+2)(k+4)\)\(k^3+6k^2+8k\)
4\(k(k+2)(k+4)(k+6)\)\(k^4+12k^3+44k^2+48k\)

The expansions are obtained as follows. For \(n=3\), \((k+2)(k+4)=k^2+6k+8\), then multiply by \(k\). For \(n=4\), let \(u=k^2+6k\). Then \(k(k+6)=u\) and \((k+2)(k+4)=k^2+6k+8=u+8\), so the product is \(u(u+8)=u^2+8u\). Now \(u^2=k^4+12k^3+36k^2\) and \(8u=8k^2+48k\), and the sum is \(k^4+12k^3+44k^2+48k\).

3.3 A negative moment as a bonus

Formula (2) also covers \(r=-1\), valid when \(k/2-1\gt 0\), that is \(k\gt 2\). Using \(\Gamma(k/2)=\left(\frac k2-1\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2-1\right)\):

\[ E\!\left[\frac1V\right]=2^{-1}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2-1\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2\right)}=\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{\frac k2-1}=\frac{1}{k-2} . \]

This is the reason why \(1/V\) overshoots: \(E[1/V]=1/(k-2)\) is larger than \(1/E[V]=1/k\), as Jensen’s inequality predicts for the convex function \(1/x\). For \(k\le 2\) the expectation is infinite.

4 · The moment generating function

By definition, \(M(t)=E\!\left[e^{tV}\right]=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{tx}f_k(x)\,dx\).

4.1 Direct derivation from the density

Step 1: combine the exponentials. \[ M(t)=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty x^{\frac k2-1}\,e^{-x/2}\,e^{tx}\,dx=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty x^{\frac k2-1}\,e^{-\frac{(1-2t)}{2}\,x}\,dx . \]
Step 2: check convergence. The integral converges exactly when the coefficient of \(-x\) in the exponent is positive: \(c=\dfrac{1-2t}{2}\gt 0\), that is \(t\lt\tfrac12\).
Step 3: apply (G) with \(s=k/2\) and \(c=(1-2t)/2\): \[ \int_0^\infty x^{\frac k2-1}e^{-cx}dx=\frac{\Gamma(k/2)}{\left(\frac{1-2t}{2}\right)^{k/2}}=\frac{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)}{(1-2t)^{k/2}} . \]
Step 4: cancel the constants. \[ M(t)=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\cdot\frac{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)}{(1-2t)^{k/2}} . \]
\[ M_V(t)=(1-2t)^{-k/2},\qquad t\lt\tfrac12 . \]

4.2 Independent derivation through \(Z^2\)

For a single standard normal, apply (N) with \(a=1-2t\gt 0\):

\[ E\!\left[e^{tZ^2}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tz^2}e^{-z^2/2}\,dz=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(1-2t)z^2/2}\,dz=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{1-2t}}=(1-2t)^{-1/2}. \]

The MGF of a sum of independent variables is the product of the MGFs, so for \(V=\sum_{j=1}^{k}Z_j^2\):

\[ M_V(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-2t)^{-1/2}=(1-2t)^{-k/2}, \]

which agrees with 4.1. As a by-product, this MGF proves \(\chi^2_a+\chi^2_b\sim\chi^2_{a+b}\) in one line: \((1-2t)^{-a/2}(1-2t)^{-b/2}=(1-2t)^{-(a+b)/2}\), and the uniqueness theorem finishes the argument.

4.3 Moments from derivatives of the MGF

Differentiate \(M(t)=(1-2t)^{-k/2}\) repeatedly. The chain rule gives \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(1-2t)^{-p}=2p\,(1-2t)^{-p-1}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} M'(t)&=k\,(1-2t)^{-\frac k2-1},\\ M''(t)&=k\,(k+2)\,(1-2t)^{-\frac k2-2},\\ M'''(t)&=k(k+2)(k+4)\,(1-2t)^{-\frac k2-3}. \end{aligned} \]

At each step the new factor is \(2\left(\frac k2+j\right)=k+2j\). By induction

\[ M^{(n)}(t)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(k+2j)\;(1-2t)^{-\frac k2-n},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \mu'_n=M^{(n)}(0)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(k+2j). \]
Cross-check This is identical to (3), obtained earlier by integrating the density directly. Two independent routes agree.

5 · The cumulant generating function

Take the logarithm of the MGF on the interval \(t\lt\tfrac12\):

\[ K(t)=\ln M(t)=-\frac k2\,\ln(1-2t) . \]

5.1 Derivatives of \(K\)

\[ K'(t)=-\frac k2\cdot\frac{-2}{1-2t}=\frac{k}{1-2t}. \]

Each further derivative of \((1-2t)^{-r}\) produces the factor \(2r\) and raises the power by one, so

\[ K''(t)=\frac{2k}{(1-2t)^2},\qquad K'''(t)=\frac{8k}{(1-2t)^3},\qquad K^{(4)}(t)=\frac{48k}{(1-2t)^4}. \]

The numerators follow the pattern \(k,\ 2k,\ 2k\cdot 4,\ 8k\cdot 6\), where the multipliers \(2\cdot1,\ 2\cdot2,\ 2\cdot3\) are the factors \(2r\) for \(r=1,2,3\). In general, by induction,

\[ K^{(r)}(t)=k\,2^{\,r-1}(r-1)!\,(1-2t)^{-r}. \]

The step from \(r\) to \(r+1\) multiplies by \(2r\): \(k\,2^{r-1}(r-1)!\cdot 2r=k\,2^{r}\,r!\), as required.

5.2 Cumulants

\[ \kappa_r=K^{(r)}(0)=k\,2^{\,r-1}\,(r-1)!\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \kappa_1=k,\quad \kappa_2=2k,\quad \kappa_3=8k,\quad \kappa_4=48k . \]

5.3 Cross-check by a power series

For \(|u|\lt 1\), \(-\ln(1-u)=\sum_{r\ge1}u^r/r\). Put \(u=2t\), valid for \(|t|\lt\tfrac12\):

\[ K(t)=\frac k2\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2t)^r}{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{k\,2^{\,r-1}}{r}\,t^r=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\Big[k\,2^{\,r-1}(r-1)!\Big]\frac{t^r}{r!} . \]

The last step multiplies and divides by \((r-1)!\), using \(r!=r\,(r-1)!\). The coefficient of \(t^r/r!\) is \(\kappa_r\), and it matches the derivative route.

5.4 From cumulants to central moments

Let \(\mu=E[V]=\kappa_1\). Centring subtracts \(\mu\) and removes only the first cumulant, so \(V-\mu\) has \(\kappa_1=0\) and the same \(\kappa_r\) for \(r\ge2\). Its MGF is \(\exp\!\left(\kappa_2\frac{t^2}{2}+\kappa_3\frac{t^3}{6}+\kappa_4\frac{t^4}{24}+\cdots\right)\). Expand \(e^y=1+y+\frac{y^2}{2}+\cdots\) with \(y\) equal to that sum and keep terms up to \(t^4\). The \(y^2/2\) term contributes \(\frac12\left(\frac{\kappa_2t^2}{2}\right)^2=\frac{\kappa_2^2}{8}t^4\), and higher powers of \(y\) start at \(t^6\):

\[ M_{V-\mu}(t)=1+\kappa_2\frac{t^2}{2}+\kappa_3\frac{t^3}{6}+\left(\frac{\kappa_4}{24}+\frac{\kappa_2^2}{8}\right)t^4+\cdots . \]

Match with \(M_{V-\mu}(t)=\sum_n\mu_n t^n/n!\), where \(\mu_n=E[(V-\mu)^n]\) are the central moments. The coefficient of \(t^4\) is \(\mu_4/24\), so multiplying by 24:

\[ \mu_2=\kappa_2,\qquad \mu_3=\kappa_3,\qquad \mu_4=\kappa_4+3\kappa_2^2 . \tag{4} \]

6 · The characteristic function

The CF is \(\varphi(t)=E\!\left[e^{itV}\right]\) for real \(t\). It always exists, and for \(\chi^2_k\) it requires no restriction on \(t\).

6.1 Derivation from the density

Step 1: combine the exponentials. \[ \varphi(t)=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty x^{\frac k2-1}\,e^{-x/2+itx}\,dx=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty x^{\frac k2-1}e^{-c\,x}\,dx,\qquad c=\frac{1-2it}{2}. \] The constant \(c\) is complex with real part \(\tfrac12\gt 0\), so the integrand still decays like \(e^{-x/2}\) and the integral converges absolutely.
Step 2: extend (G) to complex \(c\). Identity (G) holds for real \(c\gt 0\). Both sides, \(\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-cx}dx\) and \(\Gamma(s)\,c^{-s}\), are analytic functions of \(c\) on the half-plane \(\operatorname{Re}c\gt 0\), where \(c^{-s}\) uses the principal branch. Two analytic functions that agree on the positive real axis agree on the whole half-plane. Hence (G) remains valid for our complex \(c\): \[ \int_0^\infty x^{\frac k2-1}e^{-cx}dx=\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac k2\right)c^{-k/2}=\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac k2\right)\left(\frac{1-2it}{2}\right)^{-k/2}=2^{k/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac k2\right)(1-2it)^{-k/2}. \]
Step 3: cancel the constants.
\[ \varphi_V(t)=(1-2it)^{-k/2},\qquad t\in\mathbb R . \]

This is the MGF with \(t\) replaced by \(it\). The substitution is legitimate here because the MGF exists on a neighbourhood of 0.

6.2 Independent derivation through \(Z^2\)

For \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), \(E[e^{itZ^2}]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-(1-2it)z^2/2}dz\). The complex coefficient \(a=1-2it\) has positive real part, so (N) extends in the same way by analytic continuation and gives \(\sqrt{2\pi/a}\), that is \(\varphi_{Z^2}(t)=(1-2it)^{-1/2}\). Independence turns the CF of a sum into a product, and \(\varphi_V(t)=(1-2it)^{-k/2}\) follows.

6.3 Modulus and phase

Write \(1-2it=r\,e^{-i\theta}\) with \(r=\sqrt{1+4t^2}\) and \(\theta=\arctan(2t)\). Raising to the power \(-k/2\):

\[ \varphi_V(t)=(1+4t^2)^{-k/4}\,e^{\,i\,\frac k2\arctan(2t)},\qquad |\varphi_V(t)|=(1+4t^2)^{-k/4}\le 1 . \]

The modulus decays to 0 as \(|t|\to\infty\), as it must for a distribution with a density (Riemann–Lebesgue lemma), and equals 1 at \(t=0\).

6.4 Moments from the CF

Differentiate \(\varphi(t)=(1-2it)^{-k/2}\). Now \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(1-2it)^{-p}=2ip\,(1-2it)^{-p-1}\), so each derivative brings the factor \(i\,(k+2j)\):

\[ \varphi^{(n)}(t)=i^{\,n}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(k+2j)\;(1-2it)^{-\frac k2-n}. \]

At \(t=0\) the last factor is 1, and \(\mu'_n=i^{-n}\varphi^{(n)}(0)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(k+2j)\), once more equal to (3). For example \(\varphi'(0)=ik\), so \(E[V]=i^{-1}\cdot ik=k\).

6.5 Cumulants from the CF

The log of the CF is \(\ln\varphi(t)=K(it)=-\frac k2\ln(1-2it)\). Expanding with \(-\ln(1-u)=\sum u^r/r\) and \(u=2it\):

\[ \ln\varphi(t)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\kappa_r\frac{(it)^r}{r!},\qquad \kappa_r=k\,2^{\,r-1}(r-1)!, \]

the same cumulants as in Section 5.

7 · Mean and variance

Route A: from the raw moments

From (3), \(E[V]=\mu'_1=k\) and \(E[V^2]=\mu'_2=k(k+2)\). The variance is the second raw moment minus the squared mean:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(V)=E[V^2]-\big(E[V]\big)^2=k(k+2)-k^2=k^2+2k-k^2=2k . \]

Route B: from the MGF

\(M'(0)=k\) and \(M''(0)=k(k+2)\), and the same subtraction gives \(2k\).

Route C: from the CGF

\(\kappa_1=K'(0)=k\) and \(\kappa_2=K''(0)=2k\). The variance is the second cumulant directly.

Route D: from the definition as a sum of squares

For \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), \(E[Z^2]=1\) and \(E[Z^4]=3\), the fourth moment being obtained by one integration by parts: \(\int z^3\cdot z\,\phi(z)\,dz=3\int z^2\phi(z)\,dz=3\). Hence \(\operatorname{Var}(Z^2)=3-1=2\). The \(k\) squares are independent, so means and variances add:

\[ E[V]=k\cdot1=k,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(V)=k\cdot2=2k . \]
\[ E[V]=k,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(V)=2k,\qquad \text{sd}(V)=\sqrt{2k}. \] Four routes, one answer.

8 · Skewness

Skewness is the standardised third central moment, \(\gamma_1=\mu_3\big/\mu_2^{3/2}\).

8.1 From the raw moments

Expand \(E[(V-\mu)^3]\) with \(\mu=k\), using \(\mu'_1=k\):

\[ \mu_3=\mu'_3-3\,\mu'_2\,\mu+3\,\mu'_1\,\mu^2-\mu^3=\mu'_3-3\,\mu'_2\,k+2k^3 , \]

because \(3\mu'_1\mu^2-\mu^3=3k^3-k^3=2k^3\). Insert \(\mu'_3=k^3+6k^2+8k\) and \(\mu'_2=k^2+2k\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu_3&=\left(k^3+6k^2+8k\right)-3k\left(k^2+2k\right)+2k^3\\ &=k^3+6k^2+8k-3k^3-6k^2+2k^3\\ &=\left(1-3+2\right)k^3+\left(6-6\right)k^2+8k=8k . \end{aligned} \]

8.2 From the cumulants

By (4), \(\mu_3=\kappa_3=8k\), the same value obtained with much less algebra. This is the practical advantage of cumulants.

8.3 Standardise

\[ \gamma_1=\frac{8k}{(2k)^{3/2}}=\frac{8k}{2k\sqrt{2k}}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{2k}}=\sqrt{\frac{16}{2k}}=\sqrt{\frac8k}. \]
\[ \gamma_1=\sqrt{\dfrac{8}{k}}\gt 0 . \] The distribution is right-skewed for every \(k\), and skewness vanishes like \(k^{-1/2}\).

Check: \(k=1\) gives \(\sqrt8\approx 2.83\), a very heavy right tail, and \(k=2\) gives \(2\), which is the skewness of the exponential distribution, as it should be since \(\chi^2_2\) is exponential with mean 2.

9 · Kurtosis

Kurtosis is the standardised fourth central moment, \(\beta_2=\mu_4/\mu_2^2\), and excess kurtosis is \(\gamma_2=\beta_2-3\), which equals 0 for a normal distribution.

9.1 From the raw moments

Expand \(E[(V-\mu)^4]\) with \(\mu=k\):

\[ \mu_4=\mu'_4-4\,\mu'_3\,\mu+6\,\mu'_2\,\mu^2-4\,\mu'_1\,\mu^3+\mu^4=\mu'_4-4k\,\mu'_3+6k^2\,\mu'_2-3k^4 , \]

since \(-4\mu'_1\mu^3+\mu^4=-4k^4+k^4=-3k^4\). Now evaluate each of the four pieces:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu'_4&=k^4+12k^3+44k^2+48k,\\ -4k\,\mu'_3&=-4k^4-24k^3-32k^2,\\ 6k^2\,\mu'_2&=6k^4+12k^3,\\ -3k^4&=-3k^4 . \end{aligned} \]

Add the columns by powers of \(k\):

\[ \begin{aligned} k^4:&\quad 1-4+6-3=0,\\ k^3:&\quad 12-24+12=0,\\ k^2:&\quad 44-32=12,\\ k^1:&\quad 48 . \end{aligned} \] \[ \mu_4=12k^2+48k . \]

9.2 From the cumulants

By (4), \(\mu_4=\kappa_4+3\kappa_2^2=48k+3(2k)^2=48k+12k^2\), in agreement.

9.3 Standardise

\[ \beta_2=\frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^2}=\frac{12k^2+48k}{(2k)^2}=\frac{12k^2+48k}{4k^2}=3+\frac{12}{k},\qquad \gamma_2=\beta_2-3 . \]
\[ \beta_2=3+\frac{12}{k},\qquad \gamma_2=\frac{12}{k}\gt 0 . \] The tails are heavier than normal for every \(k\), and the excess shrinks like \(1/k\).

Note that excess kurtosis equals \(\kappa_4/\kappa_2^2=48k/4k^2=12/k\), a general fact: excess kurtosis is the standardised fourth cumulant, and skewness is the standardised third cumulant, \(\kappa_3/\kappa_2^{3/2}=8k/(2k)^{3/2}\).

10 · The mode

The mode is the point where the density is largest. Since \(\ln\) is increasing, we may maximise the log-density, which is easier to differentiate. For \(x\gt0\),

\[ \ln f_k(x)=\left(\frac k2-1\right)\ln x-\frac x2-\ln\!\left[2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)\right], \]

where the last term is a constant.

First-order condition. \[ \frac{d}{dx}\ln f_k(x)=\frac{k/2-1}{x}-\frac12=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=k-2 . \] This is a positive solution only when \(k\gt2\).
Second-order condition. \[ \frac{d^2}{dx^2}\ln f_k(x)=-\frac{k/2-1}{x^2}\lt0\quad\text{for }k\gt2 , \] so \(x=k-2\) is a maximum. It is also the only stationary point, hence the global maximum.
Case \(k=2\). The density is \(f_2(x)=\tfrac12e^{-x/2}\), which is strictly decreasing, so the supremum is at the boundary \(x=0\) with value \(1/2\). The mode is 0.
Case \(k=1\). The density \(f_1(x)=x^{-1/2}e^{-x/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) is strictly decreasing and tends to \(+\infty\) as \(x\to0^+\). It has no finite maximum, and the density is unbounded at 0. By convention the mode is taken as 0.
\[ \text{mode}=\max\left(k-2,\;0\right). \]

The ordering of the three centre measures shows the right skew: mode \(=k-2\lt\) mean \(=k\) for \(k\gt2\). A useful approximate median is \(k\left(1-\frac{2}{9k}\right)^3\), which sits between the two (stated without proof, from the Wilson–Hilferty approximation).

11 · Large \(k\): the normal limit from the MGF

The skewness \(\sqrt{8/k}\) and excess kurtosis \(12/k\) both tend to zero, which suggests a normal limit. We prove it. Standardise \(V\) to \(Y=(V-k)/\sqrt{2k}\) and compute its MGF:

\[ M_Y(t)=e^{-t\sqrt{k/2}}\;M_V\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2k}}\right)=e^{-t\sqrt{k/2}}\left(1-t\sqrt{\frac2k}\right)^{-k/2}, \]

because \(2\cdot\dfrac{t}{\sqrt{2k}}=t\sqrt{2/k}\), and \(\dfrac{k}{\sqrt{2k}}=\sqrt{k/2}\). Take logarithms and put \(u=t\sqrt{2/k}\), which tends to 0 as \(k\to\infty\):

\[ \ln M_Y(t)=-t\sqrt{\frac k2}-\frac k2\ln(1-u)=-t\sqrt{\frac k2}+\frac k2\left(u+\frac{u^2}{2}+\frac{u^3}{3}+\cdots\right). \]

Evaluate the pieces: \(\frac k2u=\frac k2\,t\sqrt{\frac2k}=t\sqrt{\frac k2}\), which cancels the first term exactly. Next \(\frac k2\cdot\frac{u^2}{2}=\frac k4\cdot\frac{2t^2}{k}=\frac{t^2}{2}\). The remaining terms are of size \(k\,u^3\propto k^{-1/2}\) and vanish. Therefore

\[ \ln M_Y(t)\longrightarrow\frac{t^2}{2},\qquad M_Y(t)\longrightarrow e^{t^2/2},\qquad k\to\infty , \]

the MGF of \(N(0,1)\). By the continuity theorem for MGFs, \((\chi^2_k-k)/\sqrt{2k}\Rightarrow N(0,1)\). The first neglected term, \(\frac k2\cdot\frac{u^3}{3}=\frac{t^3}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2}{k}}\), is \(\frac{\gamma_1}{6}t^3\) with \(\gamma_1=\sqrt{8/k}\), so the leading correction to normality is exactly the skewness.
