The Ratio Estimator

Using a known auxiliary variable to estimate a mean

The sample mean ignores everything we already know about the population. This page shows how one known number, the population mean of a helper variable, can fix an unlucky sample and shrink the error. Each idea has a worked number and a widget.

See it first

Suppose we want the mean yield per plot. We know every plot's area, so we know the true mean area \(\Xb=3.2\) ha. Our sample gave \(\yb=9.37\) t. Drag the sample's mean area \(\xb\) and watch the corrected estimate move.

ū sample mean

Large \(\xb\) means the sample grabbed big plots, so \(\yb\) is probably too high and gets scaled down. Small \(\xb\) scales it up.

Why bother? The motivation

The sample mean \(\yb\) is unbiased, but a random sample can still be lopsided. If a cheap variable \(X\) moves with \(Y\) and we know its population mean, we can measure how lopsided the sample is and correct for it.

Fruit per plot

\(Y\) = fruit on a plot, \(X\) = plot area.

Firm sales

\(Y\) = sales now, \(X\) = sales in the last census.

App sessions

\(Y\) = sessions this month, \(X\) = sessions last month from logs.

Requirement. \(\Xb\) (or the total \(X_{tot}\)) must be known for the whole population. Without it there is nothing to correct against.

Setup and the estimator

Population of \(N\) units with pairs \((X_i,Y_i)\). We draw an SRSWOR of \(n\) pairs \((x_i,y_i)\). Write \(f=n/N\), so the finite population correction is \(1-f\).

SymbolMeaning
\(S_X^2,\ S_Y^2,\ S_{XY}\)population variances and covariance (divisor \(N-1\))
\(\rho=S_{XY}/(S_XS_Y)\)population correlation
\(C_X=S_X/\Xb,\ C_Y=S_Y/\Yb\)coefficients of variation
\(R=\Yb/\Xb\)population ratio

Three estimators

Estimate the ratio from the sample, then multiply by the known \(\Xb\):

\[\hat R=\frac{\yb}{\xb},\qquad \YR=\hat R\,\Xb=\yb\cdot\frac{\Xb}{\xb},\qquad \hat Y_{R(tot)}=N\,\YR\]

So \(\YR\) is just \(\yb\) times a correction factor \(\Xb/\xb\).

Question. \(N=80\) plots, \(\Xb=3.2\) ha. A sample of \(n=10\) plots has areas 2.1, 3.4, 1.8, 4.2, 2.9, 3.8, 2.5, 5.1, 3.0, 4.0 and yields 6.0, 9.5, 5.2, 12.1, 7.8, 11.0, 7.1, 14.6, 8.9, 11.5. Estimate the mean and total yield.
Solution.
\[\sum x=32.8,\quad \sum y=93.7,\quad \xb=3.28,\quad \yb=9.37\]
\[\hat R=\frac{93.7}{32.8}=2.857\ \text{t/ha}\]
\[\YR=2.857\times3.2=9.141,\qquad \hat Y_{R(tot)}=80\times9.141=731.3\ \text{t}\]

\(\xb=3.28>3.2\), so the sample leaned toward large plots and the estimate (9.141) is below \(\yb=9.37\).

When is it appropriate?

  • \(\Xb\) or \(X_{tot}\) is known for the same population.
  • \(X\) and \(Y\) are positively related and the relation is roughly a line through the origin, \(Y\approx RX\).
  • All \(x_i>0\), so the ratio exists.
  • \(n\) is not tiny, because the results are large-sample approximations.
  • The sample is SRSWOR.
Watch out. If the fitted line has a big intercept, a regression estimator does better. Always draw the scatterplot first.

Bias of the ratio estimator

\(\YR=\Xb\,\yb/\xb\) is a ratio of random variables, and in general \(\E(\yb/\xb)\ne\E(\yb)/\E(\xb)\). So the estimator is biased, and the exact bias is not tractable. The fix: write each error as a small relative deviation, expand in a series, and keep the first and second order terms (D1 to D3).

Result

To first order the bias is 0. To second order:

\[\text{Bias}(\YR)\approx\frac{1-f}{n}\,\Yb\big(C_X^2-\rho C_XC_Y\big)=\frac{1-f}{n}\cdot\frac{R\,S_X^2-S_{XY}}{\Xb}\]

It shrinks like \(1/n\). It is zero when \(C_X=\rho C_Y\), equivalently \(R=S_{XY}/S_X^2\): the regression line of \(Y\) on \(X\) passes through the origin (D4).

Question. \(N=100,\ n=25,\ \Yb=20,\ C_X=0.4,\ C_Y=0.5,\ \rho=0.6\). Find the bias.
Solution.
\[\frac{1-f}{n}=\frac{0.75}{25}=0.03\]
\[C_X^2-\rho C_XC_Y=0.16-0.6(0.4)(0.5)=0.16-0.12=0.04\]
\[\text{Bias}\approx0.03\times20\times0.04=0.024\]

Is the bias a problem?

Compare it with the standard error. With \(\hat R\) and Cauchy–Schwarz (D7):

\[\frac{|\text{Bias}(\hat R)|}{\sigma_{\hat R}}\le C_{\xb}=\sqrt{\tfrac{1-f}{n}}\,C_X\]

If \(C_{\xb}<0.1\) the bias is under about 10% of the standard error and can be ignored. Here \(C_{\xb}=\sqrt{0.03}(0.4)=0.069\), so it is safe to ignore.

Mean squared error

Because the bias is tiny, precision is judged by the MSE. Squaring the first-order error gives (D5):

\[\text{MSE}(\YR)\approx\frac{1-f}{n}\,\Yb^2\big(C_Y^2+C_X^2-2\rho C_XC_Y\big)=\frac{1-f}{n}\,S_U^2,\quad S_U^2=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum(Y_i-RX_i)^2\]

So the MSE is small when points hug the line \(Y=RX\). The residuals from that line decide precision.

Numbers (same data as the bias example).
\[\text{MSE}\approx0.03\times400\times(0.25+0.16-2(0.6)(0.2))=12\times0.17=2.04\]
\[\text{Var}(\yb)=0.03\times400\times0.25=3.00\]

The ratio estimator has about 32% less MSE. Also \(|\text{Bias}|/\sqrt{\text{MSE}}=0.024/1.428=0.017\).

Estimating it from the sample

Replace \(R\) by \(\hat R\) and population sums by sample sums. For a total multiply by \(N^2\).

\[\widehat{\text{MSE}}(\YR)=\frac{1-f}{n}s_u^2,\quad s_u^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(y_i-\hat Rx_i)^2,\qquad \YR\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{\text{MSE}}}\]

When does it beat the sample mean?

Subtract \(\text{Var}(\yb)=\frac{1-f}{n}\Yb^2C_Y^2\) from the MSE (D6). The difference is \(\frac{1-f}{n}\Yb^2(C_X^2-2\rho C_XC_Y)\), negative exactly when

\[\rho>\frac12\,\frac{C_X}{C_Y}\]
Example. \(C_X=0.4,\ C_Y=0.5\): the ratio estimator wins when \(\rho>\tfrac12(0.8)=0.4\). If \(C_X\approx C_Y\), the rule is \(\rho>0.5\).

Try it. The sliders show each estimator's MSE as a multiple of \(\text{Var}(\yb)\). Below 1 means better than the sample mean.

Negative correlation: the product estimator

If \(\rho<0\) the ratio estimator can be worse than \(\yb\). Use the product estimator \(\hat{\bar Y}_P=\yb\,\xb/\Xb\). There is no division by \(\xb\), so no series is needed (D8):

\[\text{Bias}=\frac{1-f}{n}\frac{S_{XY}}{\Xb},\qquad \text{MSE}\approx\frac{1-f}{n}\Yb^2\big(C_Y^2+C_X^2+2\rho C_XC_Y\big)\]

It beats \(\yb\) exactly when \(\rho<-\tfrac12 C_X/C_Y\).

Example. \(C_X=C_Y,\ \rho=-0.9\). MSE as a multiple of \(\text{Var}(\yb)\): ratio \(2-2\rho=3.8\); product \(2+2\rho=0.2\); regression \(1-\rho^2=0.19\). Rule of thumb: \(\rho\) clearly positive, ratio; clearly negative, product; unsure or near 0, regression.

Worked example: precision

Back to the plot data. Residuals \(e_i=y_i-\hat Rx_i\) with \(\hat R=2.8567\):

\(i\)12345678910
\(e_i\)0.001-0.2130.0580.102-0.4840.145-0.0420.0310.3300.073
\[s_u^2=\frac{\sum e_i^2}{9}=0.0479,\qquad f=\tfrac{10}{80}=0.125\]
\[\widehat{\text{MSE}}=\frac{0.875}{10}(0.0479)=0.00419,\quad \text{se}=0.0648\]
\[\text{95\% CI}=9.141\pm1.96(0.0648)=(9.01,\ 9.27)\]

The sample mean has \(s_y^2=8.733\), so \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(\yb)=\frac{0.875}{10}(8.733)=0.764\) and se \(=0.874\). The ratio standard error is about 13 times smaller because \(y\approx2.86x\) almost exactly (\(r=0.997\)). Real data are rarely this clean.

Check by simulation (from the lecture). \(N=500,\ n=25\), 20 000 samples: simulated bias \(-0.0017\) vs theory \(-0.0012\); simulated MSE 0.2336 vs theory 0.2371; \(\text{Var}(\yb)=1.611\). The approximations hold, and the ratio estimator cuts MSE about 7 times.

Practice

Exercise 1

\(C_X=0.4,\ C_Y=0.5\). For which \(\rho\) is the ratio estimator better than \(\yb\)?

Show answer
\[\rho>\frac12\cdot\frac{0.4}{0.5}=0.4\]

Exercise 2

\(N=200,\ n=20,\ \Xb=15,\ \sum x=280,\ \sum y=420\). Find \(\hat R\), \(\YR\), \(\hat Y_{R(tot)}\).

Show answer
\[\hat R=\frac{420}{280}=1.5,\quad \YR=1.5(15)=22.5,\quad \hat Y_{R(tot)}=200(22.5)=4500\]

Exercise 3

Show the bias is about 0 when the regression of \(Y\) on \(X\) passes through the origin.

Show answer

Set the bias to zero: \(R S_X^2=S_{XY}\), so \(R=S_{XY}/S_X^2\), the regression slope. A line with slope \(R\) and \(\Yb=R\Xb\) has intercept \(\Yb-R\Xb=0\). Full steps in D4.

Exercise 4 (R)

Simulate a population, then compare \(\text{MSE}(\YR)\) with \(\text{Var}(\yb)\) for \(n=10,25,50\). Expect the bias to fade as \(n\) grows.
