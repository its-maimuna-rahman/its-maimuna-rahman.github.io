Sinking Funds

Repaying a loan with one lump sum at the end, while saving up for it along the way. One page: a real case, the definition, the intuition, solved problems, derivations and a live calculator.

1 · A real-life case, solved

Harbor Café Group needs $100,000 to open a second branch. The lender agrees to a 5-year loan on these terms:

  • Interest of 9% per year is paid on the full loan at the end of every year.
  • The principal $100,000 is repaid in one lump sum at the end of year 5.

The café must not be caught short in year 5, so it makes 5 equal year-end deposits into a savings fund that pays 6% per year. The fund must hold exactly $100,000 right after the 5th deposit.

Question: how large is each deposit, what does the café pay each year in total, and how does this compare with an ordinary instalment loan at 9%?

Step 1 · Yearly interest to the lender. The loan balance never falls (no principal is repaid yet), so \[I = 100{,}000 \times 0.09 = \$9{,}000 \text{ every year.}\]
Step 2 · Deposit size. The 5 deposits \(D\) form an annuity-immediate at 6% that must accumulate to \$100,000: \[D\, s_{\overline{5}|0.06}=100{,}000,\qquad s_{\overline{5}|0.06}=\frac{1.06^5-1}{0.06}=\frac{0.3382256}{0.06}=5.637093\] \[D=\frac{100{,}000}{5.637093}=\$17{,}739.64\]
Step 3 · Total yearly outlay. \[\text{Payment}=I+D=9{,}000+17{,}739.64=\$26{,}739.64\]
Step 4 · Build the fund year by year. Interest earned in a year is 6% of the fund balance at the start of that year; the deposit is made at the end.

2 · Definition

The key phrases from the case were one lump sum, interest paid periodically, equal deposits, fund accumulating to the loan, two different rates and net amount of the loan. Put together:

Sinking fund method A way of repaying a loan in which the borrower (i) pays the lender interest on the full loan each period (called the service of the loan), so the loan amount stays constant, and (ii) makes periodic deposits into a separate fund that accumulates to exactly the loan amount at the end of the term, when it is used to repay the principal in one lump sum.
Loan interest rate \(i\)
Paid to the lender on the whole principal each period.
Fund rate \(j\)
Earned by the sinking fund. In practice \(j\le i\).
Net amount of loan
Original loan minus the accumulated value of the fund at that date.

3 · The idea in plain words

Think of it as renting the money and saving to give it back. Every period you do two separate things:

  1. Rent: pay the lender interest on the whole loan, like rent that never shrinks.
  2. Save: drop a fixed amount into a savings account, planned so that on the last day the account holds exactly the loan amount.

Because the savings earn interest, later years' deposits get a "free boost" from earlier deposits. That is why the deposit \(D\) is smaller than \(L/n\).

Reading the schedule

Five columns carry all the information: interest paid \(=iL\) (constant) · deposit \(D\) (constant) · interest earned \(=j\times\)(previous fund balance) · fund balance \(=\) previous \(+D+\) interest earned · net loan \(=L-\)fund balance. The first year's interest earned is always 0 because the fund starts empty.

Example 2 · Loan with a single time-point question

A \$20,000 loan is due in 6 years. Interest is 7%, the fund earns 7%. (a) Find the deposit. (b) After the 4th deposit, what is the fund balance and net loan?

(a) \(s_{\overline{6}|0.07}=\dfrac{1.07^6-1}{0.07}=\dfrac{0.500730}{0.07}=7.153291\) \[D=\frac{20{,}000}{7.153291}=\$2{,}795.92\]

Deposit \$2,795.92 · fund after 4 deposits \$12,413.71 · net loan \$7,586.29. The yearly interest to the lender stays \$1,400 throughout.

5 · Worked math B: two rates (\(i\neq j\))

SetupThe total payment splits in two: first pay interest at rate \(i\) on the loan; whatever is left goes into a fund growing at rate \(j\). Notation \(a_{\overline{n}|i\,\&\,j}\) is the present value of an annuity of 1 under this arrangement, so a loan of 1 needs a payment of \(1/a_{\overline{n}|i\&j}\) per period.

6 · Worked math C: the equivalent amortization rate

Example 4 · Which offer is cheaper? (the "indifference rate")

A wants \$1,000 for 4 years. Lender B: principal at the end, 10% effective interest on the loan paid yearly, A accumulates a fund earning 8%. Lender C: ordinary amortized loan over 4 years. What is the largest effective rate C may charge so that A is indifferent?

Idea. Both plans have 4 equal yearly payments, so A is indifferent when the payments are equal. B's payment is \$321.92 (Example 3). For C at rate \(i\): \[321.92\,a_{\overline{4}|i}=1000\ \Longrightarrow\ a_{\overline{4}|i}=\frac{1000}{321.92}=3.1064\] This cannot be solved by algebra, so we iterate (Newton–Raphson, derived in §8).
Starting value. With \(n=4,\ k=3.1064\): \[i_0=\frac{2(n-k)}{k(n+1)}=\frac{2(0.8936)}{3.1064\times5}=0.1151\]
Iteration 1. \((1.1151)^{-4}=0.64682\) and \((1.1151)^{-5}=0.58007\): \[i_1=i_0\left[1+\frac{1-(1+i_0)^{-n}-k\,i_0}{1-(1+i_0)^{-n-1}\{1+i_0(n+1)\}}\right]=0.1151\left[1+\frac{1-0.64682-0.35745}{1-0.58007(1.5754)}\right]=0.1151\left[1+\frac{-0.00427}{0.08617}\right]=0.10935\]

8 · Derivations

D1 · Deposit and fund balance

Deposits \(D\) at times \(1,\dots,n\) with fund rate \(j\). The deposit made at time \(k\) grows for \(n-k\) years: \[D\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1+j)^{n-k}=D\left[1+(1+j)+\dots+(1+j)^{n-1}\right]=D\,\frac{(1+j)^n-1}{j}=D\,s_{\overline{n}|j}.\] Setting this equal to the loan \(L\) gives \(\boxed{D=L/s_{\overline{n}|j}}\). The same argument stops at time \(t\): \(\ \text{Fund}_t=D\,s_{\overline{t}|j}\).

D2 · Net amount of loan and fund interest

The fund can be applied against the loan at any time, so \(\text{Net}_t=L-D\,s_{\overline{t}|j}\). Interest earned in year \(t\) is \(j\) times the opening balance: \[J_t=j\,D\,s_{\overline{t-1}|j},\qquad \text{Fund}_t=\text{Fund}_{t-1}+D+J_t.\] Check: \(D s_{\overline{t-1}|j}(1+j)+D=D\left[\frac{(1+j)^t-(1+j)}{j}+1\right]=D\,s_{\overline{t}|j}\).

D3 · Equivalence with amortization when \(i=j\)

Take a loan of \(a_{\overline{n}|i}\) with level installments of 1. Interest each period under the sinking fund method is \(i\,a_{\overline{n}|i}=1-v^n\). The rest of the payment goes into the fund: \[1-(1-v^n)=v^n.\] These deposits accumulate to \[v^n\,s_{\overline{n}|i}=v^n\,\frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i}=\frac{1-v^n}{i}=a_{\overline{n}|i},\] the original loan. So the sinking fund repays exactly the same loan with the same payment of 1: the two methods are equivalent.

Net loan equals amortization balance. After \(t\) deposits of \(v^n\): \[a_{\overline{n}|}-v^n s_{\overline{t}|}=\frac{1-v^n-v^n(1+i)^t+v^n}{i}=\frac{1-v^{n-t}}{i}=a_{\overline{n-t}|}.\] This is the amortization outstanding balance (prospective method).

D4 · The four relationships (\(i=j\))

Sinking-fund payment \(=iL+D\); amortization payment \(=1/a_{\overline{n}|}\) per unit loan, and D3 shows the two are equal. Subtracting the fund's interest from the loan interest, \(iL-J_t\), gives the amortization interest because the net loan equals the amortization balance: \(i\times\text{Net}_{t-1}=iL-i\,\text{Fund}_{t-1}=iL-J_t\). The fund increment \(D+J_t\) is the change in the fund, which is the change in (minus) the net loan, i.e. the principal repaid in amortization.

D5 · Two rates: payment and \(a_{\overline{n}|i\&j}\)

For a loan of 1 the payment is \(1/a_{\overline{n}|i\&j}\). It must pay interest \(i\) and also fund a deposit that grows at \(j\) to 1: \[\frac1{a_{\overline{n}|i\&j}}=\frac1{s_{\overline{n}|j}}+i.\] Use the identity \(\dfrac1{a_{\overline{n}|j}}=\dfrac1{s_{\overline{n}|j}}+j\): \[\frac1{a_{\overline{n}|i\&j}}=\frac1{a_{\overline{n}|j}}-j+i=\frac1{a_{\overline{n}|j}}+(i-j).\] Invert (common denominator \(a_{\overline{n}|j}\)): \[\boxed{a_{\overline{n}|i\&j}=\frac{a_{\overline{n}|j}}{1+(i-j)\,a_{\overline{n}|j}}}\] If \(i=j\) the denominator is 1, giving \(a_{\overline{n}|i\&j}=a_{\overline{n}|i}\).

D6 · Newton–Raphson for \(a_{\overline{n}|i}=k\)

Let \(f(i)=a_{\overline{n}|i}-k\) and \(v=(1+i)^{-1}\). Writing \(a_{\overline{n}|i}=(1-v^n)/i\) and using \(dv^n/di=-n v^{n+1}\): \[f'(i)=\frac{n\,i\,v^{n+1}-(1-v^n)}{i^2}.\] Newton's step \(i-f/f'\): \[i_{s+1}=i_s-\frac{(1-v^n-k i_s)/i_s}{\left[n i_s v^{n+1}-(1-v^n)\right]/i_s^2}=i_s\left[1+\frac{1-v^n-k i_s}{1-v^n-n\,i_s v^{n+1}}\right].\] Since \(v^{n+1}(1+i)=v^n\), we have \(1-v^n-n i v^{n+1}=1-v^{n+1}\{1+i(n+1)\}\), which gives the form used in §6: \[i_{s+1}=i_s\left[1+\frac{1-(1+i_s)^{-n}-k\,i_s}{1-(1+i_s)^{-n-1}\{1+i_s(n+1)\}}\right].\] Starting value (first-order approximation). The payments occur on average at time \((n+1)/2\), so \(a_{\overline{n}|i}\approx n\,v^{(n+1)/2}\approx\dfrac{n}{1+i(n+1)/2}\). Setting this equal to \(k\) and solving for \(i\): \[i_0=\frac{2(n-k)}{k(n+1)}.\]
