Stratified Sampling: Variance Under Different Allocations

StRS Part 3

Part 2 decided how many units go to each stratum. Part 3 asks what variance each of those recipes produces, which recipe buys the most precision, and what happens when sampling costs differ. Every formula comes with a worked example.

See it first

Three strata, one total sample size, three different ways to split it. Change the numbers and watch the variance move. Each stratum adds a piece \(w_i^2S_i^2/n_i\) to the total. A big weight or a big spread makes the piece bigger; a bigger sample in that stratum makes it smaller.

Finite population correction ignored, as everywhere in Part 3. The second bar shows where the total variance comes from.

What Part 3 assumes

Part 2 finished the allocation rules. Part 3 does not derive them again. It plugs each finished rule into one variance formula and reads the result. So the work is: one general variance, then four substitutions.

1. One switch is off

The finite population correction is dropped:

\[1-\frac{n_i}{N_i}\approx1\]

Each stratum is treated as if it were sampled with replacement. This is harmless when every \(n_i\) is a small slice of \(N_i\), and it keeps the algebra short.

2. Three moving parts

Every variance in Part 3 is built from:

  • \(w_i=N_i/N\): the stratum's share of the population.
  • \(S_i\): the stratum's spread.
  • \(n_i\): the sample taken from it.

3. The same estimator

The stratified mean never changes:

\[\bar y_{st}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i\bar y_i\]

Only the \(n_i\)'s change from rule to rule, so only the variance changes.

Roadmap. Write the general variance once. Then substitute the equal, proportional, Neyman and cost-optimum rules from Part 2. Each substitution gives one clean formula.

The general variance formula

Before picking a rule, write the variance for any set of sample sizes. Every later result is this formula with a particular \(n_i\) dropped in.

Step 1: variance of a weighted sum

The estimator is a weighted sum with fixed weights. For any weighted sum, constants come out squared, and every pair of terms adds a covariance.

\[\V\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i\bar y_i\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i^2\V(\bar y_i)+2\sum_{i<l}w_iw_l\,\mathrm{Cov}(\bar y_i,\bar y_l)\]

Different strata are sampled separately, so their sample means cannot move together. Every covariance is zero.

\[\mathrm{Cov}(\bar y_i,\bar y_l)=0,\qquad i\neq l\]

Only the squared-weight terms survive.

\[\V(\bar y_{st})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i^2\V(\bar y_i)\]

Step 2: variance inside one stratum

Within stratum \(i\) the sample is an SRSWOR. With the correction switched off, the variance of the stratum mean is the stratum variance divided by the stratum sample size.

\[\V(\bar y_i)\approx\frac{S_i^2}{n_i}\]

Step 3: put the pieces together

\[V\equiv\V(\bar y_{st})\approx\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{w_i^2S_i^2}{n_i}\]

This is the master formula. It says the variance is a sum of \(k\) independent contributions, one per stratum.

  • Larger \(w_i\) raises the contribution, because a big stratum carries more weight in the estimate.
  • Larger \(S_i\) raises the contribution, because a spread-out stratum is harder to estimate.
  • Larger \(n_i\) lowers the contribution, because more observations mean less noise.
Question. A population has \(N=(5000,3000,2000)\) and \(S=(10,20,15)\). Someone splits a sample of \(n=300\) as \(n_i=(120,110,70)\), which is not one of the standard rules. Find the variance.
Solution.
\[w=(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2)\]
\[\text{Stratum 1: }\frac{(0.5)^2(10)^2}{120}=0.2083\]
\[\text{Stratum 2: }\frac{(0.3)^2(20)^2}{110}=0.3273\]
\[\text{Stratum 3: }\frac{(0.2)^2(15)^2}{70}=0.1286\]
\[V=0.2083+0.3273+0.1286=0.6642\]

Stratum 2 supplies almost half the variance: it has the largest \(S_i\) and a modest sample. Moving a few units from stratum 1 to stratum 2 would lower \(V\).

Equal allocation

The rule from Part 2

Every stratum gets the same number of units, whatever its size or spread.

\[n_i=\frac{n}{k}\]

Substitute into the general variance. Dividing by \(n/k\) is the same as multiplying by \(k/n\).

\[V_{\mathrm{eq}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{w_i^2S_i^2}{n/k}\]
\[V_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{k}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i^2S_i^2\]
Question. Same population as before: \(N=(5000,3000,2000)\), \(S=(10,20,15)\), \(n=300\). Find the equal-allocation sample sizes and variance.
Solution.
\[n_1=n_2=n_3=\frac{300}{3}=100\]
\[w_1^2S_1^2=0.25(100)=25\]
\[w_2^2S_2^2=0.09(400)=36\]
\[w_3^2S_3^2=0.04(225)=9\]
\[V_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{3}{300}(25+36+9)=\frac{70}{100}=0.7000\]

Equal allocation ignores both size and spread. It is a sensible choice when the strata are about equally important and about equally variable, but it is not built to minimise variance.

Proportional allocation

The rule from Part 2

Each stratum gets the same fraction of its population:

\[n_i=nw_i\]

Substitute and cancel one \(w_i\).

\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{w_i^2S_i^2}{nw_i}\]
\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i^2\]

The sum \(\sum_iw_iS_i^2\) is the population-weighted average of the within-stratum variances. So proportional allocation gives a very readable result: variance equals that weighted average, divided by \(n\).

Question. Same data, \(n=300\). Find the proportional sample sizes and the variance.
Solution.
\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=300(0.5,\ 0.3,\ 0.2)=(150,\ 90,\ 60)\]
\[w_1S_1^2=0.5(100)=50\]
\[w_2S_2^2=0.3(400)=120\]
\[w_3S_3^2=0.2(225)=45\]
\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac{50+120+45}{300}=\frac{215}{300}=0.7167\]

No knowledge of the \(S_i\)'s is needed to build this allocation. That is its practical selling point.

Neyman allocation

The rule from Part 2

Larger and more variable strata get more units. Write \(A=\sum_iw_iS_i\).

\[n_i=n\frac{w_iS_i}{A},\qquad A=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i\]

Substitute into the general variance. Dividing by \(nw_iS_i/A\) is the same as multiplying by \(A/(nw_iS_i)\).

\[V_{\mathrm{N}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{w_i^2S_i^2}{nw_iS_i/A}\]
\[V_{\mathrm{N}}=\frac{A}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i\]

But the remaining sum is exactly \(A\), by definition.

\[V_{\mathrm{N}}=\frac{A}{n}\cdot A=\frac{A^2}{n}\]
\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i\Big)^2\]
Question. Same data, \(n=300\). Find \(A\), the Neyman sample sizes and the minimum variance.
Solution.
\[A=0.5(10)+0.3(20)+0.2(15)=5+6+3=14\]
\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=300\Big(\frac{5}{14},\ \frac{6}{14},\ \frac{3}{14}\Big)\approx(107.1,\ 128.6,\ 64.3)\]

After rounding to whole units and keeping the total at 300:

\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=(107,\ 129,\ 64)\]
\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{14^2}{300}=\frac{196}{300}=0.6533\]

This is the smallest variance any allocation of 300 units can give for these strata. Proof that no other rule beats it is in the next section and in derivation D5.

Why Neyman is never worse than proportional

The difference

Subtract the Neyman variance from the proportional variance.

\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}-V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{1}{n}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i^2-\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i\Big)^2\Big]\]

The bracket is the weighted variance of the \(S_i\)'s around their weighted mean \(S_w=\sum_iw_iS_i\).

\[\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i^2-S_w^2=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i(S_i-S_w)^2\]

Every term on the right is a square, so the total cannot be negative.

\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}-V_{\mathrm{opt}}\ge0\]
\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}\le V_{\mathrm{prop}}\]

Equality happens only when all \(S_i\) are equal. Then Neyman allocation collapses into proportional allocation and the two variances coincide.

Question. For the running data, check the identity and find the percentage reduction in variance.
Solution.
\[S_w=14\]
\[\sum_iw_i(S_i-S_w)^2=0.5(10-14)^2+0.3(20-14)^2+0.2(15-14)^2\]
\[=0.5(16)+0.3(36)+0.2(1)=8+10.8+0.2=19\]
\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}-V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{19}{300}=0.0633\]
\[\frac{0.0633}{0.7167}\times100\%\approx8.8\%\]

Neyman moves sample away from the calm stratum 1 and toward the noisy stratum 2, which is why it wins.

When unit costs differ

If one unit costs \(C_i\) in stratum \(i\), the total bill is overhead plus the variable part.

\[C=C_0+\sum_{i=1}^{k}C_in_i\]

The cost-optimum rule from Part 2 puts more sample where units are large, variable and cheap:

\[n_i\propto\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}}\]

The square root appears because the optimisation balances a variance term that falls like \(1/n_i\) against a cost term that rises like \(C_in_i\).

Fixed budget: minimum variance

Let \(C_v=C^*-C_0\) be the money available for sampling units, and define

\[B=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}\]

Then the cost-optimum sizes and the smallest attainable variance are

\[n_i^*=C_v\frac{w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}}{B}\]
\[V_{\min}=\frac{B^2}{C_v}=\frac{\big(\sum_iw_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}\big)^2}{C_v}\]
Question. \(N=(5000,3000,2000)\), \(S=(10,20,15)\), costs \(C=(1,4,9)\), variable budget \(C_v=1300\). Find \(B\), the sample sizes and the minimum variance.
Solution.
\[B=0.5(10)(1)+0.3(20)(2)+0.2(15)(3)\]
\[B=5+12+9=26\]
\[\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}}=(5,\ 3,\ 1)\]
\[n_i^*=\frac{1300}{26}(5,\ 3,\ 1)=50(5,\ 3,\ 1)=(250,\ 150,\ 50)\]
\[V_{\min}=\frac{26^2}{1300}=\frac{676}{1300}=0.52\]

Cost check: \(1(250)+4(150)+9(50)=250+600+450=1300\), which matches the budget.

Fixed variance: minimum cost

Now turn the problem around. Target variance is \(V_0\), and the aim is the cheapest sample that reaches it.

\[n_i^*=\frac{B}{V_0}\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}}\]
\[C_v^*=\frac{B^2}{V_0}=\frac{\big(\sum_iw_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}\big)^2}{V_0}\]
Question. Same strata and costs, but now the target is \(V_0=1\). Find the sample sizes and the minimum variable cost.
Solution.
\[n_i^*=\frac{26}{1}(5,\ 3,\ 1)=(130,\ 78,\ 26)\]
\[C_v^*=\frac{26^2}{1}=676\]

Check the variance and the cost:

\[V=0.5^2\frac{100}{130}+0.3^2\frac{400}{78}+0.2^2\frac{225}{26}=0.1923+0.4615+0.3462=1.0000\]
\[C_v=1(130)+4(78)+9(26)=130+312+234=676\]

The two problems share the same shape \(w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}\). Only the multiplier changes: \(C_v/B\) in the fixed-budget version, \(B/V_0\) in the fixed-variance version.

Equal costs bring back Neyman

If every unit costs the same, \(C_i=C\), then \(\sqrt{C_i}=\sqrt{C}\) is a common factor and cancels from the proportionality.

\[n_i\propto\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C}}\propto w_iS_i\]

That is exactly Neyman allocation. And if the \(S_i\)'s are also equal, it reduces further to proportional allocation.

\[\text{cost-optimum}\ \xrightarrow{C_i\text{ equal}}\ \text{Neyman}\ \xrightarrow{S_i\text{ equal}}\ \text{proportional}\]
Question. With \(C=(4,4,4)\) and \(n=300\), verify that the cost-optimum rule gives the Neyman sizes and the Neyman variance.
Solution.
\[B=\sum_iw_iS_i\sqrt{C_i}=2(14)=28\]
\[C_v=nC=300(4)=1200\]
\[V_{\min}=\frac{B^2}{C_v}=\frac{784}{1200}=0.6533\]
\[\frac{A^2}{n}=\frac{14^2}{300}=0.6533\]

The two values agree, as they should.

One full example, all three rules

The same three strata throughout: \(N=(5000,3000,2000)\), so \(w=(0.5,0.3,0.2)\); spreads \(S=(10,20,15)\); total sample \(n=300\).

Rule\(n_1\)\(n_2\)\(n_3\)Variance
Equal1001001000.7000
Proportional15090600.7167
Neyman107129640.6533

Neyman is the smallest, as guaranteed. Interestingly, equal allocation beats proportional here. Proportional gives the small third stratum only 60 units even though it is quite variable, while equal gives it 100. That extra 40 units in stratum 3 is worth more than the 50 units proportional takes away from the calm stratum 1.

Question. How much variance does Neyman remove compared with proportional, in absolute and percentage terms?
\[0.7167-0.6533=0.0634\]
\[\frac{0.0634}{0.7167}\times100\%\approx8.8\%\]
Reading the table. Allocation is a real decision. With the same 300 interviews, moving from proportional to Neyman cuts the variance by about 9%. Moving to equal would have cut it by about 2% in this example, but that is a coincidence of these numbers, not a rule.

Precision compared with simple random sampling

A stratified design should be judged against the obvious alternative: one simple random sample of the same total size \(n\). With the correction ignored, that benchmark variance is

\[V_{\mathrm{SRS}}\approx\frac{S^2}{n}\]

where \(S^2\) is the variance of the whole population, not of a single stratum.

Where the gain comes from

The population variance splits into a part inside strata and a part between strata.

\[S^2\approx\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i^2+\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i(\bar Y_i-\bar Y)^2\]

Stratification only pays for the first part. The second part never enters the stratified variance, because every stratum is always represented.

\[S^2\ge\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iS_i^2\]

Subtract the proportional variance from the SRS variance:

\[V_{\mathrm{SRS}}-V_{\mathrm{prop}}\approx\frac{1}{n}\Big[S^2-\sum_iw_iS_i^2\Big]\]
\[V_{\mathrm{SRS}}-V_{\mathrm{prop}}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_iw_i(\bar Y_i-\bar Y)^2\ge0\]

Combining this with Neyman's advantage gives the full chain:

\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}\le V_{\mathrm{prop}}\le V_{\mathrm{SRS}}\]
Question. Add stratum means \(\bar Y=(40,55,70)\) to the running data. Find \(S^2\), \(V_{\mathrm{SRS}}\) and compare with the stratified variances.
Solution.
\[\bar Y=0.5(40)+0.3(55)+0.2(70)=20+16.5+14=50.5\]
\[\text{within}=0.5(100)+0.3(400)+0.2(225)=50+120+45=215\]
\[\text{between}=0.5(40-50.5)^2+0.3(55-50.5)^2+0.2(70-50.5)^2\]
\[\text{between}=55.125+6.075+76.05=137.25\]
\[S^2\approx215+137.25=352.25\]
\[V_{\mathrm{SRS}}\approx\frac{352.25}{300}=1.1742\]

So \(0.6533\le0.7167\le1.1742\), exactly as the chain predicts.

Two different percentages

People report precision gains in two ways, and they are not the same number.

\[RV=\frac{V_{\mathrm{SRS}}-V_{\mathrm{st}}}{V_{\mathrm{SRS}}}\times100\%\]
\[G_{\mathrm{st}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{SRS}}-V_{\mathrm{st}}}{V_{\mathrm{st}}}\times100\%\]

\(RV\) is the percentage drop in variance. \(G\) is the percentage rise in precision, and precision is proportional to \(1/V\). The two are linked by

\[G=\frac{RV}{1-RV}\]
Question. For proportional allocation in the running example, find \(RV\) and \(G\) against SRS.
Solution.
\[RV=\frac{1.1742-0.7167}{1.1742}\times100\%\approx38.96\%\]
\[G=\frac{1.1742-0.7167}{0.7167}\times100\%\approx63.84\%\]

Check with the link formula:

\[\frac{0.3896}{1-0.3896}\times100\%=63.84\%\]

A 39% drop in variance is a 64% gain in precision. Never mix the two.

Confidence interval

Estimate the variance from the sample, then build the usual normal interval.

\[\hat V_{\mathrm{st}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_i^2\frac{s_i^2}{n_i}\]
\[\bar y_{st}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat V_{\mathrm{st}}}\]

For 95% confidence, \(z_{0.025}=1.96\). Because the standard errors follow the same order as the variances, the optimum allocation gives the shortest interval and SRS gives the widest.

Question. Take \(\bar y_{st}=50.5\) and suppose the estimated variance under Neyman allocation is \(0.6533\). Build a 95% interval.
Solution.
\[SE=\sqrt{0.6533}=0.8083\]
\[1.96(0.8083)=1.5843\]
\[50.5\pm1.5843=(48.92,\ 52.08)\]

Under proportional allocation the same centre would give \(SE=\sqrt{0.7167}=0.8466\) and a wider interval. Under SRS it would be wider still.

Stratification gain

\(V_{\mathrm{prop}}\) against \(V_{\mathrm{SRS}}\). It is large when stratum means are far apart and within-stratum spreads are small. It comes from how the population was cut into strata.

Allocation gain

\(V_{\mathrm{opt}}\) against \(V_{\mathrm{prop}}\). It is large when the \(S_i\)'s are very different from each other. It comes from how the sample is spread across the strata.

Population proportion

For a yes/no characteristic, the mean of the 0/1 variable is a proportion, so every mean formula carries over with \(S_i^2\) replaced by \(P_iQ_i\).

Estimator and variance

\[P_i=\frac{A_i}{N_i},\qquad p_i=\frac{a_i}{n_i},\qquad Q_i=1-P_i\]
\[P=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_iP_i,\qquad \hat P_{st}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_ip_i\]
\[\V(p_i)\approx\frac{P_iQ_i}{n_i}\]
\[\V(\hat P_{st})\approx\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{w_i^2P_iQ_i}{n_i}\]
Question. Three strata with \(N=(5000,3000,2000)\) and \(P=(0.2,0.5,0.8)\), sample \(n=300\) split in proportion to size. Find the variance.
Solution.
\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=(150,\ 90,\ 60)\]
\[P_1Q_1=0.16,\qquad P_2Q_2=0.25,\qquad P_3Q_3=0.16\]
\[\V_{\mathrm{prop}}(\hat P_{st})=\frac{0.5(0.16)+0.3(0.25)+0.2(0.16)}{300}\]
\[=\frac{0.08+0.075+0.032}{300}=\frac{0.187}{300}=0.0006233\]

Neyman allocation for a proportion

A proportion's standard deviation is \(S_i=\sqrt{P_iQ_i}\), so the Neyman rule becomes

\[n_i\propto w_i\sqrt{P_iQ_i}\]

It uses both the size of the stratum and how uncertain the proportion is there. The product \(P_iQ_i\) is largest at \(P_i=0.5\), so a stratum near 50/50 gets extra sample.

Question. Same data. Find the Neyman sizes and the resulting variance.
Solution.
\[\sqrt{P_iQ_i}=(0.4,\ 0.5,\ 0.4)\]
\[w_i\sqrt{P_iQ_i}=(0.2,\ 0.15,\ 0.08),\qquad \text{sum}=0.43\]
\[(n_1,n_2,n_3)=300\frac{(0.2,\ 0.15,\ 0.08)}{0.43}\approx(140,\ 105,\ 55)\]
\[\V_{\mathrm{opt}}(\hat P_{st})=\frac{0.43^2}{300}=\frac{0.1849}{300}=0.0006163\]

The gain is small here because the \(P_iQ_i\) values are close together. The gain would be larger if one stratum sat near 0.5 and another near 0 or 1.

Quick recap

Rule\(n_i\)Variance (FPC ignored)
Generalany\(\displaystyle\sum_i\frac{w_i^2S_i^2}{n_i}\)
Equal\(n/k\)\(\displaystyle\frac{k}{n}\sum_iw_i^2S_i^2\)
Proportional\(nw_i\)\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_iw_iS_i^2\)
Neyman\(n\dfrac{w_iS_i}{A}\)\(\displaystyle\frac{A^2}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_iw_iS_i\Big)^2\)
Unequal cost\(C_v\dfrac{w_iS_i/\sqrt{C_i}}{B}\)\(\displaystyle\frac{B^2}{C_v}\)
SRS benchmark—\(\displaystyle\frac{S^2}{n}\)
One sentence. Variance is a sum of per-stratum contributions; the allocation rule decides how big each contribution is; Neyman makes the sum as small as possible, and unequal costs bend the rule toward cheaper strata.

Practice

Exercise 1: three rules, two strata

\(N=(4000,6000)\), \(S=(8,12)\), \(n=200\). Find the equal, proportional and Neyman variances.

Show answer
\[w=(0.4,\ 0.6)\]

Equal: \(n_i=(100,100)\).

\[V_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{2}{200}\big[0.16(64)+0.36(144)\big]=\frac{10.24+51.84}{100}=0.6208\]

Proportional: \(n_i=(80,120)\).

\[V_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac{0.4(64)+0.6(144)}{200}=\frac{25.6+86.4}{200}=0.56\]

Neyman: \(A=0.4(8)+0.6(12)=10.4\).

\[V_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{10.4^2}{200}=\frac{108.16}{200}=0.5408\]

Order: \(0.5408<0.56<0.6208\).

Exercise 2: cost-optimum with a fixed budget

\(N=(1000,2000,3000)\), \(S=(5,10,15)\), \(C=(1,4,9)\), variable budget \(C_v=3000\). Find \(B\), the sizes and \(V_{\min}\).

Show answer
\[w=\Big(\frac16,\ \frac13,\ \frac12\Big)\]
\[B=\frac16(5)(1)+\frac13(10)(2)+\frac12(15)(3)=0.8333+6.6667+22.5=30\]
\[\frac{w_iS_i}{\sqrt{C_i}}=(0.8333,\ 1.6667,\ 2.5)\]
\[n_i^*=\frac{3000}{30}(0.8333,\ 1.6667,\ 2.5)=(83.3,\ 166.7,\ 250)\]
\[V_{\min}=\frac{30^2}{3000}=\frac{900}{3000}=0.3\]

Cost check: \(1(83.3)+4(166.7)+9(250)\approx3000\).

Exercise 3: precision gain

Using the running example, \(V_{\mathrm{SRS}}=1.1742\) and \(V_{\mathrm{prop}}=0.7167\). Find \(RV\) and \(G\), and check the link formula.

Show answer
\[RV=\frac{1.1742-0.7167}{1.1742}\times100\%=38.96\%\]
\[G=\frac{1.1742-0.7167}{0.7167}\times100\%=63.84\%\]
\[\frac{RV}{1-RV}=\frac{0.3896}{0.6104}=0.6384\]

So a 39% variance reduction is a 64% precision gain.
