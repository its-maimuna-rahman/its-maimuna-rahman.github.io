Demography · Nuptiality and Marital Dissolution

Marriage & Divorce Measures

Marriage and divorce are not just personal events — they restructure households, determine fertility exposure, and drive demand for housing, schooling, and elder care. This guide builds every standard measure from the denominator up: crude, refined, age-specific, duration-specific, and synthetic-cohort. It also includes the measures that are often omitted from introductory treatments: first-marriage rates, marriage squeeze, spousal age gap, widowhood, separation, remarriage, and the nuptiality table that ties them all together. Every measure is worked through with numbers, and the Bangladesh–United States comparison is drawn at the end.

1 · Rates, ratios, and the denominator problem

A marriage rate looks simple: marriages divided by population, times 1,000. The difficulty is the denominator. A marriage can only happen to an unmarried person. If the denominator includes married people, divorced people who will not remarry, and children, the rate is diluted by people who are not at risk. Every refinement of the marriage rate narrows the denominator toward the group that can actually marry in the reference period.

The same logic applies to divorce. A divorce can only happen to a married person. If the denominator is the whole population, the crude divorce rate is heavily distorted by the share of the population that is currently married and by the age structure.

\[ \text{Rate} = \frac{\text{events in a period}}{\text{population exposed to the risk}} \times k \]

The constant \(k\) is 1,000 for most marriage and divorce rates, 100 for percentages, and 100,000 for maternal and certain rare-event rates. The choice of denominator distinguishes a crude rate from a refined rate, a refined rate from an age-specific rate, and an age-specific rate from a duration-specific rate.

The four questions every nuptiality measure answers Incidence: how many marriages or divorces occur per person, per year, or per person at risk?
Timing: at what age do people marry, and at what duration do marriages dissolve?
Intensity: what proportion of a cohort will ever marry, or ever divorce?
Prevalence: what share of the population is currently in each marital state?

The four marital states

Every nuptiality measure partitions the adult population into four mutually exclusive states:

  • Never married — has never entered a legal marriage.
  • Currently married — in a legal marriage at the reference date.
  • Widowed — marriage ended by the death of a spouse and not subsequently remarried.
  • Divorced — marriage ended by legal divorce and not subsequently remarried.

Some surveys add a fifth category, separated, for legally married people who no longer live with their spouse. The exact definition matters: if separated people are counted as married, the divorced category understates the number of broken marriages; if they are counted separately, the married category excludes them. The choice changes every rate that uses marital status in its denominator.

2 · Marriage measures

2.1 Crude Marriage Rate (CMR)

Formula.

\[ \text{CMR} = \frac{M}{P} \times 1000 \]

\(M\) = number of marriages in the year; \(P\) = mid-year total population.

Why it exists. It is the simplest and most widely published marriage measure. It requires only two numbers, both of which are available even in countries with weak statistical systems. It is used for quick comparisons and for monitoring trends over time.

What it gets wrong. The denominator includes infants, children, the elderly, and people already married who cannot marry again in the reference year. A country with a young age structure will show a high CMR even if the proportion of young adults who marry is low, because a large share of the population is of marriageable age. CMR is confounded by age structure and by marital composition.

Worked example. A district has 500,000 people and 3,200 marriages in a year.

\[ \text{CMR} = \frac{3{,}200}{500{,}000} \times 1000 = 6.4 \text{ per 1,000} \]

Interpretation: about 6.4 marriages for every 1,000 people in the district during the year.

2.2 General Marriage Rate (GMR)

Formula. Two versions:

\[ \text{GMR}_{15+} = \frac{M}{P_{15+}} \times 1000 \qquad \text{GMR}_{\text{unmarried}} = \frac{M}{P_{\text{unmarried},15+}} \times 1000 \]

Why it exists. The first version removes children from the denominator. The second removes married people, who cannot marry in the reference year. The second is the more useful measure because it approaches the group actually at risk.

Worked example. A region has 4,800 marriages, a 15+ population of 600,000, and an unmarried 15+ population of 240,000.

\[ \text{GMR}_{15+} = \frac{4{,}800}{600{,}000} \times 1000 = 8.0 \text{ per 1,000} \] \[ \text{GMR}_{\text{unmarried}} = \frac{4{,}800}{240{,}000} \times 1000 = 20.0 \text{ per 1,000 unmarried persons} \]

Interpretation: the refined rate is much higher because married people are excluded from the denominator.

2.3 Refined Marriage Rate (RMR)

Formula. Further restricting the denominator to unmarried women (or to unmarried women in a narrower age band):

\[ \text{RMR} = \frac{\text{marriages to women}}{\text{unmarried women aged 15+}} \times 1000 \]

Why it exists. Most marriage registration data record the bride's age and marital status, so the refined rate is often computed for women. It answers: how many marriages occur per 1,000 women who are actually available to marry?

Worked example. 1,200 women marry in a year, against 85,000 unmarried women aged 15+.

\[ \text{RMR} = \frac{1{,}200}{85{,}000} \times 1000 = 14.1 \text{ per 1,000 unmarried women} \]

2.4 Crude First Marriage Rate (CFMR)

Formula.

\[ \text{CFMR} = \frac{M_1}{P} \times 1000 \]

\(M_1\) = number of first marriages in the year; \(P\) = mid-year total population.

Why it exists. All-marriage rates include remarriages, which are driven by divorce and widowhood rather than by the formation of new families. The first-marriage rate isolates the entry into marriage, which is the demographic event that matters for fertility, household formation, and the timing of adult transitions. A country with a high divorce rate will have a high all-marriage rate even if first marriages are declining, because remarriage inflates the numerator.

Worked example. A population records 2,400 first marriages, 600 remarriages, and has 500,000 people.

\[ \text{CFMR} = \frac{2{,}400}{500{,}000} \times 1000 = 4.8 \text{ per 1,000} \]

The all-marriage rate would be \((2{,}400+600)/500{,}000\times1000 = 6.0\) per 1,000. The difference is entirely remarriage.

2.5 Age-Specific Marriage Rate (ASMR)

Formula. For age group \(x\) to \(x+n\):

\[ {}_n\text{ASMR}_x = \frac{{}_nM_x}{{}_nP_x} \times 1000 \]

\({}_nM_x\) = marriages to persons in the age group; \({}_nP_x\) = population (or unmarried population) in the age group.

Why it exists. ASMR is the building block of every refined marriage measure. It isolates the age group, so it is not distorted by the age structure of the population. It reveals the marriage schedule: the ages at which marriage is most common. In most populations, ASMR rises through the twenties and early thirties, then declines.

Worked example. 2,400 women aged 25–29 marry, against 120,000 unmarried women in that group.

\[ \text{ASMR}_{25\text{–}29} = \frac{2{,}400}{120{,}000} \times 1000 = 20.0 \text{ per 1,000 unmarried women aged 25–29} \]

2.6 Total Marriage Rate (TMR) and Total First Marriage Rate (TFMR)

Formula. If ASMRs are computed for five-year age groups from 15–19 to 45–49:

\[ \text{TMR} = 5\sum_{x=15}^{49}\text{ASMR}_x \]

If ASMRs are for single years:

\[ \text{TMR} = \sum_{x=15}^{49}\text{ASMR}_x \]

Why it exists. TMR is the synthetic-cohort summary: the average number of marriages a person would experience if current age-specific marriage rates applied throughout their life. When computed only for first marriages, it becomes the Total First Marriage Rate (TFMR), which estimates the proportion of a cohort that will ever marry. TFMR is bounded above by 1; TMR can exceed 1 if remarriage is common.

Worked example. Suppose the five-year ASMRs (per 1,000) are:

Interpretation: at these rates, a woman would experience 1.41 marriages over her lifetime. Because this exceeds 1, remarriage must be common; the first-marriage-only version would be below 1 and would estimate the proportion of women who ever marry.

2.7 Proportion ever married and currently married

Proportion ever married at age \(x\). The fraction of the cohort at age \(x\) that has entered marriage at least once. If \(S_x\) is the proportion still never married at age \(x\), then

\[ \text{Ever married}_x = 1 - S_x \]

Proportion currently married at age \(x\). The fraction of the cohort at age \(x\) that is in a marriage at the reference date. This is not the same as the proportion ever married, because some of the ever-married are now divorced or widowed. If \(D_x\) and \(W_x\) are the proportions divorced and widowed,

\[ \text{Currently married}_x = 1 - S_x - D_x - W_x \]

Why they exist. These are the prevalence measures of nuptiality. They answer the questions that policymakers actually ask: how many households are headed by a married couple; how many elderly people live alone because they are widowed; how many single mothers are raising children. They are the raw material for household projection models and for social policy targeting.

Worked example. In a survey of women aged 35–39, 12% are never married, 8% are divorced, 3% are widowed, and 77% are currently married.

StateProportion
Never married12%
Currently married77%
Divorced8%
Widowed3%

Ever married = 100 − 12 = 88%. Currently married = 77%. The gap of 11 percentage points is the proportion whose marriage has ended and who have not remarried.

2.8 Remarriage measures

Crude remarriage rate.

\[ \text{CRR} = \frac{R}{P} \times 1000 \]

\(R\) = number of remarriages in the year; \(P\) = mid-year total population.

Refined remarriage rate.

\[ \text{RRR} = \frac{R}{P_{\text{divorced and widowed},15+}} \times 1000 \]

The denominator is the population actually at risk of remarriage: divorced and widowed persons aged 15+.

Age-specific remarriage rate. For age group \(x\) to \(x+n\):

\[ {}_n\text{ASRR}_x = \frac{{}_nR_x}{{}_nP_{\text{divorced and widowed},x}} \times 1000 \]

Total remarriage rate.

\[ \text{TRR} = 5\sum_{x=15}^{49}\text{ASRR}_x \]

Why they exist. Remarriage is the mechanism by which divorced and widowed people re-enter the married state. Without measuring it, the proportion currently married cannot be projected forward from the proportion ever married. Remarriage rates also reveal sex differences: men typically remarry faster and more often than women, and the gap widens with age.

Worked example. Suppose a population has 1,800 remarriages, 150,000 divorced and widowed persons aged 15+, and 400,000 people overall.

\[ \text{CRR} = \frac{1{,}800}{400{,}000} \times 1000 = 4.5 \text{ per 1,000} \] \[ \text{RRR} = \frac{1{,}800}{150{,}000} \times 1000 = 12.0 \text{ per 1,000 divorced and widowed persons} \]

Interpretation: about 12 out of every 1,000 divorced or widowed persons remarry in a year. The crude rate understates remarriage substantially because most of the population is not eligible.

2.9 Marriage squeeze

Formula. The marriage squeeze is the ratio of men to women (or vice versa) in the marriageable ages, after adjusting for those already married:

\[ \text{Squeeze} = \frac{P_{\text{unmarried men},15\text{–}49}}{P_{\text{unmarried women},15\text{–}49}} \times 100 \]

Why it exists. Marriage requires two people. If the number of unmarried men and unmarried women in the marriageable ages is unequal, one sex faces a shortage of partners. The squeeze is driven by sex differences in mortality, migration, and age gaps between spouses (men typically marry younger women, so a cohort of women born in a year with high infant mortality will face a shortage of older men when it reaches marriageable age).

Worked example. Suppose there are 1,200,000 unmarried men and 1,350,000 unmarried women aged 15–49.

\[ \text{Squeeze} = \frac{1{,}200{,}000}{1{,}350{,}000} \times 100 = 88.9 \]

Interpretation: there are only 89 unmarried men for every 100 unmarried women in the marriageable ages. This is a "female squeeze" — women face a shortage of partners.

2.10 Marital status distribution

Formula. For each age group \(x\), the four proportions:

\[ \pi^{\text{never}}_x,\quad \pi^{\text{married}}_x,\quad \pi^{\text{divorced}}_x,\quad \pi^{\text{widowed}}_x \]

summing to 1. The full table of these proportions by age and sex is the marital status distribution.

Why it exists. It is the input to household projection models, to the computation of life expectancy in each marital state (section 6), and to the standardisation of marriage and divorce rates. Without it, no prevalence-based measure can be computed.

Worked example. A simplified distribution for women aged 60–64:

StateProportion
Never married2%
Currently married62%
Divorced8%
Widowed28%

Interpretation: in this age group, 28% of women are widowed, a much higher figure than for men because women live longer and typically marry older men.

3 · Age at marriage: SMAM, mean, median, and spousal gap

3.1 Singulate Mean Age at Marriage (SMAM)

What it measures. SMAM is the average number of years a person remains single before age 50, under the current marriage rates. It is a synthetic-cohort measure: it does not describe any real cohort, but it summarises the marriage pattern of a period. It requires only a census or survey age distribution by marital status, with no marriage registration data.

Formula. Using proportions single by five-year age group from 15–19 to 45–49:

\[ \text{SMAM} = \frac{15 + 5\sum_{x=15}^{45}P_{\text{single},x} - 50\,P_{\text{single},50}}{1 - P_{\text{single},50}} \]

where \(P_{\text{single},x}\) is the proportion single in age group \(x\) to \(x+4\), and \(P_{\text{single},50}\) is the average proportion single in the 45–49 and 50–54 age groups.

The numerator has three parts. The 15 is the 15 years of single life everyone lives before the first age group. The sum times 5 is the single years lived between 15 and 50, given the proportions single in each group. The third term subtracts the single years lived by those who never marry, because they are not "waiting to marry" in the same sense.

SMAM = 22.70 years. Under these rates, the average person spends 22.7 years single before marrying, and about 96.3% of the cohort eventually marries.

3.2 Median age at first marriage

What it measures. The age by which 50% of a cohort has married for the first time. Calculated by linear interpolation from the cumulative percentage distribution of age at first marriage.

Formula. If the cumulative percentage married reaches \(C_1\) at the lower boundary of an age group and \(C_2\) at the upper boundary, and 50% falls between them,

\[ \text{Median} = L + \frac{50 - C_1}{C_2 - C_1} \times w \]

where \(L\) is the lower boundary and \(w\) is the group width (usually 5 years).

Worked example. Cumulative percentage married by age:

Age202530
Cumulative % married40%75%90%

The 50% point falls between ages 20 and 25.

\[ \text{Median} = 20 + \frac{50-40}{75-40} \times 5 = 20 + \frac{10}{35}\times5 = 20 + 1.43 = 21.43 \text{ years} \]

Interpretation: half of women had married for the first time by about age 21.4.

3.3 Mean age at marriage

Formula. The arithmetic mean of the ages at which a group marries:

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i} \]

\(f_i\) = number of marriages at age \(x_i\) (or at the midpoint of the age group).

Why it exists. The mean and the median answer different questions. The median is robust to outliers; the mean is sensitive to them. If a small number of marriages occur very late in life, the mean will be pulled upward while the median stays stable. Both are reported, because the gap between them is itself informative: a large gap indicates a long right tail.

Worked example. Suppose marriages by age group are:

Interpretation: the average age at marriage in this population is 27.3 years.

3.4 Spousal age gap

Formula. The mean age of husband minus the mean age of wife, for couples married in a given period, or the mean of the individual differences:

\[ \text{Gap} = \frac{\sum(\text{age of husband}_i - \text{age of wife}_i)}{N} \]

Why it exists. The spousal age gap determines the length of widowhood for women, the probability that a wife will be left alone in old age, and the marriage squeeze. In societies where men marry women much younger, a cohort of women may face a shortage of same-age men and must either marry older men or remain single.

Worked example. In a sample of five couples, the ages are:

Interpretation: on average, husbands are 3.6 years older than their wives in this sample.

3.5 Mean age at widowhood

Formula. The average age at which a person experiences the death of their spouse:

\[ \bar{x}_{\text{widowhood}} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i} \]

\(f_i\) = number of widowhoods occurring at age \(x_i\).

Why it exists. The age at widowhood determines how long a person lives alone, the demand for elder care, and the economic vulnerability of widows. In societies with large spousal age gaps and lower female remarriage rates, the mean age at widowhood for women is much earlier than for men, and the duration of widowhood is much longer.

Interpretation: on average, women in this population become widowed at age 56.5, and with a life expectancy of about 75, they spend nearly two decades as widows.

4 · Divorce measures

4.1 Crude Divorce Rate (CDR)

Formula.

\[ \text{CDR} = \frac{D}{P} \times 1000 \]

\(D\) = number of divorces in the year; \(P\) = mid-year total population.

Why it exists. The simplest divorce measure, used for quick comparisons and trend monitoring.

What it gets wrong. The denominator includes children, the never-married, and the widowed — none of whom can divorce. It is also distorted by age structure and by the share of the population currently married. A country with a large elderly population will have a low CDR even if divorce among married couples is common.

Worked example. A country has 11,000 divorces and 10,000,000 people.

\[ \text{CDR} = \frac{11{,}000}{10{,}000{,}000} \times 1000 = 1.1 \text{ per 1,000} \]

4.2 Refined Divorce Rate (RDR)

Formula.

\[ \text{RDR} = \frac{D}{P_{\text{married women}}} \times 1000 \]

Why it exists. The refined rate restricts the denominator to the group actually at risk of divorce: married women (or married persons). It removes the distortion caused by the never-married, the widowed, and children. It is the measure used by the U.S. National Center for Family and Marriage Research.

Worked example. Suppose 986,810 women divorce, against 69,500,000 married women aged 15+.

\[ \text{RDR} = \frac{986{,}810}{69{,}500{,}000} \times 1000 = 14.2 \text{ per 1,000 married women} \]

Interpretation: about 14 divorces per 1,000 married women in the population.

4.3 Age-Specific Divorce Rate (ASDR)

Formula. For age group \(x\) to \(x+n\):

\[ {}_n\text{ASDR}_x = \frac{{}_nD_x}{{}_nP_{\text{married},x}} \times 1000 \]

Why it exists. ASDR isolates the age pattern of divorce. In most populations, divorce rates peak in the late twenties and thirties, then decline. The pattern is closely related to marriage duration, because age and duration are strongly correlated.

Worked example. 8,500 women aged 30–34 divorce, against 1,200,000 married women in that group.

\[ \text{ASDR}_{30\text{–}34} = \frac{8{,}500}{1{,}200{,}000} \times 1000 = 7.08 \text{ per 1,000 married women aged 30–34} \]

4.4 Duration-specific divorce rate and Total Divorce Rate (TDR)

Duration-specific divorce rate. For marriages that have lasted \(i\) years:

\[ d_i = \frac{\text{divorces to marriages of duration }i}{\text{marriages that reached duration }i} \times 1000 \]

Total Divorce Rate (TDR). The sum of duration-specific rates over all durations, usually up to 30 years:

\[ \text{TDR} = \sum_{i=0}^{30}d_i \]

Why it exists. TDR is the divorce analogue of TFR. It estimates the proportion of marriages that would end in divorce if the duration-specific rates of a given year remained constant. It is the standard measure for comparing divorce intensity across countries and over time, because it is not distorted by the number of marriages in the reference year.

Worked example. Suppose duration-specific divorce rates (per 1,000 marriages) are 5, 10, 15, 12, 8, and 5 for durations 0–5 years, and zero thereafter.

\[ \text{TDR} = 5+10+15+12+8+5 = 55 \text{ per 1,000} = 5.5\% \]

Interpretation: at these rates, about 5.5% of marriages would end in divorce within six years. Extending the sum to 30 years gives the full TDR.

4.5 Median age at divorce and median duration of marriage

Median age at divorce. The age by which 50% of divorces have occurred. Computed by linear interpolation, exactly as the median age at marriage is computed.

\[ \text{Median age at divorce} = L + \frac{50-C_1}{C_2-C_1}\times w \]

Median duration of marriage at divorce. The number of years by which 50% of divorces have occurred, computed by interpolation from the cumulative duration distribution.

Why they matter. The median age tells you whether divorce is concentrated among young couples or older couples (the "gray divorce" phenomenon). The median duration tells you how long marriages last before dissolving. Together they describe the timing of dissolution, not just its frequency.

Worked example (median age). Cumulative percentage of divorces by age:

Age303540
Cumulative % of divorces35%60%80%

The 50% point falls between ages 30 and 35.

\[ \text{Median age} = 30 + \frac{50-35}{60-35}\times5 = 30 + \frac{15}{25}\times5 = 30 + 3 = 33.0 \text{ years} \]

Interpretation: half of all divorces involve a person aged 33 or younger.

4.6 Marriage-to-divorce ratio and divorce-to-marriage ratio

Marriage-to-divorce ratio.

\[ \text{MDR} = \frac{M}{D} \]

\(M\) = number of marriages in the year; \(D\) = number of divorces in the year.

Divorce-to-marriage ratio. The reciprocal:

\[ \text{DMR} = \frac{D}{M} \]

Why they exist. These are simple ratios, not rates: they are not per capita and not per married person. They answer the intuitive question "for every marriage, how many divorces are there?" They are widely reported in the press because they are easy to understand, but they are misleading as measures of divorce risk, because the numerator and denominator refer to different events and different populations.

Worked example. A country records 2,390,000 marriages and 986,810 divorces in a year.

\[ \text{MDR} = \frac{2{,}390{,}000}{986{,}810} = 2.42 \] \[ \text{DMR} = \frac{986{,}810}{2{,}390{,}000} = 0.413 \]

Interpretation: for every 100 marriages, there are about 41 divorces. The ratio is not a probability — it does not mean 41% of marriages end in divorce — because the marriages and divorces in a given year involve different couples.

4.7 Divorce by parity and number of children

Formula. The divorce rate for marriages with \(c\) children:

\[ d_c = \frac{\text{divorces among marriages with }c\text{ children}}{\text{marriages with }c\text{ children}} \times 1000 \]

Why it exists. The presence and number of children strongly affects divorce risk. Couples with young children divorce less than childless couples in many populations; couples with many children may divorce less because of the economic cost of separation. The parity-specific divorce rate isolates this effect and is used in family policy analysis.

Worked example. In a population, 200,000 marriages have no children and 8,000 of them divorce in a year; 400,000 marriages have one or more children and 12,000 of them divorce.

\[ d_0 = \frac{8{,}000}{200{,}000}\times1000 = 40.0 \text{ per 1,000} \] \[ d_{1+} = \frac{12{,}000}{400{,}000}\times1000 = 30.0 \text{ per 1,000} \]

Interpretation: childless marriages in this population dissolve at a rate 33% higher than marriages with children.

5 · Widowhood, separation, and annulment

5.1 Crude widowhood rate

Formula.

\[ \text{CWR}_{\text{wid}} = \frac{W}{P} \times 1000 \]

\(W\) = number of widowhoods (deaths of a married person whose spouse survives) in the year; \(P\) = mid-year total population.

Why it exists. Marriage can dissolve in two ways: divorce and death. If only divorce is measured, the picture is incomplete. The widowhood rate captures the other mechanism, which is especially important at older ages and for women, who typically outlive their husbands.

Worked example. A country records 1,250,000 widowhoods in a year against a population of 335,000,000.

\[ \text{CWR}_{\text{wid}} = \frac{1{,}250{,}000}{335{,}000{,}000} \times 1000 = 3.73 \text{ per 1,000} \]

5.2 Refined widowhood rate

Formula.

\[ \text{RWR} = \frac{W}{P_{\text{married persons}}} \times 1000 \]

Why it exists. The refined rate restricts the denominator to the population at risk — married persons. It removes the dilution caused by unmarried people in the denominator.

Worked example. With 1,250,000 widowhoods and 130,000,000 married persons:

\[ \text{RWR} = \frac{1{,}250{,}000}{130{,}000{,}000} \times 1000 = 9.62 \text{ per 1,000 married persons} \]

5.3 Age-specific widowhood rate

Formula. For age group \(x\) to \(x+n\):

\[ {}_n\text{ASWR}_x = \frac{{}_nW_x}{{}_nP_{\text{married},x}} \times 1000 \]

Why it exists. Widowhood is highly age-specific. It is rare before 50 and rises steeply after 60. The age-specific rate isolates this pattern and allows the computation of a synthetic-cohort widowhood measure.

Worked example. In age group 60–64, 4,200 women become widowed against 1,800,000 married women.

\[ \text{ASWR}_{60\text{–}64} = \frac{4{,}200}{1{,}800{,}000} \times 1000 = 2.33 \text{ per 1,000 married women aged 60–64} \]

5.4 Separation rate and annulment rate

Separation rate. Separated persons are legally married but no longer cohabiting. The crude separation rate is

\[ \text{SR} = \frac{S}{P} \times 1000 \]

and the refined rate restricts the denominator to married persons.

Annulment rate. Annulments are legal declarations that a marriage was never valid. The crude rate is

\[ \text{AR} = \frac{A}{P} \times 1000 \]

Why they exist. Both are rare in most populations, but they matter for the measurement of marital dissolution. If separated people are counted as married, the "currently married" prevalence measure overstates the share of the population in a functioning marriage. If annulments are counted as divorces, the divorce rate overstates the number of marriages ending by legal divorce.

Worked example. A population records 30,000 separations, 1,500 annulments, and has 10,000,000 people.

\[ \text{SR} = \frac{30{,}000}{10{,}000{,}000} \times 1000 = 3.0 \text{ per 1,000} \] \[ \text{AR} = \frac{1{,}500}{10{,}000{,}000} \times 1000 = 0.15 \text{ per 1,000} \]

6 · The nuptiality table and marital-status life expectancy

The life table tracks a cohort from birth to death. The nuptiality table does the same for marital states: it follows a synthetic cohort from birth through first marriage, divorce or widowhood, and remarriage, and computes the expected number of years spent in each state. It is the synthetic-cohort summary of all the measures above.

6.1 The simplest nuptiality table: first marriage

The table starts with 100,000 never-married persons at exact age 15. At each age, some proportion marry for the first time. The key columns are:

ColumnMeaningFormula

\(m_x\)First marriages between \(x\) and \(x+1\)\(S_x-S_{x+1}\)

\(L_x^{\text{single}}\)Person-years lived single between \(x\) and \(x+1\)\((S_x+S_{x+1})/2\)

The expected number of years spent single between 15 and 50 is the sum of the \(L_x^{\text{single}}\) column. The proportion ever marrying is \(1-S_{50}/S_{15}\).

6.2 Marital-status life table (multistate)

The full marital-status life table extends the simple nuptiality table to all four states and allows transitions between them: never married → married → divorced → remarried, married → widowed → remarriage, and so on. The output is the expected number of years a person will spend in each marital state over the rest of their life.

The computation is a matrix of transition rates between states, applied year by year. For most teaching purposes, a simplified version is sufficient: compute the period life table, then apply the age-specific marital-status distribution to the \(L_x\) column to get the person-years in each state.

6.3 The Sullivan method for marital-status life expectancy

Idea. Combine the period life table's \(L_x\) column (person-years lived between age \(x\) and \(x+1\)) with the observed proportion of people in each marital state at each age:

\[ e_x^{\text{state}} = \frac{\sum_{y\ge x}L_y\,\pi_y^{\text{state}}}{l_x} \]

\(\pi_y^{\text{state}}\) is the proportion of the population at age \(y\) in the given marital state (never married, married, divorced, widowed). \(l_x\) is the life table's number alive at exact age \(x\).

Why it exists. It answers a question the ordinary life table cannot: how many of the remaining years will be spent married, divorced, or widowed? This is directly relevant to pension planning, elder-care provision, and insurance pricing.

Bangladesh
Marriage. Crude marriage rate about 15.7 per 1,000 (2023, down from 18.1 in 2022). Mean age at first marriage about 24.2 years for men and 18.4 years for women (2022). About 40.9% of women marry before age 18.
United States
Marriage. About 2.39 million marriages in 2024, a crude marriage rate of roughly 7.1 per 1,000. Median age at first marriage: 30.2 years for men and 28.6 years for women.
Bangladesh

United States
Divorce. About 986,810 divorces among women in 2024. Crude divorce rate 2.3 per 1,000; refined divorce rate 14.2 per 1,000 married women. Marriage-to-divorce ratio about 2.42.
Bangladesh

United States
Spousal age gap. Husbands are on average about 2.3 years older than wives. About 17% of women aged 60–64 are widowed, against about 5% of men.
Why the comparison matters Bangladesh has a much higher marriage rate, a much lower divorce rate, and a far younger age at marriage than the United States. Its spousal age gap is much larger, and its widowhood prevalence much higher, especially for women. These differences reflect different stages of the demographic transition, different legal and religious frameworks for marriage and divorce, and different levels of female education and labour-force participation. The measures are neutral; the numbers they produce are shaped by the social and institutional context.

9 · Derivations and worked examples

Derivation 1 · Why SMAM uses 15 and 50

SMAM estimates the average number of years a person spends single before age 50. Everyone lives 15 years before the first age group. Between ages 15 and 50, single years lived equal the sum of proportions single in each five-year group, multiplied by 5. Years lived single after 50 by those who never marry must be subtracted, because SMAM is the average age at marriage, not at leaving the single state. Dividing by the proportion who ever marry rescales to the married population.

\[ \text{SMAM} = \frac{15 + 5\sum P_{\text{single}} - 50\,P_{\text{single},50}}{1 - P_{\text{single},50}} \]
Derivation 2 · Worked SMAM with all steps
Sum of proportions single. \(0.850+0.450+0.200+0.100+0.060+0.045+0.040=1.745\).
Single years 15–50. \(5\times1.745=8.725\).
Add years before 15. \(15+8.725=23.725\).
Terminal proportion single. \((0.040+0.035)/2=0.0375\).
Subtract never-marriers’ years. \(50\times0.0375=1.875\); \(23.725-1.875=21.850\).
Divide by ever-marrying proportion. \(1-0.0375=0.9625\); \(21.850/0.9625=22.70\).

SMAM = 22.70 years.

Derivation 3 · Mean age at marriage, step by step
Multiply each midpoint by its frequency. \(200(17.5)+600(22.5)+900(27.5)+500(32.5)+200(37.5)=65{,}500\).
Sum the frequencies. \(200+600+900+500+200=2{,}400\).
Divide. \(65{,}500/2{,}400=27.29\).

Mean age at marriage = 27.29 years.

Derivation 4 · Total Divorce Rate as a synthetic-cohort measure

If a marriage cohort experienced the duration-specific divorce rates of a given year at every duration, the proportion divorcing by duration 30 would be the sum of those rates divided by 1,000. This is the same logic as TFR: the sum of age-specific rates gives the average number of events per person. TDR gives the average number of divorces per marriage, which is the proportion of marriages ending in divorce under the current regime.

\[ \text{TDR} = \sum_{i=0}^{30}\frac{d_i}{1000} \]

If TDR = 0.55, about 55% of marriages would end in divorce if the rates of that year persisted.

Derivation 5 · Interpolation for the median age at marriage or divorce

Suppose the cumulative percentage married is \(C_1\) at age \(L\) and \(C_2\) at age \(L+w\). The 50% point falls inside the interval if \(C_1<50 \[ \text{Median} = L + \frac{50-C_1}{C_2-C_1}\times w. \]

This is the standard linear interpolation used by DHS, UNFPA, and most national statistical offices.

Derivation 6 · Why the marriage-to-divorce ratio is not a probability

The ratio \(M/D\) compares two counts from the same year. The marriages counted may last decades; the divorces counted may involve couples who married decades ago. A ratio of 2.42 means 2.42 marriages occur for each divorce in the year, not that 41% of marriages end in divorce. To estimate the probability of divorce, one needs the duration-specific divorce rates summed into TDR, or a cohort study that follows actual marriages. The ratio is a useful descriptive statistic but not a risk measure.

Derivation 7 · Sullivan method for marital-status life expectancy

The ordinary life table gives \(L_y\), the person-years lived by the cohort at age \(y\). If a proportion \(\pi_y^{\text{state}}\) of those person-years are lived in a given marital state, the total person-years in that state above age \(x\) is \(\sum_{y\ge x}L_y\pi_y^{\text{state}}\). Dividing by \(l_x\), the number alive at exact age \(x\), gives the expected number of years spent in that state:

\[ e_x^{\text{state}} = \frac{\sum_{y\ge x}L_y\pi_y^{\text{state}}}{l_x}. \]

The sum of \(e_x^{\text{state}}\) over all states equals \(e_x\), the ordinary life expectancy. The method is named after Daniel Sullivan, who introduced it in 1971.
